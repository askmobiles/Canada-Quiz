🏝️ Oak Island: 200 years of digging for treasure

A small island in Mahone Bay, Nova Scotia, has drawn treasure hunters since the 1790s. Millions of dollars and at least six lives later, no treasure has been found. This page separates what the records actually show from the legend that grew around them.

By the Canada Quiz editorial team · last checked 8 November 2026 · how we check our facts

Jump to: The short answer · The legend · What the records say · Timeline · What science says · What has been found · Quiz

The short answer

Oak Island is one of more than 300 islands in Mahone Bay, on Nova Scotia's South Shore, about 1.6 km long and 0.8 km wide. Since about 1800, group after group has dug a shaft there, the “Money Pit”, convinced that something valuable was buried at the bottom. Each time, water flooded the hole.

  • What is certain: people really have dug there for more than 200 years, spent large sums, and lost lives. Four men died in a single accident in 1965.
  • What is not: that anyone ever buried treasure there. The Canadian Encyclopedia states plainly that no treasure has been found.
  • Why it matters: most of the famous details, such as the carved stone and the booby-trap tunnels, were written down decades after the events, and some were added much later.

Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia — Oak Island

The legend, as it is usually told

In 1795, the story goes, a young man named Daniel McGinnis found a round hollow in the ground under an old oak tree, with what looked like the mark of a rope pulley on a branch. With two friends, John Smith and Anthony Vaughan, he started to dig. Every few metres they hit a layer of logs. They gave up, but the hole was never forgotten.

A few years later a company from Onslow, near Truro, dug much deeper. According to the legend they found platforms of logs every ten feet, coconut fibre, putty, and, at about 90 feet, a flat stone carved with strange symbols. Then the shaft filled with sea water, as if the builders had flooded it on purpose.

Read this part as a story. It is the version repeated on TV and in most videos. The next section shows how much of it can actually be traced to a written record.

What the records actually say

  • Nothing was written down at the time. The earliest known mention in print is a short note from 1857, more than 60 years after 1795. Fuller newspaper accounts appear in 1861 and 1862.
  • The first detailed account is a letter by J. B. McCully, dated Truro, 2 June 1862. It names McGinnis, Smith and Vaughan, and says the Onslow company came “about seven years afterwards”, led by Simeon Lynds.
  • The stone moves. McCully places a stone “with several characters on it” at 80 feet. Later tellings put it at 90 or 95 feet. Nobody photographed it, it no longer exists, and the first drawing of its symbols appears in a book from 1949. The “translations” came later still.
  • The discoverer's age is disputed. The legend calls McGinnis a teenager. One researcher traced a Daniel McInnis born in 1758, who would have been about 37 in 1795.
  • No object from the early digs survives. Not the logs, not the fibre, not the stone.

Source: criticalenquiry.org, chapter 3: early history to 1865 · the McCully letter of 1862 · chapter 4: how the legend grew

Two hundred years of digging

WhenWhat happened
1795The legend's date for the first dig. No record from the time.
About 1803The Onslow company digs to about 90 feet; the shaft floods.
1849–50The Truro company drills and reports bringing up three small metal links. Its new shafts flood.
1861A steam pump's boiler bursts and scalds a worker to death.
1893The Oak Island Treasure Company is formed.
1909The Old Gold Salvage and Wrecking Company digs. One investor, a young Franklin D. Roosevelt, visits the site; as U.S. president he was still writing letters about the island in 1939.
1965A causeway is built so a large digging crane can reach the island. On 17 August, four men die in a shaft, overcome by gas or engine fumes.
1971A camera lowered into a cavity about 60 m down sends back faint images that some say show chests and a hand. Nothing is recovered.
2006Brothers Rick and Marty Lagina, from Michigan, buy a controlling interest in the company that owns most of the island.
2011Nova Scotia's Oak Island Treasure Act comes into force.
2014The U.S. television series The Curse of Oak Island begins on History.

Source: criticalenquiry.org · Atlas Obscura (1861 boiler, 1909) · Oak Island Tours (1909, Roosevelt) · Dundurn (17 August 1965) · Artnet (cause) · Canadian Encyclopedia (1965, 1971) · SBS (2006)

Skeptoid, a science podcast, counts six men killed in accidents on the island over the years. The Canadian Encyclopedia records the four deaths of 1965.

The theories

Because nobody knows what, if anything, is down there, people have suggested almost everything. These are theories, not findings:

  • Pirate treasure, from Captain William Kidd, Henry Morgan or Blackbeard.
  • Gold carried away by Inca refugees fleeing the Spanish conquest.
  • Religious relics hidden by the Knights Templar.
  • Lost manuscripts, or the jewels of Queen Marie Antoinette of France.

None of these has any object, document or dig result to support it.

Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia

What science says

  • The rock dissolves. Part of the island sits on limestone, gypsum and anhydrite, rocks that water slowly dissolves. That creates natural holes, caves and underground water paths.
  • Natural sinkholes are real there. In 1878 a team ploughing a field on the island broke through into a natural sinkhole about 12 feet deep, away from the Money Pit.
  • The “flood tunnels” have never been found. No treasure hunter has ever uncovered and traced a man-made tunnel from the sea to the pit. Sea water seeping through cracks in that kind of rock explains the flooding without one.
  • The geologists' view: a geologist who studied the shafts in the 1960s wrote that the water enters “through a natural course”, through caves typical of that limestone and gypsum.

Source: criticalenquiry.org: the flood tunnel · Top ten Money Pit myths · Skeptoid #129

What has actually been found

  • Small, disputed items: the three metal links of 1849 and a tiny scrap of parchment with two letters on it. Neither survives to be examined.
  • Coconut fibre: in 1970 samples from the island were identified as coconut fibre and radiocarbon-dated to about the year 1200, according to Skeptoid. Fibre can wash ashore, and no large deposit was ever shown.
  • TV finds: the television series has shown old coins, a lead cross and other objects. We found no independent museum or university study confirming any of them as part of a buried treasure.
  • Treasure: none. The Canadian Encyclopedia: “no treasure has been found.”

Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia · Skeptoid #129

Can anyone dig there?

No. The island is privately owned, and searching for treasure there is governed by a special Nova Scotia law, the Oak Island Treasure Act, in force since 1 January 2011. Under it:

  • A searcher needs a licence from the provincial minister, and pays the province a royalty on anything kept.
  • Anyone who finds treasure must report it to the minister in writing, under oath.
  • “Treasure” means precious stones or metals that are not in their natural state, such as coins or jewellery, not gold still in the rock.

Visitors can see the island only on organised tours. Oak Island sits in Mi'kma'ki, the traditional territory of the Mi'kmaq, near the community of Western Shore in Lunenburg County.

Source: Oak Island Treasure Act, S.N.S. 2010, c. 39

Why the story never ends

Each new dig adds holes, flooded shafts and odd bits of wood and metal, which become new clues for the next group. Some of those bits come from the earlier treasure hunters themselves. After two centuries the island is honeycombed with old shafts, and telling a natural cave from an 1850 tunnel, or a 1909 tunnel from an 1850 one, is genuinely hard. That uncertainty is what keeps the mystery alive.
