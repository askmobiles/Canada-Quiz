The thing to know about British Columbia. In most of Canada, the Crown signed treaties with First Nations before the land was settled. In British Columbia that almost never happened. The BC Treaty Commission puts it plainly: when BC joined Canada in 1871 only 14 treaties on Vancouver Island had been signed, and Aboriginal title to the rest of the province was left unresolved. That is not a footnote to BC's story. It is the reason treaty negotiations are still going on today.

Where British Columbia is

British Columbia is Canada's Pacific province — the only one with a coastline on the Pacific Ocean. Alberta is to the east, Yukon and the Northwest Territories to the north, and the American state of Washington along most of the southern border. Alaska wraps around the top of BC's coast.

The capital is not in the biggest city, and it is not even on the mainland. Victoria sits at the southern tip of Vancouver Island, so getting to the provincial legislature from most of the province means a ferry or a plane.

What the land is like

British Columbia is the mountain province. Range after range runs north to south — the Rockies along the Alberta border, then the Columbias, then the Coast Mountains dropping straight into the sea. Between them are long, narrow valleys and long, narrow lakes, because the mountains decide where the water can go.

The coast is not a smooth line. It is thousands of islands, and fjords that cut so far inland that a boat can sail into the mountains. Vancouver Island alone is larger than Nova Scotia.

How big is that, really?

  • British Columbia is bigger than all four Atlantic provinces put together — and the space left over is almost exactly the size of Newfoundland and Labrador.
  • Only about 2% of the province is fresh water, which is remarkably little for a place this famous for its lakes. Compare Manitoba, where it is closer to 15%.

How people make a living

Forests, mining, and the sea have paid BC's bills for a long time, and the ports still matter enormously: goods heading to and from Asia land in British Columbia first. Film and television production, technology, and tourism have grown into large employers too, mostly in the southwest corner where most of the people live.

The Okanagan valley is orchard and vineyard country, which surprises people who picture BC as nothing but rain and cedar. The valleys east of the Coast Mountains are dry, because the mountains catch the rain on the way in.

How British Columbia became a province

People have lived on this coast for thousands of years, and the nations here — Haida, Nuu-chah-nulth, Coast Salish, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw, Nisga'a, Secwépemc, Ktunaxa and many more — were not few and were not scattered. The coast supported large permanent towns, because the sea fed them.

Britain set up two colonies here, on Vancouver Island and on the mainland, and joined them in 1866. Gold brought a rush of newcomers up the Fraser River. The new colony was deeply in debt, and Canada offered a railway to the Pacific if it would join. It joined on 20 July 1871, as the sixth province.

The part usually left out is that the land was never given up. Elsewhere in Canada treaties came first; here the colony was created, and then the province, on land whose ownership had not been settled with the people living on it. Modern treaty negotiations only began in 1993.

Things most people do not know

  • British Columbia's official fossil is an elasmosaur — a long-necked sea reptile. It was added to the province's emblems in 2023, which makes it one of the newest official symbols in Canada.
  • The provincial mammal is the spirit bear — a black bear born with white or cream fur. It is not a polar bear and it is not an albino.
  • The provincial motto means “splendour without diminishment”, and the province explains it: it refers to the sun on the shield, which is setting but never grows smaller.
  • Victoria is the capital, but you cannot drive there from the rest of Canada without putting your car on a boat.
  • The province has less fresh water, as a share of its area, than Manitoba, Ontario, Quebec or Saskatchewan. All those mountains hold snow and ice, not lakes.
  • Steller's jay, the provincial bird, is a loud dark-blue bird with a black crest. It is a relative of the blue jay, which is Prince Edward Island's bird instead.

Unceded land, and why it is still in the news

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. In a province where almost no treaties were signed, that recognition has to be worked out case by case, which is why British Columbia has a three-party treaty process — First Nations, the province, and Canada — that has been running since 1993.

The exception in the northeast is Treaty 8, signed in 1899, which covers part of the Peace River country and reaches across into Alberta, Saskatchewan and the Northwest Territories. A treaty boundary does not stop at a provincial one.

Why the mountains decide almost everything

Air coming off the Pacific is pushed up over the Coast Mountains, cools, and drops its water on the west side. What crosses to the far side is dry. That single mechanism explains the rainforest on the coast, the orchards of the Okanagan and the sagebrush near Kamloops — three landscapes in one province, all caused by the same range of hills.

It also explains the railway. Getting a line from the Prairies to the Pacific meant crossing four mountain ranges, not one, and the difficulty of that crossing is a large part of why British Columbia could bargain for a railway in the first place.
