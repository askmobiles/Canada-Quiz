New Brunswick is the only officially bilingual province, and it is in the Constitution. Section 16(2) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms says English and French are the official languages of New Brunswick and have equality of status and equal rights and privileges as to their use in all institutions of the legislature and government of New Brunswick. Quebec's official language is French; every other province has no official language in its own constitution at all. New Brunswick is the exception, and the federal Discover Canada guide adds that about one-third of the population lives and works in French.

Where New Brunswick is

New Brunswick is the only Maritime province attached to the rest of Canada by land. Quebec is to the north and west, Nova Scotia to the southeast across a narrow neck of land, and the American state of Maine along the west. The Bay of Fundy is to the south and the Gulf of St. Lawrence to the east.

The capital, Fredericton, is inland on the Saint John River. The biggest city, Saint John, is on the coast at the river's mouth — two different places with almost the same name, which trips up a lot of projects.

What the land is like

Most of New Brunswick is forest — a higher share than almost any other province — cut through by big rivers. The Saint John River runs the length of the province and its valley holds the farmland.

The coast comes in two kinds. The eastern shore on the Gulf of St. Lawrence is sandy and shallow. The southern shore on the Bay of Fundy is rock and mud flats, and it is where the tides do their work.

How big is that, really?

  • New Brunswick is bigger than Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island put together — 72,908 km² against their combined 60,944 km².
  • Only about 2% of the province is fresh water, which is what you would expect of a place made of rivers rather than lakes.

How people make a living

Forestry and the things made from wood — lumber, pulp, paper — have been central for a long time. Fishing and seafood processing matter on both coasts, and the Saint John area has heavy industry, including one of Canada's largest oil refineries and a deep-water port.

Being bilingual is itself an industry here. Call centres, translation and federal government work locate in New Brunswick because it can staff both languages from one labour market.

How New Brunswick became a province

This is Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet) and Peskotomuhkati (Passamaquoddy) territory, and it has been for thousands of years. The rivers that carried their canoes are the same rivers the towns were later built on.

French settlers called this region Acadie, and Acadian communities grew here from the early 1600s. In 1755 the British deported the Acadians — thousands of people removed from their farms and scattered. Many came back, and their descendants are why New Brunswick has a large French-speaking population today. The province's motto, “Hope restored”, dates from the Loyalist arrival of the 1780s, but the phrase carries a second meaning for Acadians.

New Brunswick became a separate colony in 1784 and one of the four original provinces of Canada on 1 July 1867. Its bilingual status came much later: the province passed an Official Languages Act, and section 16(2) of the Charter put the guarantee in the Constitution in 1982.

Things most people do not know

  • Parks Canada calls the Bay of Fundy's tides the highest in the world, and gives the reason: an unusual combination of resonance and the shape of the bay. At Fundy National Park the difference between high and low tide can be as much as 12 metres; at the head of the bay, Parks Canada says the tide can rise 16 metres — the height of a four-storey building.
  • There are two high and two low tides a day, and Parks Canada gives the average gap between a high and a low as six hours and thirteen minutes.
  • At Alma Beach at low tide, Parks Canada says you can walk more than a kilometre from the high-tide line out to the water.
  • New Brunswick has an official provincial soil — the Holmesville series, named in 1997. Very few places in the world have a favourite kind of dirt.
  • It also has an official provincial fishing fly, made a symbol in 1993.
  • Fredericton is the capital but Saint John is the bigger city — and Saint John is always written out in full, never “St. John”, to keep it apart from St. John's in Newfoundland and Labrador.

Why the Bay of Fundy tides are so extreme

Parks Canada gives the mechanism: resonance, plus the shape of the bay. Water in any enclosed basin has a natural rhythm at which it sloshes back and forth. The Bay of Fundy's natural rhythm happens to be close to the roughly twelve-and-a-half-hour rhythm of the Atlantic tide, so each tide arrives just as the water is ready to move that way — and the movement builds instead of cancelling out.

The narrowing and shallowing of the bay then squeezes the same volume of water into a smaller space, which forces it higher. This is the same effect as a wave steepening as it runs up a beach, scaled up to the size of a bay.

What official bilingualism actually requires

New Brunswick's Official Languages Act sets out three purposes: to ensure respect for English and French as the official languages of the province, to ensure they have equality of status and equal rights and privileges as to their use in all institutions of the province, and to set out the powers and duties of those institutions regarding the two languages.

The practical version is that a person can deal with the provincial government in either language and be answered in it — courts, hospitals, schools, licences. That is a much heavier commitment than a slogan, and it is why the guarantee sits in the Constitution rather than in an ordinary law that a later government could simply repeal.
