Nunavut, from the fact box to the hard questions

Canada's largest and newest territory, created by a land claims agreement, with three official languages, three time zones, and one person for every forty-five square kilometres. Every figure comes from a government source, and they are all listed at the bottom.

Nunavut exists because of a land claim. The Legislative Assembly of Nunavut's own account: Inuit ratified the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement in November 1992; the Prime Minister of Canada signed it on 25 May 1993 in Iqaluit; the $1.1 billion settlement was proclaimed on 9 July 1993 in Kugluktuk; and the territory was created on 1 April 1999. Almost every other border in Canada was drawn by governments negotiating with each other. This one was negotiated by the people who live inside it.

Where Nunavut is

Nunavut is the north and northeast of Canada: the mainland above the treeline west of Hudson Bay, plus most of the Arctic Archipelago. The Northwest Territories is to the west, Manitoba and Ontario to the south, and Quebec and Newfoundland and Labrador across the water to the southeast.

There are no roads between communities. Every one of them is reached by air, or by ship during the short season when the sea is open. The capital, Iqaluit, is on Baffin Island.

What the land is like

Nunavut is almost entirely above the treeline: tundra, rock, ice and sea. The land is not flat — Baffin and Ellesmere islands have serious mountains and glaciers — but there are no forests, which is why the territory has no official tree.

The sea is as much a part of the territory as the land. Sea ice is a surface people travel and hunt on for much of the year, which is why changes in when it forms and breaks up matter here in a way they do not further south.

How big is that, really?

  • Nunavut is about 21% of Canada — more than a fifth of the whole country, in one territory.
  • It is bigger than Ontario and British Columbia put together.
  • Divide the land area by the population and Nunavut has roughly one person for every 45 square kilometres. Prince Edward Island has about 32 people per square kilometre. That arithmetic is ours, from the two tables at the bottom of this page, and you can check it.

How people make a living

Government is the largest employer, and mining — gold and iron ore especially — is the largest private industry. Hunting, fishing and sewing are not hobbies here: they feed and clothe households, and country food is a serious part of what people eat.

Inuit art is a genuine export industry. Carving and printmaking from Nunavut communities are sold internationally, and some communities are known worldwide for a particular style.

Everything else is expensive, because everything else arrives by plane or by ship. That single fact shapes the cost of food, housing and construction across the whole territory.

How Nunavut was created

Inuit have lived across the Arctic for thousands of years, and were living here long before Canada existed. Nunavut means “our land” in Inuktitut, which tells you what the territory is for.

Through the twentieth century Inuit were increasingly governed from far away, moved into settlements, and had children sent to residential schools. Out of that came an organised political movement: rather than asking for a better administration, Inuit organisations negotiated a land claims agreement that included creating a new territory with its own government.

Inuit ratified the agreement in November 1992. The Prime Minister signed it in Iqaluit on 25 May 1993, the $1.1 billion settlement was proclaimed in Kugluktuk that July, and on 1 April 1999 Nunavut was separated from the Northwest Territories and began governing itself.

Things most people do not know

  • Nunavut has three official languages, not four. Inuktut, English and French. Inuktut is one official language that covers both Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun — so a list of four names describes three languages, and that trips up almost everybody.
  • Nunavut's official animal is a dog — the Canadian Inuit dog, a working sled dog, not a pet breed.
  • The territory spans three time zones, and Southampton Island keeps Eastern Standard Time all year while the rest of Nunavut changes its clocks.
  • It has no official tree, because almost the whole territory is above the treeline.
  • Nunavut is the largest province or territory and has the fewest people. Ontario, the most populous, would fit inside it almost twice.
  • No road connects any two Nunavut communities. Travel between them is by air, or by ship during the open-water season.

Going deeper

Why a land claim created a territory

Most Canadian land claims settle questions of ownership, money and rights to hunt and fish. The Nunavut Agreement did that and added something unusual: a commitment to divide the Northwest Territories and create a new public government in a territory where Inuit are the great majority of the population.

That design matters. The Government of Nunavut is not an Inuit government in law — it is a public government that everyone in the territory votes for. But because of who lives there, it functions in Inuktut, runs on Inuit priorities and is staffed largely by Inuit. Self-determination was achieved through an ordinary public institution rather than a separate one.

Three time zones and one island that opts out

The federal time page records that Nunavut covers three time zones, that all of them change at 2:00 local time, and that Southampton Island remains on Eastern Standard Time all year long. A territory spanning more than 2 million square kilometres of longitude cannot sensibly run on one clock.

Nunavut did try. For a period after it was created, the whole territory used one time zone, and it was abandoned because it did not work in practice — a reminder that time zones exist to suit people, and get changed back when they do not.
