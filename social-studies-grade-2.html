🌍 Grade 2 Social Studies: Traditions and the World

A Grade 2 lesson on family traditions, the celebrations Canadians share, and the big wide world, with a quiz, endless practice, a challenge round, and printable worksheets with answer sheets. No accounts, nothing to download.

Curriculum: Ontario Social Studies, Grade 2: Strand A, Heritage and Identity: Changing Family and Community Traditions, and Strand B, People and Environments: Global Communities. Unofficial practice, not approved by any school board. Read the official curriculum.

📖 For teachers and parents

Six tabs, one topic. Learn is a short lesson with a timeline. Quiz is ten questions with an explanation after each answer. Practice never ends and keeps no score. Challenge is twelve harder questions. Worksheet and Answers print — pick the same letter for both, and the answer sheet matches the worksheet. Three versions, so a class can have three different sheets side by side.

📖 Families, heritage and change

Every family has its own story. Families pass on traditions, like special foods, songs, stories and languages. Some traditions stay the same for a long time. Others change.

Families

Many kinds of families

Families come in many shapes. Some children live with a mom and a dad. Some live with one parent, two moms or two dads, grandparents, or an adoptive or foster family. What makes a family is caring for each other.

Family tree

A family tree

A family tree is a picture of your family. You are at the bottom. Your parents are above you, and your grandparents are above them. It shows where your family came from.

Heritage

Heritage

Heritage is what a family or community passes on to its children: languages, foods, songs, clothing, stories and celebrations. Old photos, letters and special objects help us remember the past.

Stories

Elders and stories

In many First Nations, Inuit and Métis communities, Elders pass on knowledge, history and language by telling stories out loud. This is called oral tradition. Children listen and later tell the stories to their own children.

Change

Traditions change

Traditions can change over time. A family that moves to Canada may keep its old celebrations and also start new ones, like Canada Day. Families who live far apart may share a holiday by video call.

October

Thanksgiving in Canada

In Canada, Thanksgiving is on the second Monday of October. It has been on that day since 1957. Families give thanks for the harvest and share a meal. Long before that, First Nations held harvest feasts in the fall.

July 1

Canada Day

On July 1, 1867, Canada became a country. July 1 used to be called Dominion Day. In 1982 its name changed to Canada Day. Today people celebrate with flags, music, parades and fireworks.

Then and now

Then and now

When your grandparents were young, families sent letters by mail and took photos on film. Today many families send messages and photos on phones. The way we do things changes, but families still keep in touch.

📖 Celebrations across Canada

Canadian families come from many backgrounds, so Canada has many celebrations. Learning about them helps us understand and respect our neighbours.

New Year

Lunar New Year

Lunar New Year comes in late January or February. Many Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese Canadian families celebrate. Children often get money in red envelopes, because red stands for good luck. There are lion dances, drums and family visits.

Harvest

Thai Pongal

Thai Pongal is a harvest festival celebrated by Tamil families in January. It marks the rice harvest and the return of longer days. Families cook a sweet rice dish called pongal. In Canada, January is also Tamil Heritage Month.

Eid

Eid al-Fitr

During the month of Ramadan, many Muslims fast from dawn until sunset. Eid al-Fitr is the celebration at the end of Ramadan. Families go to a special morning prayer, wear new clothes, visit friends and share treats and gifts.

Lights

Diwali

Diwali is a festival of lights, in October or November. Hindus celebrate it, and Sikhs and Jains have special days at the same time. Families light small lamps called diyas, make colourful rangoli designs, share sweets and watch fireworks.

Hanukkah

Hanukkah

Hanukkah is a Jewish festival of lights that lasts eight nights, in late November or December. Each night, families light one more candle on a menorah. Children play with a spinning top called a dreidel and eat potato pancakes called latkes.

Christmas

Christmas

Christmas, on December 25, is when Christians celebrate the birth of Jesus. Many families decorate a tree, sing carols, give gifts and share a big meal. Some go to church on Christmas Eve or Christmas morning.

Powwow

Powwows

Many First Nations hold powwows, gatherings with drumming, singing and dancing. Drum groups sing around a large drum. Dancers wear regalia, special clothing that is often handmade. Many powwows welcome visitors, who are asked to be respectful.

June 21

National Indigenous Peoples Day

On June 21, Canada celebrates First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples. It is the summer solstice, the longest day of the year, a time of year when many Indigenous peoples have long celebrated. The day began in 1996.

Métis and Inuit

Fiddles and throat singing

Métis celebrations often have fiddle music and a fast dance called the Red River Jig. Many Métis wear a colourful woven sash with pride. In Inuit throat singing, two people, usually women, face each other and trade sounds until one laughs or runs out of breath.

📖 Our world

Canada is one country on a big round Earth. People live in hot places, cold places, wet places and dry places, and the weather where they live shapes their homes, clothes and food.

Globe

Globes and maps

The Earth is round like a ball. A globe is a round model of the Earth. A map is a flat picture of a place. A compass rose on a map shows north, south, east and west.

Continents

Seven continents

The Earth has seven continents, huge pieces of land: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America and South America. Asia is the biggest. Canada is in North America, with the United States and Mexico.

Oceans

Five oceans

Most of the Earth is covered by water. The five oceans are the Pacific, the Atlantic, the Indian, the Southern and the Arctic. The Pacific is the biggest. Canada touches three of them: the Pacific, the Atlantic and the Arctic.

Equator

The equator and the poles

The equator is a pretend line around the middle of the Earth. Places near it are hot all year. The North Pole is at the top of the Earth and the South Pole is at the bottom. Both are very cold. The South Pole is on Antarctica.

Climate

Weather and climate

Weather is what it is like outside today: sunny, rainy or snowy. Climate is the usual weather of a place over many years. Some places are hot and dry, some hot and rainy, and some cold for most of the year.

Ways of life

Climate shapes how we live

In snowy places, people wear warm coats and boots and heat their homes. In hot deserts, people wear loose clothes that cover the skin from the sun. Long ago, Inuit families built snow houses called igloos on winter hunting trips.

Canada

Canada in the world

Canada is the second-biggest country in the world, after Russia. It is north of the equator. Its longest border is with the United States, the longest border between two countries in the world. When it is winter in Canada, it is summer in Australia.

Newcomers

Families from around the world

Canadians have family roots all over the world. In the 2021 census, people named more than 450 different ethnic or cultural origins. Almost 1 in 4 people in Canada were born in another country. Recent newcomers came most often from India, the Philippines and China.

🗓️ Timeline

  • 1867 Canada becomes a country on July 1
  • 1947 The first Canadian citizenship law takes effect
  • 1957 Thanksgiving is set on the second Monday of October
  • 1965 Canada's maple leaf flag is raised for the first time
  • 1971 Canada adopts multiculturalism as an official policy
  • 1980 O Canada becomes the national anthem
  • 1982 Dominion Day is renamed Canada Day
  • 1988 Parliament passes the Canadian Multiculturalism Act
  • 1995 February is recognized as Black History Month
  • 1996 June 21 becomes National Aboriginal Day
  • 1997 Canada Post issues its first Lunar New Year stamp
  • 2016 January is declared Tamil Heritage Month
  • 2017 June 21 is renamed National Indigenous Peoples Day
  • 2021 The first National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, September 30

About this quiz

This page covers Grade 2 social studies: families and how they pass on heritage, how traditions change over time, the celebrations Canadians share, from Lunar New Year, Thai Pongal, Eid, Diwali, Hanukkah and Christmas to powwows, National Indigenous Peoples Day, Canada Day and Thanksgiving, and then the world: globes and maps, continents, oceans, the equator and poles, climate, and the many countries Canadian families come from.

It works six ways. Read the lesson and its timeline, take the ten-question quiz, practise for as long as you like with no score, try the harder challenge round, then print a worksheet and its answer sheet. There are three versions, lettered A, B and C, and the answer sheet with the same letter matches its worksheet exactly.

Good to know

  • National Indigenous Peoples Day is on June 21, the summer solstice, the longest day of the year.
  • In 1971, Canada became the first country in the world to adopt an official multiculturalism policy.
