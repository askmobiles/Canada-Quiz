📜 Grade 5 History: First Peoples and Europeans, to 1713

A Grade 5 lesson on the First Peoples and the Europeans who came before 1713, with a quiz, endless practice, a challenge round, and printable worksheets with answer sheets. No accounts, nothing to download.

Curriculum: Ontario Social Studies, Grade 5, Strand A (Heritage and Identity: Interactions of Indigenous Peoples and Europeans Prior to 1713, in What Would Eventually Become Canada). 📖 The First Peoples

For thousands of years before any European arrived, this land was home to many nations, each with its own language, laws, beliefs and way of life, shaped by the land around them.

Thousands of years

Many nations, many ways of life

On the Pacific coast, nations fished for salmon and built plank houses of cedar. On the Plains, nations followed the buffalo. In the Arctic, Inuit hunted seals and whales. Around the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence, many nations farmed.

Before contact

The Haudenosaunee

The Haudenosaunee lived in villages of longhouses, each shared by several related families. They grew the Three Sisters: corn, beans and squash. Their Confederacy united several nations under the Great Law of Peace.

Before contact

The Wendat

The Wendat (Huron) lived south of Georgian Bay in large farming villages. They were great traders, exchanging corn with nations to the north for furs and fish.

Before contact

The Anishinaabe and the Cree

Across the forests of the north, Anishinaabe and Cree families moved with the seasons to hunt, fish and gather. Birchbark canoes let them travel the lakes and rivers.

Before contact

The Mi'kmaq

The Mi'kmaq lived in what are now the Maritimes and the Gaspé, moving between the coast in summer and the forest in winter. They were among the first to meet European fishers.

Before contact

Wampum

Wampum belts, made of white and purple shell beads, recorded agreements and important events. A belt was a promise that people could see and remember.

📖 Meeting the Europeans

Europeans came for fish, furs and a route to Asia. What followed was trade, alliance, disease and conflict, and it changed life for everyone.

About 1000

The Norse

About a thousand years ago, Norse sailors from Greenland built a small camp at L'Anse aux Meadows, at the northern tip of Newfoundland. It is the earliest known European site in Canada, and the only confirmed Viking site in North America outside Greenland.

1497

John Cabot

Sailing for England, John Cabot reached the coast of Newfoundland or Cape Breton. He reported seas so full of cod that fishing boats from Europe soon came every summer.

1534–1535

Jacques Cartier

Sailing for France, Cartier raised a cross at Gaspé in 1534. In 1535 he sailed up the St. Lawrence to the St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages of Stadacona (Quebec City) and Hochelaga (Montreal). The name "Canada" comes from kanata, the Iroquoian word for village.

1605–1608

Port-Royal and Quebec

French settlers built Port-Royal in Acadia in 1605. In 1608 Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec. That same year, French traders hired Mathieu Da Costa, a Black interpreter who may have travelled to Acadia.

1600s

Furs, alliances and missions

The French allied with the Wendat, Anishinaabe and Innu, trading metal pots, knives and cloth for beaver furs. Catholic missionaries, many of them Jesuits, came to convert First Nations, and built Sainte-Marie among the Hurons in 1639.

1600s

Disease

Europeans brought diseases such as smallpox that First Nations had never met. Epidemics killed a huge share of the people in many communities, including more than half of the Wendat between 1634 and 1642.

1649

War and the Wendat

Weakened by disease, the Wendat were attacked by the Haudenosaunee in a war over the fur trade. In 1649 their villages were abandoned and the survivors scattered. Some went to live near Quebec, where the Huron-Wendat Nation lives today at Wendake.

1663–1673

New France grows

The king of France took direct control of New France in 1663. About 800 young women, the filles du roi, were sent to marry settlers and start families, and the colony's population grew.

1670

The Hudson's Bay Company

England gave the Hudson's Bay Company the right to trade in all the lands draining into Hudson Bay. Cree and other nations brought furs to its posts by the bay.

1701

The Great Peace of Montreal

Representatives of about 40 First Nations met the French in Montreal and signed a peace treaty, ending nearly a century of war. Many signed with a drawing of their nation's symbol, such as a turtle or a bear.

About this quiz

This page covers the Grade 5 unit on First Nations, Inuit and the Europeans who came before 1713: how nations lived across the land, the first European contacts, New France and the fur trade, and the alliances, disease and conflicts that followed, up to the Great Peace of Montreal.

It works six ways. Read the lesson and its timeline, take the ten-question quiz, practise for as long as you like with no score, try the harder challenge round, then print a worksheet and its answer sheet. There are three versions, lettered A, B and C, and the answer sheet with the same letter matches its worksheet exactly.

Good to know

  • L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, a Norse camp from about the year 1000, is the earliest known European site in Canada.
  • The Great Peace of Montreal in 1701 was signed by about 40 First Nations and the French, many of them signing with a drawing of their nation's symbol.

Common questions

Which grade is this in my province?

Do the worksheet and the answer sheet really match?

Yes. Worksheet A always has the same questions in the same order, and Answer Sheet A lists those answers, including the order of the timeline events and what a good written answer should mention.
