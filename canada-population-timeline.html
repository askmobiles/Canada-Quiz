📈 Canada's Population: The Whole Timeline, 1666 to Today

The first count found 3,215 people. The latest official figure is 41,417,056. In between is a story with two world wars, a baby boom, a record-smashing immigration surge, and — right now — the first population decline Canada has ever recorded.

Last checked 9 August 2026

The headline first. Canada's population is falling. Statistics Canada put it at 41,417,056 on 1 April 2026, down for a third quarter in a row. The 2025 calendar year brought a decline of 102,436 people — the first annual drop in records that go back to 1946. Two years earlier Canada was growing faster than in any year since 1957. The same lever caused both: temporary residents.

Before Canada: the first census, 1666

In 1666 Jean Talon, the first Intendant of New France, walked from door to door and counted everybody. He found 3,215 people in 538 families — 625 at Montreal, 547 at Quebec City, 455 at Trois-Rivières. He wrote down each person's name, age, sex, marital status and job. That is what makes it a real census rather than a guess, and it is generally called the first true census taken anywhere in North America.

The count also revealed a problem: 2,034 men against 1,181 women. That imbalance is the reason France sent the Filles du Roi, roughly 800 young women whose passage the king paid, between 1663 and 1673. A great many French-Canadian families today trace back to them.

Who was left out — and this matters. Talon's census counted settlers. It did not count Indigenous peoples at all, nor the religious orders, nor the soldiers. Almost every colonial count that followed did the same. Before sustained European contact, the Indigenous population of what is now Canada is estimated at 350,000 to 500,000 people, and some estimates run far higher. By 1867 it had fallen to roughly 100,000 to 125,000 First Nations people, about 10,000 Métis and about 2,000 Inuit — a collapse of 70 to 80 percent or more, driven by epidemic disease against which there was no immunity, together with starvation and warfare. Every early number on this page should be read as a count of settlers, not of everyone living here.

Population estimates, which adjust for people missed and for temporary residents, run about two to three percent higher. That is why Canada passed 30 million by estimate in 1997 but only by census count in 2001. Mixing the two series is the easiest way to publish a wrong number.

Each ten million took less time than the one before — until now. That acceleration has stopped abruptly, and the next milestone is further away than it looked two years ago.

The moments that bent the line

The First World War and the flu that followed

Three blows at once. Immigration, which had been running at one of the highest rates any country has ever recorded, stopped almost completely. About 61,000 Canadians died in the war, nearly all of them young men. Then the influenza pandemic of 1918 and 1919 killed roughly 50,000 more, concentrated in people aged 20 to 40 — almost as many as the war itself.

The baby boom

From 1946 to 1965 Canada had children at a rate it had not seen in generations. The peak year was 1959, when the average woman had 3.94 children and about 479,300 babies were born — nearly double the number thirty years earlier. In 1957 the population grew 3.3 percent in a single year, boosted by boom births and by refugees from the Hungarian Revolution. That 1957 figure stood as the record for 66 years.

Fertility today: a record low

In 2024 the average was 1.25 children per woman, the lowest ever recorded in Canada and the second year running below 1.30, which demographers call ultra-low fertility. The rate has been under the replacement level of 2.1 since 1971. From the 1959 peak of 3.94 to 1.25 is a fall of just under 70 percent. The average age of mothers is now 31.8 years, up from 26.7 in 1976.

When immigration took over from births

For most of Canadian history the population grew because Canadians had children. That changed in the late 1990s — Statistics Canada dates the durable crossover to 1999, and some of its own publications say as early as 1995. By 2023, immigration accounted for 97.6 percent of all population growth, and births net of deaths for just 2.4 percent.

What is happening right now

In 2022 Canada grew by more than a million people in twelve months for the first time ever. In 2023 it grew by 1,271,872 — a rate of 3.2 percent, the highest since 1957. Then in 2024 the federal government set out to reduce the number of temporary residents, cutting permanent resident targets and, for the first time, planning a fall in temporary residents of roughly 445,000 in each of 2025 and 2026.

It worked, and faster than expected.
