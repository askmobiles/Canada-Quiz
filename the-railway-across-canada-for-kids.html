The Railway That Had to Cross the Mountains

To hold the country together, Canada promised to build a railway all the way to the Pacific Ocean. Then somebody had to actually do it.

In 1871 British Columbia agreed to join Canada, but only if Canada built a railway to reach it. That meant laying track across prairies, forests, swamps and then the Rocky Mountains.

The mountains were the hardest part. Workers had to blast tunnels through solid rock and build bridges over deep canyons, with hand tools and dynamite.

More than 17,000 men came from China to do that work. They were given the most dangerous jobs and were paid about half of what other workers were paid.

Many of them died in explosions, rockslides and accidents, and from illness and cold. Nobody kept a proper count. Historians say at least 600 died, and possibly many more.

The last spike was hammered in on the 7th of November 1885, at a place in British Columbia called Craigellachie. Trains could now cross Canada.

When the railway was finished, Canada made a law charging Chinese people money just to enter the country. It started at fifty dollars and rose to five hundred, which was more than most people could save in a year. It kept families apart for decades.

On the 22nd of June 2006, the Prime Minister stood up in Parliament and apologised for it. Saying sorry does not undo it, but a country that tells the truth about its own past is a stronger country.

Did you know? The railway is on old photographs and postcards. The people who built the hardest part of it are mostly not, and that is why we say their part out loud.
