🏥 Health coverage for children in Ontario

If you have just arrived in Ontario with a child, the most common thing you will be told is wrong. There is no three-month wait any more. This page is about what your child is actually entitled to, what you have to do to get it, and what is still not covered.

Last checked 23 September 2026 — government rules change, so check the official pages linked at the end before you act

The short answer

If your child is eligible for OHIP, coverage starts straight away and costs nothing. Doctor visits, hospital care, emergency care and the routine childhood vaccines are paid for. Prescription medicine from a pharmacy, the dentist and eyeglasses are not paid for by OHIP — but there are separate programmes for all three, and a child under 25 can get prescriptions free.

The thing almost everybody gets wrong. Ontario used to make you wait three months before OHIP started. That wait was removed from the regulations in March 2020. The government's own page now says: “There is no longer a waiting period for OHIP coverage. If you are eligible, you will have immediate health insurance coverage.” A great deal of advice online still tells newcomers to buy private insurance for those three months. For Ontario, that advice is out of date.

Who can get OHIP for a child

There is no separate test for children. A child has to meet the same rules as an adult: all three of the basic conditions, and at least one of the status categories.

The three basic conditions

  • Ontario is the child's main home.
  • The child will be physically in Ontario for 153 days in any 12-month period.
  • The child will be physically in Ontario for at least 153 of the first 183 days after moving here.

That 153-day rule is about staying covered, not about getting covered. You do not wait for 153 days before applying — the government says you can apply as soon as you arrive, and the forms ask you to confirm the child will be here that long.

And at least one of these

  • Canadian citizen.
  • Permanent resident.
  • An Indigenous person registered under the Indian Act.
  • Applying for permanent residence, where Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada has confirmed it reviewed the application and the eligibility requirements are met, and it has not been refused.
  • A convention refugee or other protected person.
  • In Ontario on a valid work permit, working full time for an Ontario employer for at least six months — and then, in the government's words, “your spouse and dependants may also qualify for OHIP.”
  • On a work permit under the Live-in Caregiver Program or the Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program.
Read that work permit line carefully. It says dependants may also qualify, not that they do. The child's coverage rests on the parent's job — full time, an Ontario employer, at least six months. The government does not publish what happens to the child's coverage if that job ends, and we are not going to guess at it.

A new baby: two separate jobs, not one

Parents mix these up constantly, and the reason is that the famous online bundle does not include the health card.

If the baby was born outside a hospital and without a midwife, or the form was missed, you go to a ServiceOntario centre in person. Within 90 days of the birth you bring proof of where the baby was born, such as a letter from the hospital or the attending doctor. After 90 days you need proof of the child's Canadian citizenship, such as a birth certificate — which is why the two jobs are connected, and why registering the birth promptly matters.

A child who has just arrived in Canada

A first health card cannot be applied for online. You go to a ServiceOntario centre in person. Normally an adult has to bring three separate original documents — one proving status, one proving they live in Ontario, and one proving identity. Photocopies are not accepted.

The rule worth knowing before you queue. The government's page says: “children under 16 do not require proof of residency or identity.” For a child under 16 you need the status document only — the passport, permanent resident card, birth certificate, confirmation of permanent residence or equivalent. For a child of 16 or 17, all three documents are needed, and a school report card counts as proof of residency.

Two things the government does not publish, and we will not invent: whether the child has to be there in person with you, and how long the card takes for a child who is not a newborn. The 8-week figure applies to babies registered on the infant form. Phone the ServiceOntario centre before you travel across the city with a child in tow.

If you are not a permanent resident

This is the part newcomers most need and the part the official pages say least about. Here is what is actually written down, and where it stops.

Your situationWhat the government says
Work permitIf you work full time for an Ontario employer for at least six months on a valid work permit, your spouse and dependants may also qualify. Caregiver and seasonal agricultural permits qualify without that employment test.
Study permitStudy permit holders are not on the OHIP eligibility list. Students at public universities are told to enrol in the University Health Insurance Plan; college and private-school students are told to ask their international student office. The official pages say nothing at all about a student's children. We are not going to tell you your child is ineligible, because no government page says that — ask ServiceOntario directly.
VisitorNot eligible. The Ministry's own wording: visitors to the province, people whose main home is outside Ontario, and tourists are not eligible.
Refugee claimant, waiting for a decisionOntario's list covers convention refugees and protected persons — which you become after a decision. While you wait, the federal Interim Federal Health Program covers your child. See below.

The Interim Federal Health Program, for refugee claimants

This is federal, not Ontario. In the government's words it gives “limited and temporary health care coverage until you're eligible for provincial or territorial insurance or leave Canada.” Basic coverage is free and includes hospital care in and out of patient, doctors and nurses, laboratory and diagnostic services, ambulance, and pre-natal and post-natal care.

  • Prescriptions cost $4 each to fill or refill.
  • Supplemental care — urgent dental, vision, physiotherapy, psychology and counselling, assistive devices — is covered at 70 percent. The family pays the other 30 percent, and the provider must tell you the amount before treating.
  • Broken or lost eyeglasses are replaced or repaired for children 18 and under, which is not the case for adults.
  • Take one of these to the clinic or pharmacy: the Acknowledgement of Claim letter, a Refugee Protection Claimant Document or Refugee Protection Identity Document, or an Interim Federal Health Certificate of Eligibility. Families are often turned away simply for bringing the wrong paper.

The programmes that fill the gaps

Medicine: OHIP+ makes prescriptions free under 25

If your child is 24 or under, has OHIP, and is not covered by a private plan, prescriptions on the list are filled at no charge — more than 5,900 medicines, including antibiotics, asthma inhalers, diabetes medicines, epinephrine auto-injectors, ADHD medicines and mental health medicines. There is no separate card: you take the health card or the number to any Ontario pharmacy.

The condition that catches working families. If you have a private plan through your job, your child is not eligible for OHIP+ — you use the workplace plan instead, with whatever deductible and co-payment it carries. The test is whether you have a private plan at all, not whether it covers that particular medicine. Families on Ontario Works, ODSP, long-term care or home care keep OHIP+ regardless.

If you have a private plan and the costs are still high: Trillium

This is the part most families never hear about, and it is the province's answer to the problem above. The Trillium Drug Program is for households whose prescription costs are high compared to their income, and it accepts people who already have private insurance.

  • You need a valid Ontario health card, and you must not have a plan that pays 100 percent of your drug costs.
  • The deductible is about 4 percent of your household's after-tax income, taken from the net income you reported to the Canada Revenue Agency.
  • You do not pay it all at once. It is split into four equal quarterly amounts across the program year.
  • Once the deductible is met, you pay up to 2 dollars for each prescription filled or refilled for the rest of the year.
  • The program year runs 1 August to 31 July. Apply by 30 September to be reimbursed for eligible drugs from the previous year.
The catch worth knowing before you count. Only what your household paid out of its own pocket counts toward the deductible. Anything your private plan, a drug company or a discount card paid does not count. So the co-payments you are left with are what builds toward it — which is exactly the situation a family with a weak workplace plan is in.

You apply through the Ontario Drug Benefit online applications and forms website. Paper forms can be requested by phone at 416-642-3038 or 1-800-575-5386, or by email at trillium@ontariodrugbenefit.ca.

A child already covered by OHIP+ does not need this — their prescriptions are free already. Trillium is for the family that was told no because a workplace plan exists.

Dental: two programmes, different income limits

OHIP does not pay a dentist. Two programmes do, and a family on a modest income may qualify for one or the other.

Two traps for a family that has just arrived. The federal plan needs a Canadian tax return for the previous year, which a family that arrived this year does not have. Healthy Smiles Ontario has a way round the same problem: an application through a guarantor, for families without a Social Insurance Number or a tax filing history. If you have just landed, that is the form to ask about.

Equipment: the Assistive Devices Program

For a child with a physical disability expected to last six months or longer — wheelchairs, hearing aids, communication aids, prosthetic limbs, visual aids, respiratory equipment. It pays 75 percent of the approved price, and up to the full amount for families on Ontario Works, ODSP or Assistance for Children with Severe Disabilities. Income is not a factor, but a valid Ontario health card is required.

What it costs

OHIP itself has no premium. There is an Ontario Health Premium collected through income tax, and it confuses people, so here is the government's own sentence: “The health premium is not linked to OHIP and does not affect a person's eligibility to receive health care in Ontario.” It is nothing for taxable income of $20,000 or less, and rises to $900 at over $200,600.

  • Doctor, hospital, emergency, medically necessary tests, for a covered child: nothing.
  • Routine vaccines, one eye exam a year under 20, physiotherapy at a funded clinic under 20: nothing.
  • Prescriptions under OHIP+ with no private plan: nothing. With a private plan: whatever that plan charges.
  • Dentist and eyeglasses: full private cost unless one of the programmes above applies.
  • Birth certificate $25; registering the birth and the Social Insurance Number are free; registering a birth more than 12 months late costs $50.
  • Under the federal programme for refugee claimants: $4 a prescription, and 30 percent of urgent dental, vision and similar care.

Where to get help

  • ServiceOntario — health cards and OHIP registration. You can book an appointment and find a location online, and many centres open extended hours including Saturdays.
  • Ministry of Health general enquiries — 1-800-268-1153 toll free, 416-327-4327 in Toronto, TTY 1-800-387-5559.
  • Health811 — dial 811, or 711 for TTY. Free, confidential advice from a registered nurse, 24 hours a day, with an online chat as well.
  • A Community Health Centre — this is the right place if your child is not covered yet. The government names new immigrants and people without health insurance among the populations these centres exist to serve.
  • Your local public health unit — for vaccines, school immunization records and the free dental screening done at school in kindergarten and Grade 2.
  • A settlement agency — free and funded by the federal government, but note the eligibility: permanent residents, resettled refugees, approved applicants and protected persons. Work permit and study permit families are generally not on that list and should try a Community Health Centre or a municipal newcomer service instead.

What we could not find out

These are questions a parent will reasonably ask, and the government does not publish an answer to any of them. We would rather say so than fill the gaps with something that sounds right.

  • What a family is charged for an ambulance. The figure repeated everywhere online is not on any government page we could reach.
  • Whether a child of a study permit holder can get OHIP. Not stated either way.
  • Whether the child has to come with you to ServiceOntario, and how long a card takes for a child who is not a newborn.
  • Whether care is covered between the day you apply and the day the card arrives.
  • Whether the free routine vaccines are free for a child who has no OHIP. The pages say vaccines are free if you meet the eligibility criteria, without saying what those are. Ask your public health unit.
  • Whether a Community Health Centre charges an uninsured family, and whether Health811 needs a health card.
  • What happens to a child's coverage when the parent's full-time job or work permit ends.
