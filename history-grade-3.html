📜 Grade 3 History: Early Canada, 1780–1850

A Grade 3 lesson on the communities of early Canada, with a quiz, endless practice, a challenge round, and printable worksheets with answer sheets. No accounts, nothing to download.

📖 Who lived here?

Around 1800, many different communities lived in what is now Canada. First Nations had lived here for thousands of years. French families farmed along the St. Lawrence. New settlers kept arriving.

Long before 1780

First Nations

First Nations lived all across the land long before settlers came. In what is now southern Ontario lived the Anishinaabe and the Haudenosaunee. They knew how to travel by canoe, hunt, fish and grow corn, beans and squash, and they shared that knowledge with newcomers.

1700s

French communities

French-speaking families, called Canadiens, farmed long, narrow fields beside the St. Lawrence River in what is now Quebec. Each village had a church, and the river was their road.

1780s

The Loyalists arrive

After a war in the American colonies, thousands of people who stayed loyal to the British king moved north. Many started new farms along the St. Lawrence River and Lake Ontario, in what became Upper Canada.

1780s

Haudenosaunee allies

Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee people who had helped the British also moved north. Led by Joseph Brant, many settled along the Grand River. Others settled at the Bay of Quinte. Their communities are still there today.

1780s

Black Loyalists

About 3,000 Black Loyalists came to Nova Scotia, promised freedom and land. They built a town called Birchtown. Most were given poor land or none, but they built churches and schools and kept their community going.

1800s

Freedom seekers

People escaping slavery in the United States came north to Upper Canada, where a 1793 law had begun to end slavery. Black families built farms, churches and schools in communities such as Amherstburg, Chatham and Buxton. At Oro, near Lake Simcoe, Black soldiers from the War of 1812 were given land.

1800s

The Métis

In the West, the Métis grew into a people of their own, with First Nations and European roots. They hunted buffalo, worked in the fur trade and lived along the Red River.

1830s–1840s

New settlers from across the sea

Thousands of settlers came by ship from England, Scotland and Ireland. The trip across the Atlantic took weeks, and many arrived with very little.

📖 Everyday life

There were no cars, no electricity and no stores full of food. Families grew, built and made most of what they needed, and neighbours helped each other.

First year

Clearing the land

A new family's land was usually covered in thick forest. They cut down trees with axes, burned the stumps or pulled them out with oxen, and planted crops between them.

First home

The log cabin

The first home was often a one-room log cabin. Gaps between the logs were filled with mud or moss. A big fireplace was used for heat, light and cooking.

All year

Children worked too

Children fed animals, carried water and wood, picked berries, weeded the garden and looked after younger children. School came when there was time.

Many grades

The one-room school

Children of all ages learned together in one room with one teacher. They wrote on slates with chalk and walked to school, sometimes a long way.

Neighbours

Bees

A bee was when neighbours came together to do a big job in one day: raising a barn, clearing a field or making quilts. Afterwards everyone shared a big meal.

In the village

Mills and the general store

A grist mill ground wheat into flour, and a sawmill cut logs into boards. Both used water power from a river. The general store sold things families could not make, like tea, salt, nails and cloth.

Getting around

Travel

Roads were muddy trails full of stumps. People travelled by canoe, boat, horse and wagon, or on foot. In winter, a sleigh on the snow was often the easiest way to go.

Making things

Made at home

Families made their own candles from animal fat, soap from fat and ashes, and cloth from wool they spun themselves. Maple sap was boiled into sugar every spring, something First Nations taught settlers.

🗓️ Timeline

  • 1783 Loyalists, including Black Loyalists, arrive in Nova Scotia
  • 1784 Haudenosaunee allies settle along the Grand River
  • 1791 Upper Canada and Lower Canada are created
  • 1793 Upper Canada passes a law to limit slavery
  • 1812 A war with the United States begins
  • 1832 The Rideau Canal opens
  • 1834 Slavery ends in British North America
  • 1847 Thousands of famine migrants arrive from Ireland

About this quiz

This page covers the Grade 3 unit on communities in Canada from 1780 to 1850: who lived here, from First Nations to French, Loyalist, Black and Métis communities and settlers from across the sea, and what everyday life was like on a pioneer farm and in a village.

It works six ways. Read the lesson and its timeline, take the ten-question quiz, practise for as long as you like with no score, try the harder challenge round, then print a worksheet and its answer sheet. There are three versions, lettered A, B and C, and the answer sheet with the same letter matches its worksheet exactly.

Good to know

  • About 3,000 Black Loyalists came to Nova Scotia in the 1780s. Their town, Birchtown, was then one of the largest free Black communities in North America.
  • Maple sugar, canoes and snowshoes all came to settlers from First Nations knowledge.
