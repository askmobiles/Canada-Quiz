Nova Scotia's coat of arms is older than Canada by more than two centuries. King Charles I granted it in 1625 — the oldest coat of arms of any province or territory. Confederation was 242 years later.

Where Nova Scotia is

Nova Scotia is a peninsula with an island attached. The mainland hangs off New Brunswick by a narrow neck of land, and Cape Breton Island sits at the northeast end. The Atlantic Ocean is on one side, the Bay of Fundy on the other, and the Gulf of St. Lawrence to the north.

Because of that shape, nowhere in the province is far from salt water. The capital, Halifax, sits on one of the world's large natural harbours — the federal Discover Canada guide calls it Canada's largest east coast port, deep-water and ice-free.

What the land is like

The province is rolling upland and worn-down rock, with a lot of small lakes and a deeply indented coast. Cape Breton's highlands are the most dramatic part, rising straight out of the sea.

Along the Bay of Fundy side, the same tides that make New Brunswick famous do their work here too, and the marshland behind the dykes of the Annapolis valley is some of the best farmland in Atlantic Canada.

How big is that, really?

  • Nova Scotia is the second-smallest province, and you could fit it inside Manitoba almost twelve times.
  • Prince Edward Island would fit inside Nova Scotia almost ten times over.

How people make a living

The sea pays a lot of the bills. Lobster, scallops and other fisheries are major exports, and Halifax's port and naval base are both large employers. Nova Scotia also has universities well out of proportion to its population, which brings students and research money in.

Wild blueberries — the official berry since 1996 — are a real export crop, not a souvenir. Film production, shipbuilding and tourism fill in the rest.

How Nova Scotia became a province

This is Mi'kma'ki, the territory of the Mi'kmaq, and it has been for thousands of years. The Mi'kmaq are still here, and their treaties with the Crown from the 1700s are still legally live — Canadian courts have ruled on them within living memory.

The French built Port-Royal here in 1605 and called the region Acadie. Britain and France fought over it repeatedly; the coat of arms of 1625 belongs to one of the earliest British attempts to claim it. In 1755 the Acadians were deported from here as well, and Mi'kmaq land was taken without treaty in much of the province.

Nova Scotia became one of the four original provinces of Canada on 1 July 1867 — and it is worth knowing that it was not enthusiastic. Many Nova Scotians felt the deal was made over their heads, and an anti-Confederation party swept the province's federal seats in the first election after it joined.

Things most people do not know

  • Nova Scotia has an official dog — the Nova Scotia duck tolling retriever, declared in 1995 — and an official horse, the Sable Island horse, declared in 2008. The horses run wild on a sandbar island out in the Atlantic.
  • Its coat of arms, granted in 1625, is the oldest of any province or territory in Canada.
  • The official gemstone is agate and the official mineral is stilbite, both declared in 1999. Most provinces have one or the other; Nova Scotia has both.
  • The provincial flower, the mayflower, was adopted in 1901 — one of the earliest official flowers in Canada, chosen before most provinces had thought of the idea.
  • Nova Scotia is nearly surrounded by water but is not an island: it hangs on to New Brunswick by a narrow strip of land.
  • The name is Latin for “New Scotland”, which is why the coat of arms carries a Scottish saltire.

A treaty from the 1700s that still decides cases

The Mi'kmaq signed a series of treaties with the British Crown in the eighteenth century. Unlike the numbered treaties on the Prairies, these were not land surrenders — they were peace and friendship treaties, and they include rights to hunt, fish and trade.

Because section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises existing treaty rights, those eighteenth-century documents are current law. That is why a fishing dispute in Nova Scotia can turn on the wording of a treaty signed in the 1760s, and why the province's history is not only history.

Why a small province has so many universities

Nova Scotia's universities were founded by different religious and language communities in the 1800s, each wanting its own college rather than sending students to somebody else's. The result is a province of about 1.1 million people with more degree-granting institutions than places several times its size.

That has an economic effect worth noticing in a school project. Students arriving from elsewhere are, in accounting terms, an export — the province sells education the way it sells lobster.
