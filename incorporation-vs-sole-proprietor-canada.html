Should a Small Business Incorporate?

The honest version: what incorporating genuinely gives you, what it does not, and the four things that quietly reduce the benefit.

What a sole proprietorship actually is

If you are working for yourself and you have not incorporated, you are a sole proprietor. The CRA's description is short: you pay personal income tax on the net income your business generates. It goes on your ordinary personal return with a business statement attached. There is no second return.

The catch is in the same CRA page: you assume all the risks of the business, and those risks extend to your personal property and assets. There is no legal wall between you and the business, because in law there is no separate business at all.

The real advantages, stated plainly

Limited liability is the big one, and it is genuine. Corporations Canada puts it this way: shareholders are not responsible for a corporation's debts, and if the corporation goes bankrupt, shareholders only lose up to what they invested.

The second is that the corporation is a separate legal person. It can own property, sign contracts and borrow in its own name. And it keeps existing independently of its owners — Corporations Canada notes that with other structures the business stops existing when the owner dies.

The third is the small business rate. After the small business deduction, the federal rate on the first 500,000 dollars of active business income for a Canadian-controlled private corporation is nine percent, against a general federal corporate rate of fifteen percent. Provincial corporate tax is added on top of both.

The point almost everyone gets wrong about the tax

Nine percent against a personal rate that can approach fifty looks like an enormous saving. It is not a saving. It is a deferral, and that is the federal government's own framing rather than ours.

The Department of Finance described it this way: the owner will still have to pay personal taxes when the money is distributed, and what the strategy provides is a significant tax deferral advantage. The money is taxed lightly while it stays in the company and taxed again in your hands when you take it out.

The advantage is real, but it is about timing and about what those retained dollars can earn in the meantime. If you need to take everything out to live on each year, the deferral has very little to work with.

Four things that quietly shrink the benefit

These are the rules that make the arithmetic less impressive than a nine percent headline suggests:

  • Passive investment income. Once a corporation's adjusted aggregate investment income passes 50,000 dollars in a year, the 500,000 dollar business limit falls by five dollars for every extra dollar of that income, and reaches nil at 150,000 dollars.
  • Tax on split income. The rules on income sprinkling target owners who divert income to family members with lower tax rates. Income caught by them is taxed at the top federal rate, with ordinary personal credits unable to shelter it. There are exclusions, including for family members who work in the business on a regular, continuous and substantial basis.
  • Losses stop being yours. The CRA states it directly: the shareholders cannot claim any loss the corporation incurs. A sole proprietor claims losses on their personal return, which is worth real money in early years when a business loses money and the owner has other income.
  • No sales tax advantage at all. The small supplier threshold is 30,000 dollars over four consecutive calendar quarters, and it works the same way for a sole proprietor and a corporation. Incorporating does not reset it or raise it.

Limited liability has holes, and you should know where

A corporation protects shareholders from the company's ordinary debts. It does not make a director untouchable, and the exceptions are the ones a small business is most likely to hit.

Directors are personally liable for payroll source deductions the company failed to remit, plus a penalty of ten or twenty percent and interest, and for unremitted sales tax plus penalties and interest. Under the federal act they are also jointly liable for up to six months of unpaid employee wages.

There is a due diligence defence — a director who exercised the care, diligence and skill a reasonably prudent person would have exercised is not liable — and a two-year limitation running from when you last ceased to be a director. But the plain reading is that the money you withhold from an employee's pay is never really shielded.

What it costs you every year, forever

Incorporating is not a one-off decision, it is a permanent set of obligations. There is the incorporation fee, then registration in each province where you operate, then an annual return to the corporate registry every year, then a corporate tax return every year whether or not the company earned anything, then bookkeeping that has to be kept genuinely separate from your own money.

None of that is difficult. All of it is a standing cost in money and attention that a sole proprietor does not carry. That is the honest comparison, and it is the part that gets left out of the enthusiastic version.

So who is it actually for?

We are not going to tell you whether to incorporate, because that depends on numbers we cannot see and it is a question for an accountant who can see them. What we can do is say which facts decide it.

The deferral is worth most when the business earns more than you need to live on, so profit can stay in the company. Limited liability matters most when the business could genuinely be sued or could owe money it cannot pay. And the loss point cuts the other way: a business expected to lose money for its first year or two, run by somebody with other income, is often better off unincorporated until it turns.

Take those three sentences to an accountant with your actual numbers. That conversation costs a fraction of what getting the decision wrong costs.

Where these facts come from

The corporate rates, the small business deduction, the passive income rules, the split income rules, the sole proprietorship treatment and the sales tax threshold all come from the Canada Revenue Agency. The deferral framing and the passive income threshold come from the Department of Finance. Limited liability, perpetual existence and director liability come from Corporations Canada, the CRA's directors' liability circular and the Canada Business Corporations Act.

Two claims often made for incorporation are missing here on purpose. We do not say shares are freely transferable, because most small corporations restrict transfers in their own articles. And we do not discuss personal guarantees, which lenders and landlords commonly require, because no government source we could find describes their effect.

About this page

A sole proprietor pays personal income tax on business income and assumes all the risk personally. A corporation is a separate legal person with limited liability and a 9 percent federal small business rate on the first $500,000 of active business income.

The corporate tax advantage is a deferral rather than a saving — the Department of Finance's own framing — and it is reduced by passive investment income above $50,000, by the tax on split income, by the fact losses cannot offset personal income, and by director liability for payroll and sales tax remittances.

Common questions

Does incorporating save tax?

It defers tax rather than saving it. The Department of Finance describes the benefit as a tax deferral advantage: income is taxed lightly inside the company and taxed again personally when you take it out.

What is the small business tax rate in Canada?

The federal rate after the small business deduction is 9 percent on the first $500,000 of active business income for a Canadian-controlled private corporation, against a 15 percent general federal rate. Provincial corporate tax is added on top.

Can I claim business losses if I incorporate?

Not on your personal return. The CRA states that shareholders cannot claim any loss the corporation incurs. A sole proprietor claims losses personally, which matters in early loss-making years.

Does limited liability protect me completely?

No. Directors remain personally liable for unremitted payroll source deductions and sales tax, plus penalties and interest, and under the federal act for up to six months of unpaid employee wages. A due diligence defence exists.

Does incorporating change the GST/HST registration threshold?

No. The small supplier threshold is $30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters and works the same way for sole proprietors and corporations.
