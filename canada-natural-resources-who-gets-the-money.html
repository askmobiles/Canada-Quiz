🗻 Canada's natural resources — who owns them, and who gets the money

Canada sits on some of the largest reserves of oil, potash and uranium on earth. Resource industries are 16 percent of the economy. And output per person has fallen further behind the United States every year for a decade. All three of those are true at once, and this page is about why.

Last checked 19 September 2026 — several figures here are forecasts and will move

The short answer

The provinces own the resources, not Ottawa. The provinces charge royalties and set the rates. Companies — mostly Canadian-headquartered, mostly foreign-owned by shareholding — dig, pump and sell. Governments take their share through royalties first and corporate tax second, and the whole of it comes to about 33.7 billion dollars a year across every government in the country.

As to why the economy is not bigger: the resource sector is 16 percent of it. The other 84 percent is where the problem lives. Canada's output per hour worked was 88 percent of the American level in 1984 and 71 percent by 2022. That gap is not caused by the mines and the oil sands, and it would not close if every one of them doubled.

The fact that surprised us most. Canada kept pace with American growth in output per person from 1997 to 2015 — 1.53 percent a year against 1.52. The gap opened after 2015, which is after the oil sands boom, not during it. Any story that blames Canada's economy on its resources has to explain that timing, and none of the ones we found does.

The water number you have heard is the wrong one. Canada holds about 20 percent of the world's fresh water as a stock — but much of that is locked in glaciers and ancient lakes and is not renewed. The figure that matters for actually using water is Canada's share of the world's annually renewable supply, which is nearly 7 percent. Both come from the same Environment and Climate Change Canada page. The 20 percent is the one that circulates; the 7 percent is the one that means something.

On oil reserves, Canada's own government publishes two different numbers. Natural Resources Canada's crude oil overview says 163 billion barrels and fourth-largest. Its oil resources page says 171 billion and third-largest — but that page's figures are explicitly from the end of 2014. The Canada Energy Regulator says 11 percent of world reserves and third. We give all three rather than quietly choosing one.

Who owns it, in law

This is the part almost everyone gets wrong. Ottawa does not own Canada's oil, gas or minerals. The provinces do, and they have since Confederation. The words are in section 109 of the Constitution Act, 1867:

“All Lands, Mines, Minerals, and Royalties belonging to the several Provinces … shall belong to the several Provinces … in which the same are situate or arise, subject to any Trusts existing in respect thereof, and to any Interest other than that of the Province in the same.” — Constitution Act, 1867, section 109

Read the last clause again. “Any Interest other than that of the Province” is the constitutional door through which Indigenous rights enter. Provincial ownership was never absolute, and that phrase is why.

Four things that complicate it

  • The Prairie provinces got theirs late. Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba did not own their resources when they were created. Ottawa kept them and handed them over only through the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements, confirmed by the Constitution Act, 1930 — sixty-three years after Confederation, and twenty-five years after Alberta and Saskatchewan became provinces.
  • Section 92A was added in 1982. After the National Energy Program fight, provinces were given the express power to legislate on resource development and the rate of production, and — unusually — the power to levy indirect taxes on production, which they had not had. They may not price or supply differently for other parts of Canada, and where a federal law conflicts, the federal law wins.
  • Offshore is Canada's, and Newfoundland is paid as though it were not. In 1984 the Supreme Court held that Canada, not Newfoundland, owns continental shelf resources and legislates over them. The Atlantic Accords that followed are political settlements, not a reversal: Finance Canada's own words are that the Accord allows the province to tax these resources as if it were the owner. Newfoundland and Labrador draws about 19 percent of its revenue from a resource it does not legally own.
  • The territories are still finishing the transfer. The Northwest Territories took control of its lands and resources on 1 April 2014. Nunavut signed its devolution agreement on 18 January 2024, with the actual transfer set for at least three years later. Under both, Indigenous governments receive a share of the royalties, and the territory's share of what it keeps is capped — money above the cap is deducted from its federal transfer.

Indigenous rights are not a footnote to this

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognises existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. From it comes the duty to consult, set out by the Supreme Court in Haida Nation in 2004: the Crown must consult when it knows, or ought to know, of a potential right and is considering conduct that might affect it — and that duty applies even where title has never been proven in court. How much consultation is required slides with how strong the claim is and how serious the impact would be, from simple notice at one end to, in some cases, securing consent at the other. It is not a veto in Canadian law. It is also not optional, and the Crown cannot hand it off: a company or a regulator can carry out steps, but the responsibility stays with the Crown.

How the money is actually collected

“Royalties” sounds like one thing. It is not. Every province designs its own, and the design decides how much the public gets and when.

The oil sands: 1 percent first, 25 to 40 percent later

Alberta charges oil sands royalties project by project, and the rate depends on whether the project has reached what the province calls payout — the point at which the project's cumulative revenue first equals its cumulative costs.

StageWhat Alberta takes
Before payout1 percent of gross revenue when West Texas Intermediate is at or below 55 Canadian dollars a barrel, rising along a sliding scale to 9 percent at 120 dollars or more.
After payoutThe greater of the gross rate above, or a net royalty of 25 percent at 55 dollars or below, rising to 40 percent at 120 dollars or more.

That single design choice is most of the public argument about Alberta's “fair share”. A multi-billion-dollar oil sands project can spend years in the pre-payout phase, during which the province may collect as little as one percent of gross revenue while the operator recovers its entire construction cost. Only once the whole capital cost is back does the 25-to-40-percent rate engage. Critics call that letting the company go first. Industry calls it the only way to attract capital into a resource that costs billions up front and pays back over decades. Both descriptions are of the same rule.

Conventional wells: paid on what the well should have cost

For ordinary oil and gas wells Alberta uses its Modernized Royalty Framework, which works on a number the province calls C-star — a cost allowance calculated from the well's depth, lateral length and proppant tonnage. It is a formula, not a receipt. Until the well's revenue reaches C-star the royalty is a flat 5 percent; after that it becomes price-sensitive and runs from 5 to 40 percent. Because C-star is an estimate of what the well ought to have cost, a company that drills more cheaply keeps the difference and one that overspends absorbs it. That is deliberate: it pays for efficiency rather than reimbursing whatever was spent.

Saskatchewan potash: three charges, not one

Saskatchewan charges a 3 percent Crown royalty on Crown-owned potash, then a Potash Production Tax in two parts: a base payment set at 35 percent of resource profits but floored at 11.00 dollars and capped at 12.33 dollars per tonne of K2O sold, and a profit tax of 15 percent on profit per tonne up to a threshold and 35 percent above it. Against all of that run allowances — including depreciation that recognises 120 percent of what a company actually spent on capital. That last provision is the one most often criticised, because it lets a company deduct more than it invested.

Then corporate tax, on what is left

Royalties are an expense. Companies deduct them, then pay federal and provincial corporate income tax on the profit that remains. That is the federal government's main share of provincially owned resources — it collects no royalty on them at all. Ottawa's own accounting shows the sector's specific tax deductions reduced federal revenue by about 1.9 billion dollars a year on average between 2015 and 2019, and 1.8 billion in 2021, according to the Parliamentary Budget Officer.

How much governments actually collect

Across every government in Canada, the natural resources sector produced an average of 33.7 billion dollars a year between 2019 and 2023, on Natural Resources Canada's own count. The same source puts the sector at 16.0 percent of national output — 459 billion dollars in 2024 — and at 383 billion dollars of exports, which is 53 percent of everything Canada sells abroad.

What nobody publishes. How that 33.7 billion splits between Ottawa and the provinces is not stated in the source, and we could not find it published anywhere else. It is the single largest hole in the public picture of who gets the money, and it is a strange thing not to know.

The provinces depend on it to very different degrees, and the gap between them is far wider than most people assume.

Alberta's 23.0 billion breaks down as 18.0 billion from bitumen royalties, 3.1 billion from crude oil, 1.3 billion from natural gas and by-products, 390 million from Crown lease sales and 143 million from rentals, fees and coal. The province's own first-quarter update raised that resource forecast by 9.7 billion dollars from the February budget — on a change in the oil price assumption alone, from 60.50 to 73.50 US dollars a barrel.

That nine-billion-dollar swing is the real lesson. One assumption about a price set in New York moved a provincial budget by more than the entire yearly resource revenue of Saskatchewan. Resource money is not income. It is a bet on a price nobody in Canada controls.

Who owns the companies

Statistics Canada measures foreign control of assets, and for 2023 it reports 33.0 percent of oil and gas extraction assets and 31.9 percent of mining and quarrying assets under foreign control. Across the whole economy the figure is 14.4 percent, and more than half of all foreign-controlled assets in Canada are American. Statistics Canada also notes that the foreign-controlled share has been falling steadily since 2007.

For the oil sands specifically you will see two numbers that appear to contradict each other. They do not. They measure different things, and an article that gives only one of them misleads.

So why is the economy not bigger?

This is the question that prompted the page, and the honest answer has five parts, only one of which is about resources at all.

1. Resources are a sixth of the economy, not the economy

Sixteen percent of output and 53 percent of exports is a lot — but five out of every six dollars of Canadian output come from something else. A country cannot be made rich or poor per person by a sixth of itself. Whatever has gone wrong has gone wrong across the whole of it.

2. The productivity gap — and when it opened

In March 2024 the Senior Deputy Governor of the Bank of Canada, Carolyn Rogers, gave a speech titled “Time to break the glass”. Her central number: in 1984 the Canadian economy produced 88 percent of the value an American hour of work produced; by 2022 it produced 71 percent. She pointed at fifty years of Canadian firms investing less per worker than American ones, and at weak competition at home.

Statistics Canada's own comparison puts dates on it, and the dates are the surprise.

For eighteen years Canada matched the United States almost exactly. The gap is a post-2015 event. By the third quarter of 2025 Canada's output per person stood 14 percent below where it was, relative to the United States, in early 1997, and relative productivity had fallen 26 percent since the late 1990s.

This is why the resource-curse story does not fit. The oil sands boom ran through the 2000s and early 2010s — exactly the years when Canada was keeping pace. The falling behind started afterwards. Whatever is wrong, the timing says it is not the oil sands.

3. Part of the recent gap is arithmetic

Output per person is a fraction, and Canada has been growing the bottom of it fast. In 2023 the American economy grew 3.3 percent while Canada's grew 1.1 percent — but the American population grew 0.5 percent while Canada's grew 3.2 percent. Total Canadian output kept rising. Output per person did not, because it was being divided among many more people. That is not a judgement about immigration; it is what the arithmetic does, and it explains part of the last few years rather than the whole decade.

One more measure is worth knowing. Gross national income counts money that actually accrues to people living in Canada, rather than money produced inside Canada. On that measure the gap with the United States is 10 percent rather than 14. That is evidence against the strongest form of the “foreign owners take it all” argument — not a refutation of it, but it points the other way.

4. Canada sells the rock, and buys back the thing made from it

In 2020 Alberta's upgraders processed 42 percent of the 3.0 million barrels a day of bitumen produced. The other 58 percent left the country as raw or diluted bitumen. Canada produces roughly 5 million barrels a day of crude and refines about 1.6 million. Refining capacity has been essentially unchanged since 2000 — 1.9 million barrels a day across 16 refineries, down from 20 — while production roughly doubled. In 2025 Canada was a net importer of about 130,000 barrels a day of refined products.

The reason is not a conspiracy, and the industry's own explanation is the mundane one: upgrader construction costs rose sharply, and the price gap between heavy and light crude — which is what made upgrading profitable — narrowed. Eastern refineries are built for light crude and cannot easily take western heavy oil, which is why the country exports heavy crude and imports light at the same time. It is still a real loss of value added, and it is still the answer to “why don't we make more from it”.

5. Dutch disease — a real argument, and it is older than people think

The theory is that a resource boom pushes the currency up, which makes factories uncompetitive. Canada has had this argument properly, with numbers on both sides.

  • For. Beine, Bos and Coulombe, in Resource and Energy Economics in 2012, attributed between 33 and 39 percent of manufacturing job losses from 2002 to 2007 to Dutch disease. Their methodological point matters: of the 48 percent currency move they studied, 42 percent was the Canadian dollar rising and 58 percent was the American dollar falling.
  • Against. Mark Carney, then Governor of the Bank of Canada, in Calgary in September 2012: higher commodity prices are “unambiguously good” for Canada, and the Dutch disease diagnosis is “overly simplistic and, in the end, wrong”. He put about 40 percent of the currency's rise down to American dollar weakness and described manufacturing decline as a broad trend across the whole advanced world, driven by globalisation and technology.

The two sides are closer than the shouting suggests: both agree much of the currency move was American weakness, and both agree the exchange rate explains part of the manufacturing decline. They differ on how much. Note also that both are now well over a decade old and describe the years before 2015 — which are precisely the years when Canada was keeping pace. Neither explains the period that actually needs explaining, and we could not find recent work that does.

What was saved, and what was not

Two countries turned oil into a national savings fund. The sizes are not close.

Alberta Heritage Savings Trust Fund31.9 billion Canadian dollars31 December 2025

At the Bank of Canada's rate in mid-September 2026 the Norwegian fund is worth roughly 3.4 trillion Canadian dollars — on the order of a hundred times Alberta's. Norway's own figures add something worth knowing: more than half of that value is accumulated investment return rather than deposits. Money left alone for thirty years does that. Alberta's fund received a 2.8 billion dollar deposit for 2025-26 and returned 7.7 percent in the fiscal year to date.

We are not going to tell you why they diverged. The comparison is real and the two figures are from each fund's own page. The explanation — deposit rules, withdrawals for general spending, inflation protection, political decisions taken over fifty years — is genuinely contested, and we have not done the work to set it out fairly. Treat anyone who gives you a one-sentence reason with suspicion, including us.

So who benefits more?

Taking only what can be sourced: governments collect about 33.7 billion dollars a year. The sector supports 1.8 million jobs, 958,000 of them direct, and 421 communities are significantly or highly reliant on it. Companies keep the profit after royalties and tax, and a majority of the shares in those companies are held outside Canada, so a majority of the dividends leave. Whether that is a fair division is a political question and we are not going to answer it for you — but the ownership rules, the royalty formulas and the timing of payout are where the answer actually lives, and they are all written down and all changeable by a provincial legislature.

What is not known

  • How the 33.7 billion dollars a year splits between the federal government and the provinces. Not published in the source, and not found elsewhere.
  • Alberta's audited 2024-25 resource revenue. The province's final year-end report is on a site this research could not reach, so every Alberta figure here is a forecast and is labelled as one.
  • Why Canada's output per person began falling behind the United States around 2015. The measurements are solid; the explanation is not settled, and the well-known Dutch disease work predates the period entirely.
  • Where resource profits ultimately go, beyond the ownership shares above. No independent recent accounting was located.
