That is not a coincidence — it is the test we run before publishing this page, because a comparison table whose columns do not add up is comparing figures from different dates.

Six things the table shows at a glance

  • Size and population have almost nothing to do with each other. Nunavut is the biggest and has the fewest people. Prince Edward Island is the smallest and has more people than all three territories put together.
  • Ontario and Quebec hold about three in every five Canadians on about a quarter of the land.
  • The three territories are nearly 40% of Canada and about a third of one percent of its people.
  • Four provinces joined on the same day — Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick, on 1 July 1867. Alberta and Saskatchewan also share a date, 1 September 1905, and so do Manitoba and the Northwest Territories, 15 July 1870.
  • Yukon has more people than the Northwest Territories, even though the Northwest Territories is nearly three times as big.
  • Quebec is the largest province but only the second-largest place. Nunavut is bigger. A territory is not a small province — the difference is how it is governed, not how big it is.

How to use this for a school project

Pick your province and open its page. Copy the fact box into your notes first — that is the part a marker looks for. Then read “what the land is like” and “how it became a province”, which is where the sentences you write in your own words come from.

If you are comparing two, use the table above rather than taking a number from each page separately. The table's figures are all from the same date and the same source, which is what makes a comparison mean anything.

Every page lists its sources at the bottom, with a link to each one. A teacher who asks where you got a figure can follow the same link you did.

You may also want our map of Canada, or the page on how provinces and territories differ.
