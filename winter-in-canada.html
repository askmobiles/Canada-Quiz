❄️ Winter in Canada: What to Expect and How to Enjoy It

A practical guide to your first Canadian winter — how cold it really gets, how to dress, driving in snow, and fun winter activities for families.

For many newcomers, winter is the biggest surprise about life in Canada. It is long, it is real, and no photograph prepares you for the first time you step outside at minus 25. The good news is that Canadians have had a few hundred years to figure this out, and once you know the tricks, winter becomes something you can actually enjoy.

How cold does it really get?

It depends enormously on where you live. Vancouver and Victoria on the west coast are mild and rainy, often staying above freezing all winter. Toronto and Montreal get real snow and temperatures that regularly sit between minus 5 and minus 15. The Prairies — Winnipeg, Regina, Saskatoon, Edmonton — get the coldest weather, sometimes below minus 30. In the territories it gets colder still, and there are only a few hours of daylight in December.

You will also hear about the wind chill. This is how cold the air feels on your skin once wind is added. If the temperature is minus 15 but the wind chill is minus 28, dress for minus 28.

How to dress: layers, not one big coat

The single most useful thing to learn is layering. Three thinner layers keep you warmer than one thick one, because the trapped air between them does the work — and you can remove a layer indoors instead of overheating.

  • Base layer — long underwear in wool or synthetic fabric. Avoid cotton next to your skin, because it holds sweat and makes you cold.
  • Middle layer — a fleece or wool sweater for warmth.
  • Outer layer — an insulated, windproof and waterproof winter coat, ideally covering your hips.
  • Boots — waterproof, insulated, with deep grippy soles. This is the item worth spending real money on.
  • Hat, mittens, scarf or neck warmer — you lose a lot of heat from your head, and mittens are warmer than gloves.
  • Wool socks — one good pair beats two cotton pairs.

Getting around safely

If you drive, winter tires are not optional in most of the country — they are legally required in Quebec, and required on many British Columbia highways in winter. They stay soft and grippy in cold weather in a way that all-season tires do not. Leave extra distance, brake gently and early, and keep a snow brush, scraper, blanket and booster cables in the car.

Walking has its own hazards. Black ice is the invisible kind and it is the one that hurts. Take short, flat steps, keep your hands out of your pockets for balance, and use the handrail on outdoor stairs.

Life at home

  • Winter air indoors is very dry — a humidifier helps with dry skin, nosebleeds and sleep.
  • Keep the heat on even when you travel, so pipes do not freeze and burst.
  • Check whether you or your landlord is responsible for shovelling the sidewalk. Many cities fine homeowners who do not clear it in time.
  • Learn your city's snow-clearing and parking-ban rules, or your car may get ticketed or towed.

The fun part

Canadians survive winter by going outside in it. Skating on outdoor rinks and frozen canals, tobogganing, cross-country skiing, snowshoeing, building snow forts, ice fishing, winter festivals with ice sculptures, and the simple pleasure of hot chocolate afterwards. Many cities run free or cheap programs to help families try these for the first time.

And there is one more benefit: on a clear, cold night away from city lights, the northern sky can put on a show that no summer evening can match.
