🏠 Ontario property tax and land transfer tax, explained

Two taxes come with owning a home in Ontario: land transfer tax, paid once when you buy, and property tax, paid every year after that. Here is how each one is worked out, what the rate is in your city and why it differs, with calculators for both and a comparison with the rest of Canada.

Last checked 12 October 2026

Jump to: Land transfer tax · LTT calculator · Property tax · Rates by city · Why rates differ · Property tax calculator · Other provinces

The short version. Land transfer tax is a one-time tax on buying property, set by the province, and in Toronto the city charges a second one on top. On a $700,000 home it is $10,475 in most of Ontario and $20,950 in Toronto, before any first-time buyer refund. Property tax is yearly: your home's assessed value times your city's rate. Rates run from about 0.72% in Markham to more than 2% in Windsor, Timmins and Chatham-Kent, mostly because home values differ, not because some cities spend far more.

Land transfer tax: paid once, when you buy

When you buy land or a home in Ontario, you pay the province a land transfer tax on the price. It works like income tax brackets: each slice of the price is taxed at its own rate, so only the part above each threshold pays the higher rate.

Toronto charges it twice

Toronto is the only Ontario city allowed to charge its own municipal land transfer tax, on top of the provincial one. Up to $2 million it uses the same brackets, so a Toronto buyer pays roughly double. From 1 April 2026 its rates above $3 million climb steeply:

$2 million to $3 million2.5%
$3 million to $4 million4.40%
$4 million to $5 million5.45%
$5 million to $10 million6.50%
$10 million to $20 million7.55%
Over $20 million8.60%

First-time buyers get money back

  • Ontario refunds up to $4,000, which covers the whole tax on a home up to $368,333.
  • Toronto rebates up to $4,475 of its own tax.
  • To qualify you must be at least 18, a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, never have owned a home anywhere in the world, and move in within nine months. If you have a spouse, they must not have owned a home anywhere in the world while they were your spouse; if they did, neither of you gets the refund.
  • Most buyers who are not Canadian citizens or permanent residents also pay Ontario's 25% Non-Resident Speculation Tax on residential property. There are exemptions, for example for provincial nominees, protected persons and some spouses of citizens. In Toronto, foreign buyers also pay the city's 10% Municipal Non-Resident Speculation Tax, in force since 1 January 2025.

Property tax: paid every year

Property tax pays for the city: roads, police, fire, transit, parks, libraries, garbage and more. The bill is simple arithmetic: your home's assessed value × your city's tax rate. A home assessed at $500,000 in a city with a 1% rate pays $5,000 a year.

  • The assessed value comes from MPAC, the Municipal Property Assessment Corporation, not from what you paid. Province-wide reassessment has been postponed since 2020, so 2026 bills are still based on what homes were worth on 1 January 2016. Your own assessment can still change if the property changes, for example after an addition, a new garage or a new home on the lot.
  • The rate has two or three parts: the city's own rate, the region's rate if you live in a two-tier area like Peel or York, and the province's education rate, which is the same 0.153% everywhere in Ontario.
  • In Toronto in 2026 those parts are 0.605295% for the city, 0.009016% for the City Building Fund and 0.153% for education: 0.767311% in all.

Why every city sets a different rate

Each council first decides how much money it needs for the year, the levy. The rate is then worked out backwards: the money needed ÷ the total assessed value of all the property in the city. That one division explains most of the differences.

  • Expensive homes mean a low rate. Markham, Vaughan and Toronto have very high property values, so a small rate raises a lot of money. Windsor, Sudbury and Thunder Bay have lower home values, so they need a higher rate to pay for the same kinds of services.
  • A low rate is not a low bill. A $1 million Toronto assessment at 0.767% costs $7,673 a year; a $400,000 Windsor assessment at 2.097% costs $8,386.
  • Businesses carry part of the load. Councils set higher rates for commercial and industrial property. A city with lots of offices, factories and malls can keep the homeowner rate lower.
  • Services and size differ. A city that runs its own transit system, police service and long-term care, or covers a huge area of roads, spends more per home than a compact suburb.
  • Two councils, one bill. In Peel, York, Durham, Halton, Niagara and Waterloo, the region's share is often bigger than the city's. In Mississauga's 2026 budget, about two-thirds of the tax increase came from Peel Region.
  • Old values. Because assessments are still based on 2016 values, cities whose prices rose fastest since then have rates that look low compared with today's prices.

How the other provinces and territories do it

Every province lets municipalities charge property tax, but transfer taxes vary far more. Three provinces and all three territories charge only a land titles registration fee.

British ColumbiaProperty transfer tax: 1% on the first $200,000, 2% to $2 million, 3% above, plus a further 2% on residential value over $3 million. First-time buyers pay nothing up to $500,000 and save $8,000 up to $835,000; the saving ends at $860,000.
AlbertaNo land transfer tax. A land titles fee of $50 plus $5 for every $5,000 of value: about $550 on a $500,000 home.
SaskatchewanNo land transfer tax. A title transfer fee of roughly 0.4% of the value.
ManitobaLand transfer tax: nothing on the first $30,000, then 0.5% to $90,000, 1% to $150,000, 1.5% to $200,000 and 2% above.
QuebecThe “welcome tax”, collected by the municipality: 0.5% to $62,900, 1% to $315,000 and 1.5% above (2026 amounts). Cities may add higher rates above $500,000; Quebec City charges 2.5% to $750,000 and 3% above.
New BrunswickA flat 1% real property transfer tax.
Nova ScotiaA municipal deed transfer tax of up to 1.5%, set by each municipality; Halifax charges the full 1.5%. Since 1 April 2025 most non-resident buyers pay an extra 10%.
Prince Edward IslandA flat 1% real property transfer tax, on the greater of the price or the assessed value. Qualifying first-time buyers are exempt.
Newfoundland and LabradorNo land transfer tax. A deed registration fee of about $100 plus 0.4% of the value over $500.
Yukon, Northwest Territories, NunavutNo land transfer tax. Land titles registration fees only, usually a few hundred dollars on an ordinary home.

Property tax rates in other big cities

Rough 2026 residential rates from nesto's national guide: Vancouver about 0.3%, Calgary 0.66%, Edmonton 1.01%, Saskatoon 1.34%, Regina 1.55%, Winnipeg 1.38%, Montreal 0.9%, Quebec City 0.85%, Halifax 0.61%, Moncton 1.36%, Saint John 2.12% and St. John's 0.91%. Treat these as a rough guide only: each province assesses homes differently. British Columbia reassesses every year at market value, Manitoba taxes only 45% of a home's value and Saskatchewan 80%, and Ontario still uses 2016 values, so the same percentage does not mean the same bill.

Common questions

Why did my property tax go up when my assessment did not change?

Because the rate changed. Each year your council sets a budget, then the tax rate needed to raise it. When the budget grows, the rate goes up, and in a two-tier area the region's share can rise too. Your 2016-based assessed value can stay the same for years while the bill still grows.

Does buying a house change its assessed value?

No. MPAC does not reset the assessment to the price you paid. It stays at the 2016-based value until the province holds a new province-wide reassessment, unless the property itself changes, for example with an addition or a new building.

Is land transfer tax based on the price or on the assessment?

On the price you pay for the home, not on MPAC's assessed value.
