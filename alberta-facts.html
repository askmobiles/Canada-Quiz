Alberta and Saskatchewan have the same birthday. Both became provinces on 1 September 1905, by two separate Acts of Parliament passed together. Before that day, both were part of the North-West Territories. That is why neither province is usually given a number the way Manitoba is called the fifth: they arrived at the same moment.

Where Alberta is

Alberta is the westernmost Prairie province. British Columbia is to the west, Saskatchewan to the east, the Northwest Territories to the north, and the American state of Montana along the south. It has no ocean coast at all.

Its western border is not a straight line like Saskatchewan's. It follows the mountains — specifically the line where water starts running west instead of east.

What the land is like

Alberta is three landscapes stacked from south to north. The south is dry open prairie and badlands. The middle is parkland, with farms and poplar bluffs. The north is boreal forest, muskeg and rivers, and it is by far the biggest of the three even though almost nobody thinks of it first.

Along the western edge, the Rocky Mountains rise out of the foothills. The badlands near Drumheller are the opposite kind of landscape: not built up, but cut down, carved by water into the layers of rock that hold the fossils.

How big is that, really?

  • Alberta and Saskatchewan side by side are bigger than Ontario — 1,312,884 km² against 1,076,395 km².
  • Alberta on its own is bigger than all four Atlantic provinces put together, with over 120,000 km² to spare.
  • Only about 3% of Alberta is fresh water — very nearly the same amount as British Columbia has, which is a coincidence worth noticing given how different the two provinces look.

How people make a living

Oil and gas are the part everyone knows, and they are genuinely large: the oil sands in the northeast are one of the biggest industrial projects on Earth. But Alberta is also a major farming and cattle province, and Calgary and Edmonton between them hold a great deal of engineering, finance, health care and university work.

Tourism is real money here too. The mountain national parks bring visitors from all over the world, and the dinosaur country near Drumheller brings a different crowd entirely.

How Alberta became a province

Long before any of this, the plains here belonged to the Blackfoot Confederacy — Siksika, Kainai and Piikani — along with the Tsuut'ina, the Stoney Nakoda, Cree and Dene nations further north, and Métis communities across the middle. The buffalo were the centre of life on the plains, and when the herds were destroyed in the 1870s and 1880s that way of living was destroyed with them.

Treaties 6, 7 and 8 were signed across what is now Alberta between 1876 and 1899. Then came the railway, and then settlers in very large numbers, and by 1905 there were enough people that the federal government carved two new provinces out of the North-West Territories on the same day.

Things most people do not know

  • Alberta's official bird was chosen by children. The great horned owl won a province-wide children's vote and was adopted in 1977.
  • Alberta runs a rat control programme and keeps a Rat Control Zone along its eastern border. The province says most residents know Alberta is rat-free and that there is a programme to keep it that way — while also saying small infestations are occasionally found and dealt with. So the famous claim is close to true, and the province's own wording is more careful than the version you usually hear.
  • Alberta's clocks still change twice a year. A referendum in October 2021 asked whether to stop, and 50.2% of those who voted chose to keep changing them. The province says the result was binding.
  • The provincial flower is the wild rose, which is also called the prickly rose. It has been Alberta's floral emblem since 1930 — one of the oldest provincial flower choices in the country.
  • Alberta and Saskatchewan are the only two provinces with a straight line for a border between them, and the only two that became provinces on the same day.
  • The capital is Edmonton, but the bigger city is Calgary. Alberta is one of several provinces where those are two different places.

Why the badlands hold so many fossils

Fossils form in layers of sediment, and they stay hidden until something strips the layers back off. In most of Canada the last ice age left a thick blanket of till over everything. Around Drumheller, meltwater rivers cut down through that blanket and deep into the rock beneath, exposing bed after bed of late Cretaceous sediment at the surface.

So Alberta's dinosaur wealth is not really about more dinosaurs having lived there. It is about erosion doing, for free, the work a palaeontologist would otherwise have to do with a shovel.

What a straight border tells you

Alberta's eastern border is a line of longitude. Its western border follows the continental divide. The difference is the whole story of how the two were drawn: one was negotiated with a colony that already existed and had mountains in it, the other was ruled onto a map of land the federal government was administering directly.

It also has a practical consequence that still matters. Because the divide decides which way rivers run, Alberta and British Columbia share almost no rivers along that border — but Alberta and Saskatchewan share a great many, which is why the two provinces have to agree about water.
