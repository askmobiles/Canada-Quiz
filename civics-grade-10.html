🗳️ Grade 10 Civics and Citizenship

A Grade 10 civics lesson on how Canada is governed, your rights and how to make change, with a quiz, endless practice, a challenge round, and printable worksheets with answer sheets. No accounts, nothing to download.

Curriculum: Ontario Civics and Citizenship, Grade 10, Open (CHV2O, revised 2022), the compulsory half-credit course: Strand B (Civic Awareness) and Strand C (Civic Engagement, Service, and Action), with the source-checking skills of Strand A. Other provinces teach much of this between Grades 8 and 10. Unofficial practice, not approved by any school board. Read the official curriculum.

📖 Democracy, government and elections

Canada is a democracy, a federation and a constitutional monarchy, all at once. Who makes which decisions, how a law is made, and how we choose the people who make them.

1867

A federation

The Constitution Act, 1867 split the power to make laws between Ottawa and the provinces. The federal government handles things like defence, citizenship, money and criminal law; the provinces handle things like education, health care and highways.

Today

Three levels, and Indigenous governments

Federal, provincial or territorial, and municipal governments all serve you. Your city or town runs local things such as water, garbage pickup, local roads, parks and fire services. Many First Nations, Inuit and Métis communities also have their own governments.

Ottawa

A constitutional monarchy

The King is Canada's head of state, represented by the Governor General, and by a lieutenant governor in each province. The prime minister is the head of government. The King and his representatives act on the advice of elected leaders.

Ottawa

Three branches

The legislative branch (Parliament: the King, the Senate and the House of Commons) makes laws. The executive branch (the prime minister and cabinet) puts them into action. The judicial branch (the courts) interprets them and settles disputes.

Ottawa

Two houses of Parliament

The House of Commons has 343 members of Parliament, one elected in each riding. The Senate has 105 senators, appointed by the Governor General on the prime minister's advice; they can serve until age 75.

1848

Responsible government

Since Nova Scotia won it in 1848, the government must keep the support, or "confidence", of the elected assembly. If it loses a confidence vote in the House of Commons, it must resign or ask for an election.

Ottawa

How a bill becomes law

Most bills start in the House of Commons. A bill gets three readings, with debate at second and third reading and study in committee in between. It then goes through the same steps in the other house, and becomes law with Royal Assent from the Governor General.

Your vote

Elections

In a federal election, the candidate with the most votes in each riding wins the seat, even without half the votes. This is called first-past-the-post. A party needs 172 of the 343 seats for a majority. Elections Canada, an independent agency, runs the vote.

Today

Taxes and budgets

Governments pay for services with taxes. Income tax is the federal government's biggest source of money, and the GST adds 5% to most purchases. Each year the finance minister presents a budget saying how the money will be raised and spent.

Today

Indigenous governance and treaties

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy governed itself under the Great Law of Peace long before Confederation, with clan mothers choosing the chiefs. Today, section 35 of the Constitution recognizes Aboriginal and treaty rights, and modern treaties such as the Nisga'a Treaty (2000) include self-government.

📖 Rights and responsibilities

The Charter of Rights and Freedoms protects everyone in Canada, but rights have limits and have not always been respected. Rights also come with responsibilities.

1215

Magna Carta

In England in 1215, barons forced King John to accept that even the king had to follow the law. It is one of the roots of the rule of law: no one, not even the government, is above the law.

1948

A Canadian drafts world rights

John Peters Humphrey, a law professor from New Brunswick, wrote the first draft of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the United Nations on December 10, 1948.

1982

The Charter of Rights and Freedoms

The Charter became part of the Constitution on April 17, 1982. Because it is in the Constitution, governments cannot simply pass a law to remove it, and courts can strike down laws that break it.

Section 2

Fundamental freedoms

Everyone has freedom of conscience and religion; of thought, belief, opinion and expression, including the press; of peaceful assembly; and of association.

1985

Equality rights

Section 15 says everyone is equal before the law, without discrimination based on things like race, religion, sex, age or disability. It came into force three years after the rest of the Charter, so governments had time to fix their laws.

Section 33

Rights have limits

Section 1 allows "reasonable limits" on rights that can be justified in a free and democratic society. Section 33, the notwithstanding clause, lets a government override some rights for five years at a time.

1918

Who could vote

Most women won the federal vote in 1918; women in Quebec won the provincial vote only in 1940. Chinese and South Asian Canadians won back the vote in 1947, Japanese Canadians in 1948, Inuit in 1950, and First Nations people without giving up their status in 1960.

1942

When rights were taken away

In 1942, using the War Measures Act, the government forced more than 20,000 Japanese Canadians away from the BC coast, and took their homes and businesses. In 1988 the government apologized and paid compensation to survivors.

Today

Responsibilities

Citizens have responsibilities too: obeying the law, serving on a jury when called, voting in elections, helping others in the community, and protecting Canada's heritage and environment.

📖 Being an active citizen

Voting is only one way to take part. Citizens volunteer, speak up, sign petitions, remember, and check what they read before they share it.

Today

Ways to take part

Vote when you are 18. Before then you can volunteer, join a student council, write to your MP, MPP or councillor, speak at a public meeting, start or sign a petition, or join a peaceful protest.

Today

Petitions to Parliament

Anyone can start an e-petition to the House of Commons with the support of an MP. If it gets at least 500 signatures, it is presented in the House and the government must respond in writing.

1980

Terry Fox

With one leg lost to cancer, Terry Fox ran 5,373 km in 143 days in 1980, from St. John's to near Thunder Bay, to raise money for cancer research. Terry Fox Runs are still held every year.

2008

Shannen Koostachin

At 13, Shannen Koostachin of Attawapiskat First Nation led a youth campaign for a safe, proper school for her community, and took it to Ottawa in 2008. After her death in 2010, her campaign grew into Shannen's Dream, for fair schools for all First Nations children, and a new school opened in Attawapiskat in 2014.

2021

Remembering together

Commemoration is part of citizenship: Remembrance Day on November 11, and the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation on September 30, first held in 2021. Orange Shirt Day, started by residential school survivor Phyllis Webstad in 2013, is held the same day.

Online

Check before you share

Before you believe or share a post, ask: who made it, and why? What is the evidence? Do trustworthy sources say the same thing? Fake photos, edited videos and accounts run from other countries can all try to sway how people vote.

Today

Community involvement

To earn the Ontario Secondary School Diploma, students complete 40 hours of community involvement. Volunteering is also how many people first see how their community works, and where it needs help.

About this quiz

This page covers Grade 10 Civics and Citizenship: how Canada is governed at every level, including Indigenous governments and treaties; how laws are made and elections work; the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, its limits and the times rights were not respected; and the many ways young people can take part, from volunteering to checking what they read online. It follows the 2022 Ontario course and names no sitting politician.

It works six ways. Read the lesson and its timeline, take the ten-question quiz, practise for as long as you like with no score, try the harder challenge round, then print a worksheet and its answer sheet. There are three versions, lettered A, B and C, and the answer sheet with the same letter matches its worksheet exactly.
