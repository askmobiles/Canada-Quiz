Manitoba has two birthdays. The law that created it, the Manitoba Act, was signed on 12 May 1870 — that day is Manitoba Day. But the province only officially joined Canada when the Act was proclaimed on 15 July 1870. Both dates are correct; they answer different questions.

Where Manitoba is

Manitoba sits in the middle of Canada. Saskatchewan is to the west, Ontario to the east, Nunavut to the north, and two American states — North Dakota and Minnesota — are along the southern border.

The surprise: a Prairie province with a seaport. Manitoba's top-right corner touches Hudson Bay, which is salt water joined to the Arctic Ocean. The town of Churchill sits there, and it is Manitoba's only saltwater port — the only deepwater Arctic port in Canada connected to the main railway network. Cargo ships and polar bears in the same place.

What the land is like

Manitoba covers three of Canada's seven big physical regions, and they are nothing alike.

  • The Interior Plains in the south — flat farmland. Most of Manitoba's people and almost all of its farms are here.
  • The Canadian Shield across the north and east — ancient rock, forests and lakes. This is where the mines, the forestry and the hydro dams are.
  • The Hudson Bay Lowland in the northeast — flat, cold, treeless towards the coast, and too cold for farming. Churchill is here.

Manitoba has more than 100,000 lakes. The biggest, Lake Winnipeg, covers about 23,750 km² and is the 10th largest lake in the world by surface area. It is 436 km long but only about 12 metres deep on average — a huge, shallow sheet of water.

The highest point in the whole province is Baldy Mountain, 831 metres. For comparison, Alberta's highest mountain is more than four times that. Manitoba is genuinely flat.

How big is that, really?

647,797 km² is a hard number to picture, so here are two ways to see it.

  • Manitoba is bigger than Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick and Prince Edward Island put together — those four come to 539,064 km².
  • Manitoba is about 1.8 times the size of Germany, and about 2.6 times the size of the United Kingdom.

How people make a living

Manitoba grows and sells food to the world. In the first half of 2026 the province exported $9.2 billion worth of goods, and the top three were all food: wheat ($832 million), pork ($754 million) and canola oil ($629 million).

Two other things worth knowing. More than 96 percent of Manitoba's electricity comes from moving water — rivers flowing north towards Hudson Bay, turning turbines at sixteen generating stations. And Manitoba mines about a fifth of all the nickel in Canada.

How Manitoba became a province

People have lived in what is now Manitoba for more than 11,000 years, arriving as the glaciers pulled back. First Nations — Cree, Ojibway, Oji-Cree, Dene and Dakota — along with Métis and Inuit peoples have been here throughout, and are here now. Manitoba today has 63 First Nations.

The name comes from them. At the narrows of Lake Manitoba, waves hitting the rocks make an unusual sound. It was said to be the Manitou, or Great Spirit — in Ojibway, Manito-bau. The province is named after a sound.

In 1869 the Hudson's Bay Company sold a vast territory to Canada without asking any of the people who lived there. When Canadian surveyors arrived to mark up the land, the Red River Métis stopped them.

Led by Louis Riel, who was 25 years old, they formed their own government — French-speaking and English-speaking Métis together — and wrote a list of rights they wanted guaranteed before joining Canada. Most of that list became the Manitoba Act of 1870. Manitoba is the only province that began this way.

Two things this story is usually told without. Riel's provisional government put an Ontario settler named Thomas Scott on trial and executed him, which turned much of Ontario against the Métis and against Riel himself. And the land the Manitoba Act promised to Métis families was badly mismanaged afterwards, so many of those families left for further west. The resistance won the province; it did not win what it was promised.

This page calls it the Red River Resistance. Older books call it a rebellion. The Manitoba Métis Federation — the Métis government itself — uses “resistance”, because the Métis were defending land they already lived on rather than rising against a government they had joined. Which word gets used is itself a piece of history.

Ottawa officially recognised Louis Riel as the founder of Manitoba in 1992 — 122 years after the province was created, and 107 years after he was hanged for treason.

Seven things most people do not know

  • Polar bears are born in Manitoba. Wapusk National Park holds one of the world's largest known polar bear denning areas. Mothers dig dens in the snow and give birth there.
  • About 55,000 beluga whales spend the summer off Manitoba's coast. It is Canada's largest beluga population. Between 3,000 and 5,000 of them crowd into the Churchill River estuary on a peak day.
  • Winnie-the-Pooh is named after Winnipeg. In 1914 a Canadian soldier, Harry Colebourn, bought a bear cub and named her Winnie after his home city. His diary entry reads: “Bought Bear $20.” He left her at London Zoo, where a boy called Christopher Robin met her.
  • Money for more than 80 countries is made in Winnipeg. The Royal Canadian Mint's Winnipeg plant makes every coin in your pocket, and coins for dozens of other nations too.
  • A 13-metre sea monster was dug out of a Manitoba field. Bruce, a mosasaur that swam here 80 million years ago, holds the Guinness World Record for the largest mosasaur on display. Southern Manitoba used to be a sea.
  • Manitoba's official animals are a polar bear and a bison. An Arctic sea-ice animal and a prairie grassland animal, in the same province. That tells you how much the land changes from south to north.
  • Tens of thousands of snakes wake up together every spring. At the Narcisse Snake Dens, red-sided garter snakes come up from underground in late April or early May. In a mating ball, one female can be surrounded by up to a hundred males.

Language rights: given, taken away, given back

Manitoba has no Official Languages Act. What it has is section 23 of the Manitoba Act, 1870, which guarantees that English and French have equal status in the legislature and the courts. In 1890 Manitoba passed a law making English the province's only official language, and that stood for 95 years. In 1985 the Supreme Court of Canada ruled the English-only approach unconstitutional, and every Manitoba statute has had to be passed in both languages since.

So neither “Manitoba is officially bilingual” nor “Manitoba's only official language is English” is correct. The accurate version: by law, Manitoba's legislature and courts must work in both English and French. That right was written into the law that created the province, removed twenty years later, and restored by the Supreme Court a century after that.

The province that grew

Manitoba was created tiny — a small square around the Red River valley, nicknamed the postage stamp province. Its boundaries were pushed out in 1881 and again in 1912, which is when it finally reached Hudson Bay. The Manitoba on today's map is roughly eighteen times the size of the Manitoba of 1870.

1919: the largest strike in Canadian history

Between 15 May and 25 June 1919, more than 30,000 workers walked off the job in Winnipeg. On 21 June — remembered as Bloody Saturday — mounted police charged a crowd of strikers; two people were killed and about thirty injured, and federal troops occupied the streets. The strike failed at the time. Several of its leaders were later elected to Parliament and helped found the party that became the NDP.

Where the numbers disagree

Three worth knowing about, because you will meet them. Manitoba's land area is given as 553,556 km² by Natural Resources Canada and 540,310 km² by the census, which measure it differently — this page uses one source for the whole row so the numbers add up. Lake Winnipeg's rank among Canadian lakes is given as fifth on one provincial page and sixth on another, so this page avoids the Canadian ranking and uses the world one, where the province and Ottawa agree. And Manitoba's motto is Gloriosus et liber — “Friendly Manitoba” is the licence plate slogan, not the motto.

One thing this page does not tell you: the weather. Manitoba winters are famously cold, but the temperature figures repeated online could not be traced to Environment and Climate Change Canada, and the popular claim that Winnipeg is the coldest major city in the world has no source behind it at all. Rather than repeat a number nobody measured, this page leaves it out. The official climate normals are published by Environment and Climate Change Canada.
