Farming in Canada, in Honest Numbers

Canadian farms sold more than a hundred billion dollars of product last year. That is not the same thing as farmers earning a hundred billion dollars, and the gap is where the real story is.

The shape of Canadian farming today

There were 189,874 farms in Canada at the 2021 Census of Agriculture, run by 262,455 operators. The number of farms fell by 1.9 percent from 2016 — but Statistics Canada points out that this was the smallest decline in twenty-five years. Canadian farming is still consolidating, just more slowly than it used to.

The farms that remain are big. The average Canadian farm was 809 acres in 2021. For comparison, Statistics Canada puts the American average at 445 acres, the British at 210 and the Japanese at about 10. Canadian agriculture is unusual in the world for its scale.

Nearly a third of operators are women — 79,795 of them, or 30.4 percent, up from 28.7 percent five years earlier. That share has risen at every recent census.

The money, and why nobody can tell you the average farmer's income

Farm cash receipts reached 102.2 billion dollars in 2025, the first time they have passed a hundred billion since records began in 1926. But operating expenses were 83.0 billion of that. Realized net income was 8.3 billion.

At the census level the ratio is starker still: 83 cents of every dollar of farm revenue went straight back out as an expense. Farming is a high-turnover, thin-margin business, and a big revenue number tells you very little about what anybody takes home.

You will notice this page does not give you an average farmer's income. That is because Statistics Canada does not publish one, and inventing a figure would be easy and dishonest. What it does publish is farm family income, and that number tells the real story anyway.

The statistic that changes how you see the job

More than half the money in the average Canadian farm household does not come from farming. Off-farm sources made up 54.1 percent of total farm family income in 2023, up from 53.4 percent two years earlier.

And it is not just investment income. Almost half of all farm operators — 47.7 percent — reported working off the farm in 2020, and two thirds of those worked full-time hours somewhere else. A great many Canadian farms are run around another job.

There is also a large split between incorporated and unincorporated farms. Incorporated farm families averaged 358,850 dollars of total income in 2023; unincorporated ones averaged 151,458. Talking about the average Canadian farm hides two rather different businesses.

What the government actually offers a farmer

Canada runs four business risk management programmes, and they do different jobs. It is worth knowing which is which before you need one:

  • AgriStability catches large income drops. It pays when your production margin falls more than thirty percent below your reference margin. For the 2025 programme year the compensation rate was raised to ninety percent and the payment cap doubled to six million dollars.
  • AgriInvest handles small drops. It is a savings account you contribute to and the government matches the first one percent of your allowable net sales. Sales count up to a million dollars, so the maximum government contribution is ten thousand dollars a year.
  • AgriInsurance is production insurance against natural hazards — weather, pests, disease — cost-shared between Ottawa, the province and the producer.
  • AgriRecovery is not an ongoing programme at all. It is a framework used to build one-off help after a specific disaster, when the other three are not enough.

For someone starting out there is also the Canadian Agricultural Loans Act, under which Ottawa guarantees ninety-five percent of a lender's net loss, up to five hundred thousand dollars per farmer. Farm Credit Canada, a federal Crown corporation, offers a Young Farmer Loan of up to two million dollars to producers under forty, with no loan processing fees.

One tax rule worth knowing about early

Qualified farm property carries a lifetime capital gains exemption of 1,250,000 dollars for 2025, with indexation resuming in 2026. There is also an intergenerational rollover that lets a farmer transfer farm property to a child on a tax-deferred basis, provided the child was resident in Canada immediately before the transfer and the property was used in a farming business somebody in the family was actively engaged in on a regular and ongoing basis.

Qualified property includes land and buildings, shares of a family farm corporation, a family farm partnership interest, and quota such as milk and egg quota. This is genuinely complicated and worth an accountant rather than a website.

Where these facts come from

Figures come from the 2021 Census of Agriculture, Statistics Canada's farm income and farm family income releases, Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's programme pages, Farm Credit Canada and the Canada Revenue Agency.

There is no average farmer's salary on this page because no such figure is published. Anyone who gives you one has either made it up or has quietly used farm family income, more than half of which is earned somewhere other than the farm.

Common questions

How much does a Canadian farmer earn?

There is no published figure for an average farmer's income. Statistics Canada publishes farm family income, which averaged $216,021 in 2023 — but only $99,233 of that came from farming, with 54.1% coming from off-farm sources.

Do most Canadian farmers have another job?

Almost half do. 47.7% of farm operators reported off-farm employment in 2020, and two thirds of those worked full-time hours elsewhere.

How big is the average Canadian farm?

809 acres in 2021. Statistics Canada puts the American average at 445 acres, the British at 210 and the Japanese at about 10.

What help does the government give farmers?

Four business risk management programs: AgriStability for large income drops, AgriInvest for small ones, AgriInsurance for production losses from natural hazards, and AgriRecovery as a framework for one-off disaster help.
