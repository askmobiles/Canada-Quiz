Canada Built an Arm, and Sent It Into Space

Canada is not famous for rockets. Canada is famous for the arm on the end of one.

A country that built the arm

In the 1970s the United States was building a space shuttle. It needed something that could reach out of the cargo bay and lift things: satellites to let go of, satellites to catch and bring home.

Canada said it would build the arm. Engineers at Spar Aerospace in Toronto, with CAE and DSMA and the National Research Council, designed a robot arm with a shoulder, an elbow and a wrist, just like yours.

It was 15 metres long, about as long as a school bus, and it weighed only about 410 kilograms. It flew for the first time on 13 November 1981, on the shuttle Columbia. Painted on the side was one word: CANADA.

Strong, but gentle

Here is the strange part. In space nothing weighs anything, so an arm that is thin and light can still push something enormous. The Canadian Space Agency says the Canadarm could move objects as heavy as 266,000 kilograms in space, using less electricity than a kettle.

Being strong was not the hard part. Being gentle was. The arm moved slowly on purpose. A satellite is delicate, and there is no way to say sorry to something you have just bent in half two hundred kilometres above the ground.

An astronaut flew it from inside the shuttle, watching through a window and on cameras, with two hand controllers. Canadian astronaut Chris Hadfield was the first Canadian to fly it, in 1995.

The Canadarm flew 90 times over 30 years and retired in July 2011. You can go and look at the very first one. It is at the Canada Aviation and Space Museum in Ottawa.

The arm that walks

In April 2001 Canada sent up a second, bigger arm: Canadarm2, 17 metres long. It lives outside the International Space Station and it has never come home.

Canadarm2 has a hand on BOTH ends. That means it does not need to stay in one place. It grabs on with one hand, lets go with the other, and tips itself over, end over end, like an inchworm crossing a leaf. That is how it gets around the station.

It built most of the station, piece by piece. And when a cargo ship arrives with food and equipment, it does not dock by itself. Canadarm2 reaches out and catches it. By 2021 it had caught more than forty of them.

On 28 April 2001 the two Canadian arms worked together for the first time, passing something from one to the other. People called it the first robotic handshake in space.

Dextre, the robot handyman

In March 2008 Canada sent up a third robot, and this one is the strangest of the three. Dextre has two arms, each about three and a half metres long, and it hangs on the end of Canadarm2 like a hand on the end of an arm.

Dextre does the fiddly outdoor jobs: changing batteries, swapping broken parts, sniffing for leaks. It works to within a few millimetres, which is thinner than a pencil.

Every job Dextre does is a job an astronaut does not have to go outside to do. Going outside means hours of preparing, a heavy suit, and real danger. A robot that can change a battery is a robot that keeps people safe.

The arm came back down to Earth

Here is the part almost nobody knows. The people who worked out how to move a robot arm very precisely in space used what they had learned to build robot arms for hospitals.

In 2008 a robot called neuroArm, built with that same Canadian know-how, helped surgeons in Calgary remove a tumour from a young woman's brain. Later the same company worked with SickKids in Toronto on an arm made for operating on children.

So a machine built to hold a satellite in the dark, two hundred kilometres up, taught us how to hold a person very carefully in a room full of lights.

Canada is now building a third arm, Canadarm3, clever enough to do some jobs with nobody telling it what to do. It was first meant for a small station going round the Moon. In 2026 that plan changed, and Canada announced it would use the work to help astronauts on the Moon's surface instead. Where it ends up, it will still say CANADA on the side.

Words to know

Satellite
A machine that circles around the Earth, sending pictures or messages.
Cargo bay
The big open space in the middle of the shuttle where things were carried.
Space station
A home in space where astronauts live and work for months at a time.
Spacewalk
When an astronaut goes outside the spacecraft in a suit.
Did you know? The Canadarm never once lifted anything on Earth. It only ever worked where nothing weighs anything, and that is exactly why something so thin could move something so huge.
