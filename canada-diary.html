Long before Canada

People have lived here for thousands of years. Archaeologists still argue about how long, and the honest answer is that the first peoples arrived a very long time before anyone wrote anything down. Their descendants are here now. That is where this diary starts, not in 1867.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Parks Canada

⚓The sea About the year 1000

The Norse reach Newfoundland

About a thousand years ago, Norse sailors built a small settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, on the northern tip of Newfoundland. They stayed only a few years. It is the only confirmed Norse site in North America, and it means Europeans reached this coast roughly five hundred years before Columbus.

Read more on this

— Parks Canada, L'Anse aux Meadows National Historic Site; UNESCO

⚓The sea June 24, 1497

John Cabot sights land

Sailing for England, John Cabot reached the east coast, most likely Newfoundland or Cape Breton. He went home with news of a sea so thick with cod that you could lower a basket and pull it up full. That story brought fishing fleets across the Atlantic for the next four hundred years.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Library and Archives Canada

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history July 7, 1534

The first trade

In the Baie des Chaleurs, Mi'kmaq people came out in canoes holding up furs on sticks, and Cartier's men traded knives and iron goods for them. It is one of the first recorded exchanges between the French and Indigenous peoples here. Both sides had done this kind of thing before, just not with each other.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, 'Jacques Cartier'

🧭Exploration and gold July 24, 1534

Cartier plants a cross at Gaspé

Jacques Cartier raised a ten-metre cross at Gaspé and claimed the land for France. Chief Donnacona of the Iroquoians came out to object, because he understood exactly what the cross meant. The claim stood anyway, and New France began.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Parks Canada

🎭Culture and stories 1535

The country gets its name by mistake

On his second voyage, Cartier was shown the way to Stadacona, near today's Quebec City. His guides used the Iroquoian word kanata, which means a village. Cartier took it to mean the whole country. Canada is a village that grew until the name stopped fitting.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Name of Canada'; Canadian Heritage, "Origin of the name Canada"

🧭Exploration and gold October 2, 1535

Jacques Cartier reaches Hochelaga

Jacques Cartier sailed up the St Lawrence River and reached Hochelaga, an Iroquoian town on the island where Montreal stands today. In his log he described a town behind a wooden wall, with about 1,500 people living in some fifty longhouses. It is one of the first written descriptions of a large Indigenous town in the St Lawrence valley.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Exploration timeline

🧭Exploration and gold 1576

Frobisher looks for a passage

Martin Frobisher reached Baffin Island searching for a route to Asia. He found no passage. He did carry home a shipload of glittering rock that everyone was sure was gold, and it turned out to be worthless. The search for a northern passage went on for another three hundred years.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Library and Archives Canada

🔥Disaster August 5, 1583

England claims Newfoundland

Sir Humphrey Gilbert stood in St John's harbour, among fishing crews from four countries who were already using it, and claimed the island for Queen Elizabeth. It was England's first claim in what is now Canada. He drowned on the voyage home.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, 'Humphrey Gilbert'

🧭Exploration and gold 1605

The first winter that worked

The French built a habitation at Port-Royal, in what is now Nova Scotia. Earlier attempts had ended in scurvy and death. This one held, partly because they planted gardens and partly because they made the winter bearable on purpose, with shared meals and a small theatre.

— Parks Canada, Port-Royal National Historic Site

🧭Exploration and gold July 3, 1608

Champlain founds Quebec City

Samuel de Champlain built a small trading post where the St. Lawrence narrows. Of the twenty-eight men who spent that first winter with him, twenty died. The post survived. It is the oldest city in Canada, and the only walled city north of Mexico.

— Parks Canada; Canadian Encyclopedia

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history July 30, 1609

Champlain fires on the Haudenosaunee

Champlain joined his Innu, Algonquin and Huron-Wendat allies in a fight against the Haudenosaunee beside the lake that now bears his name. His firearm decided the day. It also bound the French to one side of a conflict that shaped the next hundred and fifty years.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, exploration timeline; Parks Canada, Fort Chambly National Historic Site

🧭Exploration and gold October 13, 1609

Champlain sails back to France

Samuel de Champlain arrived back in France after a season in New France. He reported on how the new settlement at Quebec was doing and put it forward as a base for the fur trade. Quebec had been founded only the year before, in 1608. Champlain crossed the Atlantic many times to keep the small colony supplied.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Exploration timeline

🧭Exploration and gold 1610

Henry Hudson enters the bay

Hudson sailed into the huge inland sea that now carries his name, certain he had found the way to Asia. He had not. His crew mutinied the next summer and set him adrift in a small boat with his son and seven others. None of them were seen again.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Library and Archives Canada

🧭Exploration and gold July 5, 1610

England's first colony here

John Guy sailed from Bristol with thirty-nine settlers to found Cupids, in Newfoundland. It was the first English colony in what is now Canada, and the second anywhere in North America. The village is still there, four centuries later.

— Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador, Memorial University

🍁The land and the map May 17, 1642

Montreal is founded

A small group led by Paul de Chomedey de Maisonneuve and Jeanne Mance landed on the island and founded Ville-Marie. It was meant as a mission, not a market. Within a lifetime it was the busiest fur-trading place in North America, which is not what anyone intended.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Ville de Montréal

👤People September 22, 1653

Marguerite Bourgeoys lands at Quebec

After a hard crossing from France, Marguerite Bourgeoys stepped ashore at Quebec. She had come to teach the children of Ville-Marie, the small settlement that became Montreal. At first no child there was old enough for school, so she waited. She opened her first school in a stone stable in 1658. The order she founded, the Congregation of Notre-Dame, still teaches today.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, Marguerite Bourgeoys

🍁The land and the map 1663

New France becomes a royal colony

Louis XIV took the colony out of the hands of a trading company and ran it himself. Soldiers, administrators and settlers began arriving in numbers for the first time. Before this the French population here was smaller than a modern high school.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Library and Archives Canada

👤People 1663

The King's Daughters

Between 1663 and 1673, about 800 young women crossed the ocean with their passage paid by the King, to marry and settle New France. Most were poor, many were orphans. A very large share of French Canadians alive today can trace a line back to one of them.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Filles du Roi'; Library and Archives Canada

🧭Exploration and gold May 2, 1670

The Hudson's Bay Company gets its charter

King Charles II signed away a territory he had never seen: every scrap of land drained by rivers flowing into Hudson Bay. That is about a third of modern Canada, handed to a fur company by a signature. The company was still trading more than three and a half centuries later.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; HBC Archives, Manitoba

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history August 4, 1701

The Great Peace of Montreal

Representatives of about forty Indigenous nations and the French signed a peace at Montreal that ended decades of war. Each nation marked the treaty with its own emblem: a beaver, a crane, a fish. It held for most of a century, which is longer than many treaties made since.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Great Peace of Montreal'; Parks Canada, Directory of Federal Heritage Designations

⚔️War and the people in it April 11, 1713

Acadia changes hands

A treaty signed in a Dutch city handed mainland Acadia, Newfoundland and the Hudson Bay lands to Britain. The Acadians living there were not asked and did not move. That unresolved arrangement lasted forty-two years and then ended badly.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Treaty of Utrecht'; Newfoundland and Labrador Heritage Web Site (Memorial University of Newfoundland), "The Treaty of Utrecht, 1713"

🍁The land and the map 1749

Halifax is founded

Britain built a fortified town on Chebucto Bay to answer the French fortress at Louisbourg. It was placed on Mi'kmaq land without a purchase or a treaty of surrender, a fact the courts and the country are still working through today.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Parks Canada

⚔️War and the people in it 1755

The Acadians are deported

British authorities ordered the Acadians of Nova Scotia removed from their farms. Families were split, villages burned, and thousands were put on ships. Some ended up in Louisiana, where Acadian slowly became Cajun. Others came home years later to find their land taken.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Deportation of the Acadians'; Parks Canada

⚔️War and the people in it July 28, 1755

The order to deport the Acadians

The Nova Scotia Council decided to remove the Acadians from their farms. About ten thousand people were put on ships over the following years. They are still called the Grand Dérangement, the great upheaval, and the descendants of those families are in Louisiana, France and the Maritimes today.

— Acadian Affairs and Francophonie, Province of Nova Scotia

👤People September 5, 1755

The men of Grand-Pré are called to the church

Colonel John Winslow ordered every Acadian male aged 10 and up in the Grand-Pré area to gather in the church for a message. There he read an order from the Lieutenant-Governor of Nova Scotia. Their land, houses and animals now belonged to the Crown, and they were to be removed from the province. It was the start of the Deportation of the Acadians. Between 1755 and 1763, about 10,000 Acadians were sent away by ship.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Acadian Expulsion (the Great Upheaval)

⚔️War and the people in it July 26, 1758

Louisbourg falls

After a siege of seven weeks the French governor surrendered the fortress of Louisbourg on Cape Breton. It had been built to guard the mouth of the St Lawrence. With it gone, the road to Quebec was open, and Quebec fell the following year.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Louisbourg'; Parks Canada

⚖️Law, rights and government October 2, 1758

The first elected assembly meets in Halifax

Twenty-two elected men met in a small wooden building at the corner of Argyle and Buckingham streets in Halifax. It was the first elected assembly anywhere in what is now Canada. Only Protestant men who owned property were allowed to vote. The Nova Scotia House of Assembly still sits today, and it is the oldest in the country.

Read more on this

— Nova Scotia Legislature, History

⚔️War and the people in it July 31, 1759

Wolfe is thrown back at Beauport

Six weeks before the Plains of Abraham, Wolfe attacked the French line at Beauport below Quebec and was driven back with heavy losses. It is the battle nobody remembers. It is also why he took the risk of climbing the cliff in September.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Battle of the Plains of Abraham'; Parks Canada, Directory of Federal Heritage Designations

⚔️War and the people in it September 13, 1759

The Plains of Abraham

The battle outside Quebec City lasted about fifteen minutes. Both commanders died of their wounds, Wolfe on the field and Montcalm the next morning. Fifteen minutes decided which language would govern this half of the continent, and the argument has never entirely finished.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Parks Canada, Plains of Abraham

⚔️War and the people in it September 14, 1759

Montcalm dies in Quebec

Louis-Joseph de Montcalm, born at Candiac in France in 1712, commanded the French army in New France. He was badly wounded the day before, during the battle on the Plains of Abraham. He died in Quebec City on the morning of 14 September. His opponent, James Wolfe, had died on the battlefield. Within days, the city surrendered.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Louis-Joseph, Marquis de Montcalm

⚔️War and the people in it September 18, 1759

The siege of Quebec ends

British ships and troops had besieged Quebec since 26 June. Five days after the battle on the Plains of Abraham, the city's defenders gave up and the articles of capitulation were agreed. New France was formally handed to Britain four years later, by the Treaty of Paris.

— Parks Canada, Siege of Quebec 1759 National Historic Event

⚔️War and the people in it September 8, 1760

Montreal surrenders

A year after Quebec fell, Montreal surrendered without a battle. The terms let people keep their property and their religion. New France was finished as a French possession, but the people did not leave, and that is the whole reason Canada looks the way it does.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Capitulation of Montreal'; Parks Canada, Fort Lennox National Historic Site

⚔️War and the people in it February 10, 1763

France gives up Canada

The treaty that ended the Seven Years' War handed New France to Britain. France kept two tiny islands off Newfoundland, Saint-Pierre and Miquelon, and they are French to this day. It is the last piece of New France still on the map.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Treaty of Paris 1763'; Canadian Museum of History, Virtual Museum of New France

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history October 7, 1763

The Royal Proclamation

The King set out that Indigenous nations held their lands until they chose to give them up, and only to the Crown. It is sometimes called the Indian Magna Carta. More than two and a half centuries later it is still cited in Canadian courts, which is rare for any document.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Royal Proclamation of 1763'; Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada, "The Royal Proclamation of 1763"

⚖️Law, rights and government June 22, 1774

The Quebec Act

Britain had a choice: force the French-speaking population to become English, or let them keep their language, their civil law and their Catholic faith. It chose the second. That decision is the reason Canada is bilingual today, and it was made for practical reasons, not generous ones.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Quebec Act'; National Assembly of Québec, Encyclopedia of Quebec Parliamentarism

⚔️War and the people in it September 6, 1775

An ambush beside the Richelieu

American forces invading Canada landed near Fort Saint-Jean, in what is now Saint-Jean-sur-Richelieu, Quebec. About a hundred Mohawk and allied Indigenous fighters ambushed them beside a creek. The Americans were pushed back and withdrew to Île aux Noix. The place is a national historic site today.

— Parks Canada, Battle of September 6th, 1775 National Historic Site

⚔️War and the people in it December 31, 1775

The Americans attack Quebec

An American army marched north to bring the colony into their revolution, and attacked Quebec City in a snowstorm on the last night of the year. The attack failed. General Montgomery was killed and Benedict Arnold was wounded. Canada stayed out of that war.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Battle of Quebec 1775'; Parks Canada

🍁The land and the map September 3, 1783

The Loyalists arrive

When the United States won its independence, tens of thousands of people who had stayed loyal to the Crown left for the north. Around 3,000 were Black Loyalists, many of them formerly enslaved people promised freedom for their service. They founded towns that are still there.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Loyalists'; Library and Archives Canada

👤People July 26, 1784

The Shelburne riots

In Shelburne, Nova Scotia, a crowd of white Loyalist settlers attacked the homes of Black Loyalists, who had been promised land and freedom for their service and had received far less of both. It is usually called Canada's first race riot. Many of those families later left for Sierra Leone.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'The Shelburne Race Riots'; Black Loyalist Heritage Centre (Government of Nova Scotia), "Black Loyalist Communities in Nova Scotia"

🍁The land and the map August 16, 1784

New Brunswick is created

So many Loyalists had settled north of the Bay of Fundy that Britain split the colony in two and made New Brunswick. It was built almost entirely by refugees, in one summer, out of nothing. Saint John became the first incorporated city in what is now Canada.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Province of New Brunswick

🧭Exploration and gold September 12, 1789

Mackenzie comes home from the Arctic

On 3 June, Alexander Mackenzie had left Fort Chipewyan with a small party of voyageurs and guides, looking for a way to the Pacific. Instead, the big river carried them to the Arctic Ocean. They turned around and paddled home, reaching Fort Chipewyan on 12 September. In just over three months they had travelled more than 4,800 kilometres. The river now carries his name.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Sir Alexander Mackenzie (Explorer)

⚖️Law, rights and government 1791

Upper and Lower Canada

Britain divided the colony so English-speaking Loyalists and French-speaking Canadiens could each have their own assembly and their own laws. It was the first time either group here elected anybody. The two halves became Ontario and Quebec.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Constitutional Act 1791'; National Assembly of Québec, "Acte constitutionnel de 1791"

⚖️Law, rights and government September 17, 1792

Upper Canada's first parliament meets

The first session of the Legislative Assembly of Upper Canada opened at Newark, today Niagara-on-the-Lake. Lieutenant-Governor John Graves Simcoe had called the members together. The session lasted until 15 October. Its first laws brought in English property law and trial by jury. It was the first time people in the colony elected members to make laws.

Read more on this

— Parks Canada, First Legislative Assembly of Upper Canada National Historic Event

⚖️Law, rights and government July 9, 1793

Upper Canada limits slavery

Lieutenant Governor John Graves Simcoe pushed through a law stopping any new person from being brought into Upper Canada as a slave. It did not free anyone already enslaved, so it was a half measure. It was still among the first laws of its kind anywhere in the British Empire, and word of it travelled south.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Act to Limit Slavery in Upper Canada'; Parks Canada, "Upper Canadian Act of 1793 Against Slavery National Historic Event"

🧭Exploration and gold July 22, 1793

First across the continent

Alexander Mackenzie reached the Pacific at Bella Coola, having crossed North America by land. He mixed vermilion with bear grease and wrote his name and the date on a rock. He was the first European to cross the continent north of Mexico, twelve years before Lewis and Clark.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Alexander Mackenzie'; BC Parks (Government of British Columbia), Sir Alexander Mackenzie Park

⚔️War and the people in it June 18, 1812

The War of 1812 begins

The United States declared war and invaded, expecting an easy welcome. They did not get one. British soldiers, Canadian militia and First Nations allies pushed them back. Nobody really won the war. What it settled was that this land would not become part of the United States.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'War of 1812'; Parks Canada, Battle of the Châteauguay National Historic Site

⚔️War and the people in it July 12, 1812

The invasion begins

An American army under General Hull crossed the river into Upper Canada at Sandwich, now part of Windsor. He issued a proclamation telling the people they were being freed. Within a month he had retreated, and by August he had surrendered Detroit.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, War of 1812 timeline; Department of National Defence, "In Defence of their Homelands" (Aboriginal People in the Canadian Military)

🍁The land and the map September 4, 1812

Selkirk's colony is proclaimed at Red River

Miles Macdonell led the first settlers sent west by Lord Selkirk. They reached the place where the Red and Assiniboine rivers meet on 30 August. On 4 September he invited the North West Company men, local Métis and First Nations people to a ceremony. Selkirk's right to the land was read out, guns were fired and people cheered. This was the start of the Red River Settlement, where Winnipeg stands today.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, Macdonell, Miles

⚔️War and the people in it October 13, 1812

Brock dies at Queenston Heights

General Isaac Brock was shot leading a charge up the escarpment above the Niagara River. The battle was won, largely by Mohawk warriors under John Norton who held the heights for hours afterwards. Brock became the war's great figure, and the Mohawk part of the story took much longer to be told.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Battle of Queenston Heights'; Parks Canada

⚓The sea August 8, 1813

The Hamilton and the Scourge

Two American schooners capsized in a sudden squall on Lake Ontario in the middle of the night and sank in minutes. More than fifty sailors drowned. Both ships are still on the lake bed, upright and almost undisturbed in the cold fresh water.

— Parks Canada, Hamilton and Scourge National Historic Site

⚔️War and the people in it September 10, 1813

The Battle of Lake Erie

During the War of 1812, six British ships under Robert Barclay fought nine American ships under Oliver Hazard Perry on Lake Erie. Every British ship was captured, and all the surviving officers and sailors were taken prisoner. The Americans now controlled the lake. With no way to get supplies, General Henry Procter burned Fort Amherstburg and fell back up the Thames River, with Tecumseh and his warriors beside him.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Battle of Lake Erie (Battle of Put-in-Bay)

⚔️War and the people in it October 5, 1813

Tecumseh is killed

The Shawnee leader Tecumseh died at the Battle of the Thames, in what is now southwestern Ontario. He had built an alliance of nations to defend their own land, and had been the most effective commander on the British side. His alliance did not survive him, and neither did the promises made to it.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Tecumseh'; Dictionary of Canadian Biography, "TECUMSEH (Tech-kum-thai)"

⚔️War and the people in it July 25, 1814

Lundy's Lane

Within sound of Niagara Falls, British and Canadian troops fought an American army through the evening and into the dark, at times firing at each other from a few metres apart. Close to nine hundred were killed, wounded or missing on each side. It was the last American invasion of Upper Canada.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Battle of Lundy's Lane'; Parks Canada, Directory of Federal Heritage Designations

⚔️War and the people in it December 24, 1814

The war ends on Christmas Eve

The Treaty of Ghent ended the War of 1812 with the border exactly where it had started. News travelled by ship, so the largest battle of the war was fought at New Orleans two weeks after peace had already been signed.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Treaty of Ghent'; Canadian War Museum, 1812 Virtual Exhibition

⚖️Law, rights and government April 1817

The lakes are disarmed

Britain and the United States agreed to keep almost no warships on the Great Lakes. The agreement is still in force. It is one of the oldest disarmament treaties anywhere, and it is a large part of why the border below us became the quiet thing it is.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Rush-Bagot Agreement'; Global Affairs Canada, Canada Treaty Information

🍁The land and the map November 25, 1817

Commissioners divide the islands of Passamaquoddy Bay

Commissioners working after the War of 1812 decided who owned the islands between New Brunswick and the United States. Moose, Dudley and Frederick Islands were given to the United States. All the other islands in Passamaquoddy Bay, and Grand Manan Island in the Bay of Fundy, stayed part of New Brunswick. That line still separates the two countries in the bay.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, War of 1812 Timeline

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history October 20, 1818

The 49th parallel becomes the border

Britain and the United States signed the Convention of 1818 in London. It set the border between British North America and the United States along the 49th parallel, from the north-west point of Lake of the Woods to the Rocky Mountains. In 1846 the same line was carried on to the Pacific coast. Much of Canada's southern border still follows it.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Convention of 1818

🧭Exploration and gold 1821

Two fur companies become one

The Hudson's Bay Company and the North West Company had competed so hard that both were close to ruin, and men had been killed. They merged. The new company controlled trade across most of the northern half of the continent, which shaped where towns went for a century.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'North West Company'; HBC Archives

🔥Disaster October 7, 1825

A great fire sweeps the Miramichi

A forest fire swept through the settlements along the Miramichi River, in northeastern New Brunswick. It burned about 15,500 square kilometres, one of the largest forest fires ever recorded in North America. Up to 500 people are believed to have died, out of a population of about 8,500. The fire is now marked as a national historic event.

— Parks Canada, National historic events, The Miramichi Fire of 1825

🚂Built things November 30, 1829

The first Welland Canal opens

Two ships entered the new canal and reached Chippawa: the Canadian schooner Annie and Jane of York and the American vessel R.H. Boughton. The canal joined Lake Ontario and Lake Erie, which sit 99 metres apart in height. Before it, goods had to be carried overland around Niagara Falls. Digging had started exactly five years earlier.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, The Evolution of the Welland Canal

👤People October 28, 1830

Josiah Henson reaches Upper Canada

Josiah Henson, his wife and their four children arrived in Upper Canada on the morning of 28 October 1830, after escaping slavery in the United States. In 1841 he helped found the British American Institute near Dresden, a school and settlement for people who had come north to be free. His life story was published in 1849.

— Ontario Heritage Trust, Josiah Henson Museum of African-Canadian History, History

🚂Built things 1832

The Rideau Canal opens

A canal was cut from Kingston to Bytown, later Ottawa, so boats could avoid the American shore of the St. Lawrence in case of another war. Hundreds of workers died, most of them Irish, most of malaria. The war never came. The canal is now a World Heritage Site and the world's longest skating rink.

— Parks Canada, Rideau Canal; UNESCO

⚓The sea August 18, 1833

Across the Atlantic on steam

The Royal William left Pictou, Nova Scotia for London and became one of the first ships to cross the Atlantic essentially under steam power. She had to stop to clean salt out of her boilers along the way, which is why the claim is argued about.

— Government of Nova Scotia

⚖️Law, rights and government August 1, 1834

Slavery ends across the Empire

The Slavery Abolition Act came into force, ending slavery in British North America and across the Empire. Canada now marks the first of August as Emancipation Day. For decades afterwards this was the northern end of the Underground Railroad, and people walked here to be free.

— Government of Canada, Emancipation Day; Canadian Encyclopedia

🚂Built things July 21, 1836

The first railway

The Champlain and St Lawrence Railroad opened, running about twenty-three kilometres between La Prairie and Saint-Jean-sur-Richelieu in Lower Canada. It was Canada's first railway. The locomotive was so unreliable that horses pulled the carriages for part of the first season.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, railway history timeline; Parks Canada, Directory of Federal Heritage Designations

⚔️War and the people in it 1837

The rebellions

Rebels rose in both Upper and Lower Canada against governments that answered to London rather than to the people who lived here. The rebellions failed, and their leaders fled or were hanged. But the complaint was heard, and within about ten years it produced responsible government.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Rebellions of 1837-38'; Canadian Museum of History, Teachers' Zone / Stories of Confederation

⚔️War and the people in it November 23, 1837

The Patriotes hold their ground at Saint-Denis

British regular soldiers under Colonel Gore marched on the village of Saint-Denis, on the Richelieu River in Lower Canada. The Patriotes met them and pushed the soldiers back. It was their one clear victory in the rebellion of 1837. A monument was raised at Saint-Denis in 1934 to remember the day.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, The Victory of Saint-Denis

⚔️War and the people in it November 25, 1837

The Patriotes are defeated at Saint-Charles

Two days after Saint-Denis, about 350 soldiers under Colonel Wetherall attacked the Patriote camp at Saint-Charles, on the Richelieu River. The defenders had started with about a hundred men and were down to sixty or eighty when the attack came. The barricades and the manor house were taken. The army then held the Richelieu valley.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Battle of St-Charles

⚖️Law, rights and government 1839

The Durham Report

Lord Durham was sent to find out what had gone wrong. He recommended giving the colony real self-government, which was wise, and assimilating the French, which was not, and did not happen. Canada took the first half of his advice and quietly ignored the second.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Durham Report'; Dictionary of Canadian Biography, "LAMBTON, JOHN GEORGE, 1st Earl of DURHAM"

🎭Culture and stories November 16, 1839

Louis-Honoré Fréchette is born at Pointe-Lévy

Louis-Honoré Fréchette was born at Pointe-Lévy, now Lévis, in Lower Canada. He worked as a journalist, a lawyer and a politician, and he wrote poetry and plays in French. In June 1880 the Académie française in Paris gave him one of its Montyon prizes for two books of his poems. It was the first honour of that kind given to a Canadian by a European country.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, FRECHETTE, LOUIS (Louis-Honore)

⚖️Law, rights and government February 10, 1841

The two Canadas are joined

Upper and Lower Canada were merged into one Province of Canada with a single assembly. The design was meant to outvote the French-speaking half. Instead the two sides learned to govern together in pairs, which is the working habit that made Confederation possible.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Act of Union'; National Assembly of Québec, "Chronologie parlementaire depuis 1764 (1839-1841)"

⚖️Law, rights and government 1848

Government answers to the people

Nova Scotia was first, in February, and the Province of Canada followed weeks later under Louis-Hippolyte LaFontaine and Robert Baldwin. From then on a government had to hold the confidence of the elected assembly, not the favour of a governor. It happened without a shot being fired.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Responsible Government'; Nova Scotia Legislature, "Responsible Government"

🔥Disaster April 25, 1849

A mob burns the Parliament

A crowd angry at a law compensating people for losses in the rebellions set fire to the Parliament building in Montreal. It burned to the ground with the library inside. Parliament never sat in Montreal again, which is part of why the capital eventually landed in Ottawa.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Burning of the Parliament Buildings in Montreal'; MEM

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history September 7, 1850

The Robinson-Superior Treaty

Ojibwe leaders from the Lake Superior country, led by Chief Peau de Chat, signed a treaty with the Crown at Sault Ste. Marie. It covered the Lake Superior shore from Batchewana Bay to the Pigeon River. The Crown promised yearly payments and the continued right to hunt and fish. Two days later the Lake Huron bands signed a companion treaty.

— Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada, Treaty Texts

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history September 9, 1850

The Robinson-Huron Treaty is signed

The Matheson fire

Small fires lit by settlers clearing land, and sparks from locomotives, joined into one firestorm that swept through Matheson and Cochrane in northern Ontario. More than two hundred people died. It is the deadliest forest fire in Canadian history.

🔥Disaster September 11, 1916

The Quebec Bridge falls a second time

Workers were lifting the centre span of the Quebec Bridge into place when it fell into the St. Lawrence River. Thirteen workers died and more than 14 were hurt. The bridge had already collapsed once, in 1907, killing 76 workers. A new centre span was put in place a year later, on 20 September 1917. The bridge is still the longest cantilever bridge in the world.

⚔️War and the people in it September 15, 1916

Tanks roll at Courcelette

The Canadian Corps attacked at Courcelette, on the Somme in France. Tanks went into battle for the first time in history. Six took part, and only one reached its goal. By 8 in the morning the Canadians had taken the German trenches and a sugar refinery. That evening the 22nd Battalion from Quebec and the 25th from Nova Scotia captured the village. The fighting lasted a week and cost several thousand Canadian casualties.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Battle of Courcelette

👤People July 8, 1917

Tom Thomson paddles out

The painter set off in his canoe on Canoe Lake in Algonquin Park. The canoe was found floating upside down that afternoon. He was one of the finest painters this country has produced, and what happened to him has been argued about ever since.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, 'Tom Thomson'

👤People July 16, 1917

Tom Thomson is found

Eight days after he paddled out, Thomson's body was recovered from Canoe Lake. He was thirty-nine. He had painted for only about five years. The painters who became the Group of Seven formed after his death, and always counted him as one of their own.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, 'Tom Thomson'

⚔️War and the people in it August 15, 1917

Hill 70

The Canadian Corps, under Arthur Currie, took a low rise near Lens in France. Currie had refused to attack the town itself and chose the high ground instead, then held it against twenty-one counterattacks. Six Canadians earned the Victoria Cross. There were about 9,200 Canadian casualties.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, 'Battle of Hill 70'

⚔️War and the people in it August 25, 1917

Hill 70 ends

Eleven days after it began, the fight for the low rise near Lens was over. Arthur Currie had chosen the hill rather than the town below it, taken it, and then held it against twenty-one counterattacks. Six Canadians earned the Victoria Cross there, and about 9,200 were killed or wounded.

— Department of National Defence, Battle Honours

⚖️Law, rights and government August 29, 1917

Conscription becomes law

The Military Service Act made men liable to be called up for overseas service. English Canada largely supported it and French Canada largely did not, and the split it opened ran through the next election and well beyond. It is one of the moments this country still argues about.

— Canadian Encyclopedia; Canadian War Museum, Canada and the First World War

⚖️Law, rights and government September 20, 1917

The first women get the federal vote

In one day Parliament passed two election laws. Together they gave the federal vote to nurses serving overseas and to close female relatives of soldiers — the first time women could vote in a Canadian federal election. The same laws took the vote away from many recent immigrants. Most women aged twenty-one and over received the federal vote the following year, though property rules and other restrictions still applied to some.

— Elections Canada, A History of the Vote in Canada

⚔️War and the people in it October 26, 1917

The Canadian Corps attacks at Passchendaele

The Canadian Corps began its attack toward Passchendaele ridge in Belgium, the first of four planned steps. The ground had been shelled for months and rain had turned it into deep mud. General Arthur Currie had warned the attack might cost 16,000 Canadians killed or wounded. By mid-November the ridge was taken and 15,654 Canadians had fallen.

— Canadian War Museum, Canada and the First World War, Passchendaele

⚔️War and the people in it November 6, 1917

Canadians take the village of Passchendaele

The first stage of the Canadian attack began under a heavy artillery barrage. The 2nd Division was given the task of taking the village of Passchendaele in Belgium. The ground was a swamp of mud and shell holes where a wounded man could drown. The village was taken that day.

— National Defence, Battle Honours, Passchendaele

⚔️War and the people in it November 10, 1917

The crest of Passchendaele Ridge is taken

The last task for the Canadian Corps was the crest of the ridge, about 900 metres north of the village. The attack went in that day and met limited resistance. It closed the Canadian part of a battle that had run since 26 October. The Canadian Army still carries Passchendaele as a battle honour.

— National Defence, Battle Honours, Passchendaele

⚔️War and the people in it November 20, 1917

A Canadian cavalry charge at Masnieres

Lieutenant Harcus Strachan of the Fort Garry Horse took command of his squadron near Masnieres, in France, after his squadron leader fell. He led the men through the German machine-gun posts and reached a gun battery about three kilometres behind the front. He then cut telephone wires, gathered his men in the dark and brought them back with fifteen prisoners. He received the Victoria Cross.

— Library and Archives Canada, The Discover Blog, Lieutenant-Colonel Harcus Strachan, VC

The black day of the German Army

The Canadian Corps led the attack at Amiens and advanced about twenty kilometres in three days, which in that war was extraordinary. The German commander called it the black day of the German Army. The hundred days that followed ended the First World War.

⚔️War and the people in it August 26, 1918

The Canadians attack at the Scarpe

Three weeks after Amiens, the Canadian Corps opened a new attack east of Arras and pushed forward more than five kilometres in a day. It was part of the hundred days that ended the war. The Corps did most of the hardest fighting of that autumn and paid for it.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, The Last Hundred Days

⚔️War and the people in it September 27, 1918

Crossing the Canal du Nord

In the last months of the First World War the Canadian Corps faced a dry canal bed in northern France that the Germans had turned into a strong defence. Canadian engineers and soldiers crossed at a narrow point and pushed through. Veterans Affairs Canada calls the attack a stunning success. Two Canadians won the Victoria Cross for what they did that day, and two more for actions that began on it.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, The Last Hundred Days

⚔️War and the people in it October 1, 1918

A sergeant wins the Victoria Cross near Abancourt

Sergeant William Merrifield of the Canadian Expeditionary Force attacked two enemy machine-gun posts on his own near Abancourt, in France. He was wounded in the first attack but kept going and silenced the second post. For this he was awarded the Victoria Cross, the highest award for bravery in the British and Canadian forces.

— Library and Archives Canada Blog, Sergeant William Merrifield, VC

⚔️War and the people in it October 9, 1918

Canadian soldiers enter Cambrai

Canadian troops entered the French city of Cambrai after two days of fighting. Much of the city was burning as they came in. The attack was part of Canada's Hundred Days, the last push of the First World War. The armistice came a month later, on 11 November.

— Department of National Defence, Battle Honours, Cambrai 1918

⚔️War and the people in it October 14, 1918

A seventeen-year-old wins the Victoria Cross

Private Thomas Ricketts of the Royal Newfoundland Regiment was awarded the Victoria Cross for his action at Ledeghem, in Belgium. Under fire, he carried ammunition forward over open ground so that his gun team could keep firing. He was seventeen, the youngest army winner of the award in the First World War. Newfoundland joined Canada in 1949.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, The Last Hundred Days

🔥Disaster October 25, 1918

The Princess Sophia is lost

The steamship Princess Sophia was sailing south from Skagway, carrying people who were leaving the Yukon before the winter. She ran onto Vanderbilt Reef in Lynn Canal and stuck fast. On the night of 25 October a storm pushed her off the reef and she sank. All 343 people aboard were lost; a dog was found alive.

⚔️War and the people in it November 1, 1918

Canadians attack at Valenciennes

The Canadian Corps attacked the German position on Mont Houy, a small hill south of the French city of Valenciennes. British troops had taken the hill days earlier and had not been able to hold it. Canadian gunners fired 303 guns to open the way for the infantry. The fighting for the city lasted two days.

— National Defence, Battle Honours, Valenciennes

⚔️War and the people in it November 2, 1918

Valenciennes is taken

Canadian soldiers finished clearing the French city of Valenciennes. About 1,800 German soldiers surrendered and more than 800 were counted dead in the battle area. The people of the city had lived under occupation for four years. It was one of the last large Canadian actions of the First World War.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, The Last Hundred Days

⚔️War and the people in it November 4, 1918

Canadians cross the Aunelle

The 3rd and 4th Canadian Divisions crossed the River Aunelle and met German defences on the far bank. The guns were brought forward and the infantry made ready to attack. The German troops pulled back before the attack began, and the Canadians followed them eastward. Only one week of the war was left.

— National Defence, Battle Honours, Sambre

⚔️War and the people in it November 11, 1918

Canadians march into Mons as the guns stop

Canadian troops fought through the Belgian city of Mons in the early morning and took it. At eleven o'clock the armistice came into force and the fighting stopped. Mons was where the British and German armies had first met in August 1914. Belgian families came into the streets to meet the Canadians.

— National Defence, Battle Honours, Pursuit to Mons

Banting writes down an idea about the pancreas

In the early hours of 31 October 1920, in London, Ontario, Frederick Banting woke and wrote a short note in his notebook. It said to tie off the pancreatic ducts of dogs, wait until part of the pancreas wasted away, then try to take out the secretion left behind. That note led to the work in Toronto that gave the world insulin.

A country doctor takes his idea to Toronto

Frederick Banting met Professor John Macleod in Toronto to explain an idea he had written down eight days earlier. Banting thought the pancreas held a substance that could treat diabetes. Canada receives its coat of arms

King George V signed the proclamation that gave Canada its own royal coat of arms. The same proclamation named red and white as Canada's official colours. Those two colours were later used for the flag Canada chose in 1965. The arms can still be seen on Canadian passports and on government papers.

Canada held its fifteenth general election. The Conservatives won the most seats, 115, but not a majority of the House of Commons. A government must hold the confidence of the House, not simply the largest number of seats.

An election about the Governor General

Earlier that year the Governor General had refused the Prime Minister's request to call an election. The Prime Minister made that refusal the main issue of the campaign that followed. William Lyon Mackenzie King was sworn in as prime minister for a second time. He had won the election held eleven days earlier. At an imperial conference in London, Britain and the self-governing dominions adopted a report written under Lord Balfour. It described Britain and dominions such as Canada as autonomous communities, equal in status and not under one another. Those words were later written into the Statute of Westminster of 1931, which gave Canada control of its own laws.

The August Gale

A storm caught the fishing fleet on the Grand Banks. Close to a hundred men from Nova Scotia and Newfoundland drowned, most of them from small ports where nearly every family lost somebody. Lunenburg alone lost more than eighty. The schooners had no radios.

Women are ruled to be persons

The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London ruled that the word person in Canada's constitution included women. The ruling meant a woman could be named to the Senate. Cairine Wilson became the first woman senator on 15 February 1930.

🔥Disaster November 18, 1929

An earthquake and a wave strike the Burin Peninsula

A magnitude 7.2 earthquake shook the sea floor about 250 kilometres south of Newfoundland. Two and a half hours later a wave reached the Burin Peninsula. More than 40 villages lost homes and boats.

The first Commonwealth Games close

Hamilton, Ontario hosted the first British Empire Games, which became the Commonwealth Games. Eleven countries came. Hamilton paid the travel costs of several teams that could not afford to get there, which is a large part of why the Games happened at all.

The Welland Canal opens

The fourth Welland Canal opened, lifting ocean ships around Niagara Falls between Lake Ontario and Lake Erie. Eight locks raise a vessel about a hundred metres, roughly the height of the falls it is avoiding.

The painter J.E.H. MacDonald dies in Toronto

J.E.H. MacDonald died in Toronto. He was one of the founders of the Group of Seven, the painters who put the rocks, lakes and trees of the Canadian north on canvas. He worked first as a designer of posters and lettering. He often painted in the Algoma region of Ontario and in the Rocky Mountains.

The Neuro opens in Montreal

The Montreal Neurological Institute opened as part of McGill University. The surgeon Wilder Penfield had wanted one place where doctors who treat the brain and scientists who study it would work side by side. The Rockefeller Foundation gave 1.2 million dollars to build it. Montrealers came to call it simply the Neuro.

⚔️War and the people in it September 3, 1939

The first Canadian death of the war

Britain and France declared war on Germany. That same day a German submarine torpedoed the passenger liner Athenia in the Atlantic. Among the dead was Hannah Baird of Quebec, a stewardess on the ship. She was the first Canadian killed in the Second World War. Canada declared war seven days later.

Canada declares war

Britain had declared war on Germany on 3 September. Canada did not follow automatically. Parliament was recalled, it debated, and it voted. Canada declared war on 10 September 1939 — the first time the country declared war on another by its own decision.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, chronology of the Second World War

A Canadian surgeon dies in China

Norman Bethune, born in Gravenhurst, Ontario, died at Huangshikou in Hebei province, China. He had gone there in 1938 to treat wounded soldiers and to train Chinese doctors and nurses. He built mobile medical units that went to the wounded instead of waiting for them. He died of blood poisoning caught while operating.

— Dictionary of Canadian Biography, Henry Norman Bethune

The Ogdensburg Agreement

Mackenzie King and Roosevelt signed an agreement creating a joint board on defence. Britain was fighting alone in Europe, and Canada turned toward its neighbour for its security. The defence relationship signed that day has never been undone.

⚓The sea November 5, 1940

A convoy escort is lost in the Atlantic

The armed merchant cruiser HMS Jervis Bay was escorting Convoy HX-84 across the North Atlantic when a German warship found the convoy. The Jervis Bay was sunk. Seven Canadian sailors survived. Convoys like HX-84 carried food, fuel and weapons from Halifax to Britain, and Canadians crewed many of the merchant ships.

— Parks Canada, Role of the Canadian Merchant Navy during the Second World War National Historic Event

⚔️War and the people in it August 13, 1941

The Canadian Women's Army Corps

The army created a corps for women, and about 21,000 served in it during the war as drivers, clerks, mechanics and signallers. They were paid two thirds of a man's wage for the same work. Many of them never went back to the kitchen afterwards.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, women veterans timeline

⚔️War and the people in it August 14, 1941

The Atlantic Charter

Churchill and Roosevelt met secretly on warships in Placentia Bay, Newfoundland, and issued a statement of what the Allies were fighting for. It became one of the foundations of the United Nations. Newfoundland was not yet part of Canada.

— Parks Canada, Atlantic Charter National Historic Event

⚔️War and the people in it September 10, 1941

Two small ships sink their first submarine

HMCS Chambly and HMCS Moose Jaw were corvettes, small warships built to guard convoys. Off the coast of Greenland they found the German submarine U-501 waiting to attack a convoy. Chambly dropped depth charges and Moose Jaw rammed the submarine. It was the first German submarine sunk by the Royal Canadian Navy. One Canadian sailor, William Brown of Toronto, was lost when the submarine went down.

— Library and Archives Canada blog, First German submarine sunk by the Royal Canadian Navy

🏅Sport September 18, 1941

The Vancouver Asahi play their last game

The Asahi were a baseball team of Japanese Canadians from Vancouver, known for quick, smart base running. They had won city championships for years. This was the last game the team ever played. In 1942 Japanese Canadians were forced to leave the coast, and the Asahi never played together again.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Second World War Timeline

⚔️War and the people in it August 19, 1942

Dieppe

Of the 4,963 Canadians who sailed for the French port of Dieppe, 916 were killed and 1,946 were taken prisoner. Only 2,210 came back to England, and many of those were wounded. It was over in nine hours. What was learned that morning was used two years later on D-Day.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, 'Dieppe Raid'

⚔️War and the people in it September 7, 1942

A patrol ship is lost in the St. Lawrence

HMCS Raccoon had been a private yacht before the navy turned it into a patrol ship. Early in the morning, a German submarine sank it near Pointe-au-Père, Quebec, while it was guarding a convoy in the St. Lawrence River. In the confusion, nobody realized the ship was gone until later that day. All 37 of its crew were lost. Very little of the ship was ever found.

— Canadian War Museum, Canada's Naval History, HMCS Raccoon

⚔️War and the people in it September 13, 1942

HMCS Ottawa is lost in the Atlantic

The destroyer HMCS Ottawa was escorting convoy ON 127 across the North Atlantic. The German submarine U-91 torpedoed and sank it. One hundred and fourteen of the ship's company were lost. It was one of the hardest losses of the Battle of the Atlantic for the Royal Canadian Navy. A later destroyer was given the same name.

— Royal Canadian Navy, Ships' histories, HMCS Ottawa

⚓The sea October 11, 1942

The St. Roch reaches Halifax through the Northwest Passage

The RCMP schooner St. Roch docked in Halifax after sailing from Vancouver through the ice of the Northwest Passage. Sergeant Henry Larsen and his small crew had left in 1940 and spent two winters frozen in. It was the first crossing of the passage from west to east. The ship is kept in Vancouver today.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Henry Larsen and the St. Roch

⚓The sea October 14, 1942

The ferry Caribou is lost in the Cabot Strait

The passenger ferry SS Caribou was torpedoed in the Cabot Strait on her crossing from North Sydney, Nova Scotia, to Port aux Basques, Newfoundland. One hundred and thirty-seven people died. It was one of the worst losses of life in Canadian waters during the Second World War. A memorial at Port aux Basques remembers them.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, S.S. Caribou Memorial

⚓The sea November 2, 1942

A submarine attack at Bell Island

A German submarine came into Conception Bay in Newfoundland and attacked ships loading iron ore at Bell Island. One torpedo struck the Scotia Pier on shore. The ore carrier Rose Castle and the Free French vessel PLM 27 were sunk. Seventy men died in the two submarine attacks at Bell Island that year.

— Parks Canada, German U-Boat Attacks at Bell Island National Historic Event

⚔️War and the people in it July 10, 1943

Canadians land in Sicily

Just after dawn, the 1st Canadian Infantry Division waded ashore in Sicily. It was the start of the Italian Campaign, which lasted almost two years and cost around 26,000 Canadian casualties. Many of the men had never seen a mountain before, and they fought their way up a country made of them.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, 'Italian Campaign'

⚔️War and the people in it September 3, 1943

The Canadians cross into mainland Italy

Fifty-five days after landing in Sicily, Canadian soldiers crossed the Strait of Messina with British troops and came ashore on the toe of Italy. Ahead of them lay mountains, rivers and villages taken one at a time. Canadians went on fighting in Italy until February 1945.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Italian Campaign

⚓The sea September 20, 1943

The loss of HMCS St. Croix

The Canadian destroyer HMCS St. Croix was guarding a convoy in the North Atlantic when a German submarine hit her with two torpedoes. She sank that night. A British ship picked up 81 survivors, but it was sunk in its turn three days later. In the end 147 Canadian sailors were lost, and one man, Stoker William Fisher, came home.

— Naval Museum of Manitoba, HMCS St. Croix

⚔️War and the people in it August 18, 1944

Three days at St-Lambert

Major David Currie held a village in Normandy for three days with a small force, closing an escape route for a retreating German army. He was the only Canadian awarded the Victoria Cross in the Normandy campaign. He said afterwards that he had simply been too tired to leave.

— Department of National Defence, Victoria Cross recipients

⚓The sea August 21, 1944

HMCS Alberni goes down

A torpedo from a German submarine struck the corvette HMCS Alberni in the English Channel and she sank in about thirty seconds. Fifty-nine of her crew were lost. She had spent four years escorting convoys across the Atlantic, which was the longest and least celebrated campaign Canada fought.

— Naval Museum of Manitoba

⚔️War and the people in it August 27, 1944

Across the Seine

The 3rd Canadian Infantry Division crossed the River Seine at Elbeuf as the First Canadian Army chased the German army out of Normandy. Two months earlier these same men had come ashore at Juno Beach. Ahead of them was the long, wet, unglamorous job of clearing the Channel ports.

— Department of National Defence, official history of the Canadian Army

⚔️War and the people in it August 30, 1944

Into Rouen

Patrols of the 9th Canadian Infantry Brigade pushed into Rouen as the First Canadian Army swept north from the Seine. The city had been bombed heavily and the cathedral was standing but wounded. From here the Canadians turned toward the Channel ports and the long winter in the Scheldt.

— Department of National Defence, official history of the Canadian Army

⚔️War and the people in it September 1, 1944

The Canadians come back to Dieppe

Two years and thirteen days after the raid, Canadian soldiers walked back into Dieppe, this time as liberators. No shot was fired. The Germans had already gone and the townspeople filled the windows. Later the whole division paraded through the streets for the 916 Canadians who died there in 1942.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Chronology of the Second World War

⚔️War and the people in it October 6, 1944

Canadians attack across the Leopold Canal

The 3rd Canadian Infantry Division attacked across the Leopold Canal in Belgium. This was part of the Battle of the Scheldt, fought to clear the river so that supply ships could reach the port of Antwerp. The ground was flat, wet and open, and the fighting was hard. Over the five weeks of the battle, 6,367 of the casualties were Canadians.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Battle of the Scheldt

⚔️War and the people in it October 9, 1944

An amphibious attack breaks the line at the Leopold Canal

A Canadian amphibious assault broke the German resistance at the Leopold Canal, in Belgium. It ended the deadlock that had held up the attack since 6 October, and the bridgehead was made deeper. The Battle of the Scheldt went on until 8 November. Clearing the river finally let supply ships reach the port of Antwerp.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Battle of the Scheldt

⚔️War and the people in it October 16, 1944

Canadians take Woensdrecht

The 2nd Canadian Infantry Division captured the town of Woensdrecht in the Netherlands. The town guarded the way onto South Beveland, and so the road toward the sea. On the same day Field Marshal Montgomery ordered that clearing the Scheldt come before all other work. The aim was to open the port of Antwerp so supplies could reach the Allied armies.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Battle of the Scheldt

🧭Exploration and gold October 16, 1944

The St. Roch reaches Vancouver

The police schooner St. Roch tied up in Vancouver after sailing from Halifax through the Northwest Passage. The voyage took 86 days, from 22 July to 16 October. She was the first ship to cross the passage in a single season. Her crew had already sailed the other way, reaching Halifax in 1942.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, St. Roch

⚔️War and the people in it October 24, 1944

The way onto South Beveland is opened

By this day Canadian troops had broken through and secured the narrow neck of land leading onto South Beveland, in the Netherlands. The ground there was flat, wet and open, and the defenders could see a long way. This land had to be cleared before ships could safely use the port of Antwerp.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Battle of the Scheldt

⚔️War and the people in it October 31, 1944

Canadians attack the Walcheren causeway

Canadian troops attacked along the causeway that joined South Beveland to Walcheren Island, in the Netherlands. The causeway was a narrow raised road across open water, with no cover on either side. On the same day the 2nd Canadian Division began its advance into South Beveland. Clearing the Scheldt finally let ships reach Antwerp.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Battle of the Scheldt

⚔️War and the people in it November 3, 1944

The south shore of the Scheldt is cleared

First Canadian Army finished clearing the south shore of the Scheldt in Belgium and the Netherlands. The work had taken weeks of fighting in cold, flooded fields where men often had to wade or use small boats. Clearing the river was the only way to open the port of Antwerp so that food, fuel and ammunition could reach the Allied armies.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, The Battle of the Scheldt

⚔️War and the people in it November 8, 1944

The Battle of the Scheldt ends

The last German resistance on the island of Walcheren ended, and with it the Battle of the Scheldt. The fighting had begun on 2 October. Canadian, British and Polish troops had cleared both banks of the river so that ships could reach Antwerp. Minesweepers still had to clear the water before the first convoy arrived.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, The Battle of the Scheldt

⚔️War and the people in it August 9, 1945

The last Victoria Cross

Lieutenant Robert Hampton Gray of Trail, British Columbia, flew a damaged aircraft through heavy fire to sink a Japanese warship at Onagawa Bay, and was killed doing it. He was the last Canadian to be awarded the Victoria Cross. The war ended six days later.

— Department of National Defence, Victoria Cross recipients

⚔️War and the people in it August 15, 1945

The war ends

Japan's surrender was announced and the Second World War was over. More than a million Canadians had served, out of a country of about eleven and a half million people, and more than 45,000 did not come home.

— Government of Canada

⚔️War and the people in it September 2, 1945

Canada signs the end of the war

The war ended on the deck of an American battleship in Tokyo Bay. Colonel Lawrence Cosgrave signed for Canada. More than 10,000 Canadians had served in Asia and the Pacific. Japan had agreed to stop fighting on 15 August. On this day the papers were signed, and the Second World War was over.

— Veterans Affairs Canada, Asia and the Pacific

👤People September 5, 1945

A clerk walks out of the Soviet embassy

Igor Gouzenko was a young cipher clerk at the Soviet embassy in Ottawa. On the evening of 5 September he walked out with documents hidden on him and went first to the Ottawa Journal, which turned him away. Canada granted him protection on 7 September. They showed that the Soviet Union was running a spy network inside Canada, including a member of Parliament. Many people date the beginning of the Cold War to that walk.

At 3:45 in the afternoon, a small test reactor called ZEEP went critical at Chalk River, Ontario. That means a nuclear chain reaction was running inside it for the first time. It was the first nuclear reactor to work anywhere outside the United States. ZEEP made almost no power. Scientists used it to learn how to design the bigger reactors that came later.

Talks between Ford of Canada and the United Auto Workers broke down, and ten thousand members of Local 200 walked out in Windsor, Ontario. The biggest earthquake Canada has recorded

A magnitude 8.1 earthquake struck off the coast of Haida Gwaii, in British Columbia. It is the largest recorded in Canada since records began. Cows were knocked off their feet on the islands and chimneys fell on the mainland, but the coast is thinly settled and nobody was killed.

Fire on the Noronic

The cruise ship Noronic was tied up overnight in Toronto harbour with 524 passengers aboard. At half past one in the morning a passenger saw smoke coming from a locked closet. Alouette-1 was launched on 29 September 1962. The Canadian Space Agency calls Canada the third country to design and build its own satellite, after the Soviet Union and the United States. The new government promised a referendum on Quebec sovereignty, which was held the following year.

— Bibliothèque de l'Assemblée nationale du Québec, Élection générale de 1994

⚖️Law, rights and government October 30, 1995

Quebec holds a referendum on sovereignty

Quebec held a referendum on becoming a sovereign country. The no side won with 50.58 per cent against 49.42 per cent for yes, a difference of 54,288 votes. Almost everyone who could vote did so: turnout was 93.52 per cent. A referendum is a direct vote by the people on a single question.

— Élections Québec, 1995 referendum on Québec's accession to sovereignty

💡Discoveries November 12, 1995

Chris Hadfield flies for the first time

The space shuttle Atlantis lifted off with Chris Hadfield aboard on his first flight. His job was to use the Canadarm to attach a Russian docking module to the space station Mir. He became the first Canadian to operate the Canadarm in space, and the only Canadian ever to go aboard Mir.

Read more on this

— Canadian Space Agency, Mission STS-074

🔥Disaster July 19, 1996

The Saguenay flood

Two hundred and sixty millimetres of rain fell on the Saguenay region in three days. Rivers tore through towns and about 14,000 people had to leave their homes. One small white house was left standing alone in the mud and became the photograph everyone remembers.

— Répertoire du patrimoine culturel du Québec

🏅Sport July 27, 1996

The fastest man alive

Donovan Bailey won the Olympic 100 metres at Atlanta in 9.84 seconds, a world record. He had come to Canada from Jamaica as a boy and had been working as a stockbroker while he trained. A week later he anchored the relay team to gold as well.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Donovan Bailey'; Canadian Museum of History, "Donovan Bailey and five objects that show change in athletics history"

⚖️Law, rights and government August 20, 1998

Can a province leave?

The Supreme Court answered a question no country likes to be asked. A province cannot simply declare independence on its own. But if a clear majority answers a clear question, the rest of Canada would be obliged to negotiate. Both sides of the argument found something in it.

Read more on this

— Supreme Court of Canada, Reference re Secession of Quebec, [1998] 2 SCR 217

🔥Disaster September 2, 1998

Swissair 111 goes down off Peggy's Cove

A flight from New York to Geneva caught fire in the ceiling above the cockpit. The crew turned for Halifax, but the aircraft came down in the sea about five nautical miles from Peggy's Cove, Nova Scotia. All 229 people on board died. Fishermen and people from the villages nearby put out in their own boats to search.

— Transportation Safety Board of Canada, Swissair 111 investigation report

🎭Culture and stories November 22, 1998

The painter Jack Shadbolt dies in Burnaby

Jack Shadbolt died at Burnaby, in British Columbia. He was a painter who wanted his art to speak about the nature and the culture of his province. Forests, birds and the coast return again and again in his pictures. He lived and worked in British Columbia for most of his life.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Arts timeline

👤People October 7, 1999

Adrienne Clarkson becomes governor general

Adrienne Clarkson was installed as Canada's twenty-sixth governor general. She had come to Canada from Hong Kong as a child refugee during the Second World War, and worked for many years as a broadcaster. She was the first governor general of Chinese descent. She held the office until 27 September 2005.

Read more on this

— The Governor General of Canada, Former governors general, Adrienne Clarkson

2000 – today

🔥Disaster July 14, 2000

The Pine Lake tornado

A tornado with winds of 330 kilometres an hour struck a campground at Pine Lake, Alberta, around supper time on a Friday in July. Twelve people were killed and 140 were injured. It was the first deadly tornado in Canada in thirteen years.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, disasters timeline; Environment and Climate Change Canada, Canada's Top Ten Weather Stories of 2000

⚖️Law, rights and government November 27, 2000

The Liberals win a third election in a row

Canadians voted in a federal election and the Liberal Party won for the third time in a row. Jean Chrétien stayed prime minister. He had first won in 1993 and won again in 1997. In Canada a federal election must be held at least every five years, and the prime minister may ask for one sooner.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Politics and Government timeline

💡Discoveries November 30, 2000

Marc Garneau flies to the space station

Marc Garneau lifted off from Florida aboard the shuttle Endeavour at 10:06 in the evening, Eastern time. He was the first Canadian to reach orbit a third time. During the mission he used the Canadarm to lift the first pair of large solar panels out of the cargo bay and move them into place on the International Space Station.

Read more on this

— Canadian Space Agency, Mission STS-97

👤People September 11, 2001

Gander takes in the world

After the attacks in the United States, the United States closed its airspace. Canada grounded its own flights but opened its airports to aircraft already over the Atlantic. Thirty-four airliners and four military aircraft landed at Gander, Newfoundland, carrying about 6,600 passengers and crew. The town's population was 9,651, so it grew by almost seventy per cent in a few hours. People opened schools, halls and their own homes.

— Town of Gander

🔥Disaster August 14, 2003

The lights go out

Just after four in the afternoon, the power failed across Ontario and much of the northeastern United States. About ten million Canadians were without electricity, some for two days. People remember it as unusually calm, and as the night they could see the stars over Toronto.

— CBC Archives

🔥Disaster September 29, 2003

Hurricane Juan strikes Nova Scotia

Just after midnight, Hurricane Juan came ashore between Shad Bay and Prospect, west of Halifax. It was a Category 2 storm, with steady winds of 158 kilometres an hour and gusts over 185. Eight people died. Trees came down across Halifax, hundreds of thousands of homes lost power, and it took nearly two weeks to get the lights back on.

— Environment and Climate Change Canada, Hurricane Juan Storm Summary

🏅Sport September 14, 2004

Canada wins the World Cup of Hockey

In Toronto, Canada beat Finland 3-2 in the final of the World Cup of Hockey. Joe Sakic, Scott Niedermayer and Shane Doan scored the Canadian goals. Canada won all six of its games in the tournament. Vincent Lecavalier was named the most valuable player.

Read more on this

— Hockey Canada, Team Canada beats Finland to win the World Cup of Hockey 2004

👤People September 27, 2005

Michaëlle Jean is installed as Governor General

Michaëlle Jean became the 27th Governor General of Canada. She was born in Haiti and came to Canada as a child, when her family fled a dictatorship. She had worked for years as a journalist and broadcaster. In her installation speech she spoke of Canada as a place where people can begin again in safety.

Read more on this

— The Governor General of Canada, Installation speech of Michaëlle Jean

🔥Disaster September 21, 2010

Hurricane Igor cuts off the outports

Hurricane Igor swept across Newfoundland with heavy rain and high winds. Roads and bridges washed out in nearly two hundred communities, with more than a hundred breaches in the roads. Over ninety communities were cut off, some for as long as ten days. More than a thousand members of the Canadian Armed Forces were sent to rebuild bridges and bring in water and supplies.

— Government of Newfoundland and Labrador

👤People October 1, 2010

A new governor general is sworn in on Parliament Hill

David Johnston was sworn in as Canada's twenty-eighth governor general. The ceremony was held in the Senate Chamber on Parliament Hill in Ottawa. The governor general represents the Crown in Canada, gives royal assent to new laws and presents national honours. The job usually lasts about five years.

Read more on this

— Government of Canada, Installation Ceremony of Canada's 28th Governor General

👤People August 22, 2011

Jack Layton dies

The Leader of the Official Opposition died of cancer at 61, three months after the best election result his party had ever had. He had written a letter two days earlier ending with a line the country repeated for years: my friends, love is better than anger. He was given a state funeral.

— Canadian Heritage, Government of Canada

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history November 29, 2011

The Eeyou Marine Region agreement receives assent

A land claims agreement for the Eeyou Marine Region received assent. It recognises the rights and the title of the Cree of Eeyou Istchee to the coast of northern Quebec and to the islands offshore. Talks on the claim had begun in 1974. The area crosses a provincial boundary, which is unusual for an agreement of this kind.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Colonization and Immigration timeline

🔥Disaster July 6, 2013

Lac-Mégantic

A runaway train carrying seventy-two tank cars of crude oil rolled down a slope and exploded in the middle of Lac-Mégantic, Quebec, in the early hours of the morning. Forty-seven people were killed and the centre of the town was destroyed. It changed how Canada moves oil by rail.

— Canadian Encyclopedia, 'Lac-Mégantic Rail Disaster'; Transportation Safety Board of Canada, Railway Investigation R13D0054 (Lac-Mégantic runaway train and derailment)

💡Discoveries October 6, 2015

A Canadian shares the Nobel Prize in Physics

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced that Arthur McDonald of Queen's University had won a share of the Nobel Prize in Physics. He led the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory, a laboratory two kilometres underground in a nickel mine in Ontario. His team showed that neutrinos, tiny particles from the Sun, change form as they travel, which means they have mass.

Read more on this

— Queen's University, Department of Physics, Engineering Physics and Astronomy

⚖️Law, rights and government October 19, 2015

Canadians elect a new Parliament

Canada held its forty-second general election. The Liberal Party won a majority of the seats in the House of Commons, and Justin Trudeau became prime minister. Voters choose one member of Parliament in each riding, and the party with the most seats usually forms the government. Elections Canada runs the vote and publishes every result.

Read more on this

— Elections Canada, General Election, October 19, 2015

⚖️Law, rights and government November 4, 2015

A cabinet with as many women as men

Justin Trudeau and his ministers were sworn in as Canada's 29th Ministry. For the first time in Canadian history the cabinet had the same number of women as men. A ministry is the team of ministers who run the departments of government. Each minister answers to Parliament for the work of a department.

Read more on this

— Prime Minister of Canada, Statement following the swearing-in of the 29th Ministry

👤People November 22, 2015

The filmmaker Gil Cardinal dies in Edmonton

Gil Cardinal died in Edmonton at the age of 65. He was a Métis director from Alberta and one of the first Indigenous filmmakers in Canada whose films reached a wide audience at home and abroad. He made documentaries and dramas, many of them about Métis and First Nations families and history.

Read more on this

— Canadian Encyclopedia, Indigenous Peoples timeline

🏅Sport August 11, 2016

Penny Oleksiak

At sixteen, Penny Oleksiak won the 100 metre freestyle in Rio and became the first Canadian to win four medals at a single Summer Games. She had taken up competitive swimming only a few years before, because her sister was doing it.

— Canadian Olympic Committee

🏅Sport August 12, 2016

Twice golden

Rosie MacLennan won trampoline gold in Rio, four years after winning it in London. She became the first Canadian ever to successfully defend an individual Olympic title. She had competed in London with a concussion nobody knew about.

— Canadian Olympic Committee

⚖️Law, rights and government October 17, 2018

A new cannabis law comes into force

The Cannabis Act came into force across Canada. Adults could buy a limited amount of cannabis from sellers licensed by their province or territory. The federal minimum age was set at 18, and provinces and territories could set it higher; several chose 19. Selling or giving cannabis to anyone under 18 carries heavy penalties.

— Health Canada, Backgrounder: The Cannabis Act, The Facts

👤People November 19, 2018

Viola Desmond appears on the ten dollar bill

The Bank of Canada put a new ten dollar note into circulation. It shows Viola Desmond, a businesswoman from Nova Scotia who in 1946 refused to leave a whites-only part of a movie theatre. She was the first Canadian woman shown alone on a Canadian bank note. It was also the first Canadian note printed the tall way instead of the wide way.

Read more on this

— Bank of Canada, New vertical $10 bank note featuring iconic Canadian Viola Desmond now in circulation

🔥Disaster September 7, 2019

Dorian comes ashore near Halifax

At 7:15 in the evening, Hurricane Dorian made landfall at Sambro Creek, just south of Halifax. An hour earlier it had been declared post-tropical, but it was still as strong as a category 1 to category 2 hurricane. Overnight it crossed northern Nova Scotia and passed the eastern tip of Prince Edward Island. The next day it crossed the Northern Peninsula of Newfoundland before moving out to sea.

— Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2019 tropical cyclone season summary

⚖️Law, rights and government October 21, 2019

A minority government is elected

Canada held its forty-third general election. The Liberal Party won the most seats but not a majority, so it formed a minority government and needed help from other parties to pass laws. A minority government lasts only as long as it keeps the confidence of the House of Commons. Elections Canada publishes the result for every riding.

Read more on this

— Elections Canada, 43rd General Election, October 21, 2019

🪶Treaties and Indigenous history July 12, 2021

Kuper Island

The Penelakut Tribe announced that more than 160 unmarked graves had been found at the site of the former Kuper Island residential school, off Vancouver Island. It came in a summer of similar findings across the country. The children had been sent there by law, and their families had been told very little.

Read more on this

— Penelakut Tribe announcement, July 2021; Canadian Encyclopedia, British Columbia timeline

🏅Sport August 5, 2021

Decathlon gold

Damian Warner of London, Ontario won the Olympic decathlon in Tokyo with an Olympic record. He had trained through the winter in an unheated hockey arena, because the pandemic had closed everything else.

— Canadian Olympic Committee

🏅Sport August 6, 2021

Soccer gold in Tokyo

Canada's women beat Sweden on penalties to win the country's first Olympic gold in soccer. The team had taken bronze twice before and had been asked about it every time. They had said they wanted to change the colour of the medal, and they did.

— Canadian Olympic Committee

👤People September 30, 2021

The first Truth and Reconciliation Day

Canada held the first National Day for Truth and Reconciliation. The day honours the children who died at residential schools, the survivors, and their families and communities. It falls on the same date as Orange Shirt Day, and many people wear orange. It is now a federal statutory holiday.

— Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada

👤People September 19, 2022

A day of mourning for the Queen

Queen Elizabeth II had been Canada's head of state for seventy years. Canada held a one-time National Day of Mourning on the day of her state funeral in London. A national ceremony was held at Christ Church Cathedral in Ottawa and shown on television.

— Department of National Defence

About this quiz

The Canada Diary is a running record of this country, one short note at a time. It opens on today's date, so the page is different every morning. From there you can look back through history, month by month or era by era, or forward to dates that have not arrived yet.

Every note names where it came from. Most rest on Parks Canada, Veterans Affairs Canada, Library and Archives Canada, the Justice Laws website or a provincial government; the rest on museums, universities and The Canadian Encyclopedia, which is a charity rather than a government body. Nothing here is invented.

Where no official record gives an exact day, the day is left empty rather than guessed. Saying plainly what is not known is the same rule the driving pages follow, and it is the reason this site can be trusted on the things it does know.

The look of a note changes with its age. The oldest notes sit on aged paper in a handwritten face; the newest sit on the site's ordinary white card.
