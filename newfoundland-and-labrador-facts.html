Newfoundland and Labrador, from the fact box to the hard questions

An island and a piece of the mainland, the last province to join Canada, a meat-eating flower, and the only clock in the country set to a half hour. Every figure comes from a government source, and they are all listed at the bottom.

The half hour is written into provincial law. The Standard Time Act says time in the province “shall be reckoned as 3 1/2 hours later than Greenwich mean solar time”, and that this is to be known as Newfoundland Standard Time. Nowhere else in Canada is a clock set to a half hour. When the news says “9:30 in Newfoundland”, that is not a joke — it is the statute.

Where Newfoundland and Labrador is

Newfoundland and Labrador is Canada's easternmost province, and it is in two pieces. Newfoundland is a large island in the Atlantic. Labrador is on the mainland, north of Quebec and much larger than the island, with far fewer people.

The federal government's Discover Canada guide notes that the province is the most easterly point in North America and has its own time zone. The capital, St. John's, is on the island's east coast, closer to Ireland than to Winnipeg.

What the land is like

The island is rock, bog and stunted forest, wrapped in a coastline so folded that small communities were reachable only by boat for most of their history. Labrador is bigger, emptier and wilder: the Torngat Mountains in the far north are among the highest land in eastern Canada, and the interior is a plateau of lakes and rivers.

Icebergs drift down the coast in spring, carried on the Labrador Current. They are not local: they break off glaciers in Greenland and take a year or more to arrive.

How big is that, really?

  • Newfoundland and Labrador is bigger than New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island put together, three times over.
  • About 8% of the province is fresh water — most of it in Labrador, on the plateau.

How people make a living

For four hundred years the answer was cod. The fishery built every community on the island's coast, and its collapse in 1992 ended a way of life for tens of thousands of people. What replaced it is different work in different places: offshore oil, mining in Labrador, hydroelectricity, shellfish rather than cod, and tourism.

Labrador's iron ore and its rivers matter to the whole country. The power stations on the Churchill River are among the largest in Canada, and the arrangements for selling that electricity have been argued about for over fifty years.

How Newfoundland and Labrador became a province

The Innu and the Inuit have lived in Labrador for thousands of years, and the Beothuk lived on the island of Newfoundland. The Mi'kmaq are also long established on the island. Norse sailors reached the northern tip of Newfoundland around a thousand years ago — the only confirmed Norse site in North America is there.

Newfoundland was a British colony, then a self-governing dominion — effectively its own country, with its own parliament and its own money. It went broke in the 1930s and gave up self-government. After the Second World War, two referendums were held on what to do next, and the second one narrowly chose joining Canada.

It joined on 31 March 1949, as the tenth province — the last one. In 2001 the province's name was formally changed from Newfoundland to Newfoundland and Labrador, so that the mainland half was named as well.

Things most people do not know

  • The provincial flower eats insects. The pitcher plant, adopted in 1954, traps them in a pool of liquid at the bottom of its leaves.
  • The province has two official animals, both named after it: the Newfoundland dog and the Newfoundland pony.
  • Its gemstone, labradorite, is named after Labrador. It flashes blue and green when you turn it in the light.
  • Before 1949 Newfoundland was not part of Canada at all. It was a self-governing dominion with its own parliament, its own stamps and its own currency.
  • The province's name gained its second half in 2001. Before that, Labrador — the larger part by land — was not in the name at all.
  • The half-hour clock is set by a provincial statute, not by habit. Time zones are decided by provinces and territories, not by the federal government.

Who decides what time it is

The federal government's own time page states plainly that daylight saving time in Canada is determined by provincial legislation, and that time zones and daylight saving have usually been regulated by provincial and territorial governments. That is why one province can keep a half hour, another can stop changing its clocks, and a third can change them — with no federal rule overriding any of it.

Newfoundland's own statute is worth reading carefully, because it uses old wording: it says time is “reckoned as 3 1/2 hours later than Greenwich mean solar time”. That phrasing reads backwards to a modern reader used to UTC offsets, so this page quotes it rather than translating it into a plus or minus that the statute does not use.

Two referendums, and a country that chose to stop being one

Very few places have voted themselves out of independence. Newfoundland had responsible government from 1855 and dominion status alongside Canada, Australia and New Zealand. The Great Depression and war debt broke its finances, and in 1934 it handed government back to a British-appointed commission.

The vote that followed the war was close, and the argument it settled — whether a small place is better off alone or inside a bigger one — has never stopped being debated there. Understanding that is a better way into 1949 than memorising the date.
