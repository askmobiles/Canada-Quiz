Do Young Canadians Want to Farm?

The honest answer is that we do not know what they want, but we know exactly what they are doing — and there is a gap in the numbers big enough to see from space.

Three numbers, side by side

At the 2021 Census of Agriculture, 60.5 percent of Canadian farm operators were 55 or older. Fewer than one in ten — 8.6 percent — were under 35. And only 12.0 percent of farms had a written succession plan.

Put those together and you get the shape of the problem. Three in five people running Canadian farms are within a decade or so of when most people stop working, and almost nine in ten of those farms have nothing written down about what happens next.

None of these are new trends, but they are accelerating. The average operator was 49.9 years old in 2001 and 56.0 in 2021. The share aged 55 and over rose six percentage points in the five years to 2021 alone, the fastest jump in the series. Meanwhile the share under 35 fell, from 9.1 percent to 8.6.

Before blaming young people, look at the price

It is easy to reach for a story about young people not wanting hard work. The numbers point somewhere far more ordinary: the cost of getting in.

Statistics Canada valued all Canadian farmland at 713.3 billion dollars in 2024, against 374.0 billion in 2016. That is a rise of about ninety-one percent in eight years, over a period when the number of farms was falling. Per acre in 2024, values ranged from 2,647 dollars in Saskatchewan to 20,782 dollars in Ontario.

Farm Credit Canada, the federal Crown corporation that lends to farmers, reported that national farmland values rose a further 9.3 percent in 2025, led by Manitoba at 12.2 percent and Alberta at 11.4. Its figures are appraisal-based rather than a statistical survey, but it is the standard national series.

Now do the arithmetic that a twenty-five-year-old has to do

The average Canadian farm is 809 acres. At Ontario's 2024 value that is roughly seventeen million dollars of land — before a tractor, a barn, seed, fuel or a single hour of labour. In Saskatchewan the same acreage is closer to two million.

Against that, the help available to a new entrant is a Canadian Agricultural Loans Act guarantee capped at five hundred thousand dollars, and a Farm Credit Canada Young Farmer Loan capped at two million for producers under forty. Those are real and useful programmes. They are also not the same order of magnitude as the asset.

This is why succession matters so much more in farming than in most industries. For most young farmers the realistic route in is inheriting or buying into a family operation — which brings us back to the 88 percent of farms with nothing written down.

Who is doing the work in the meantime

Canadian agriculture employed 278,373 people in 2022, and nearly half of those positions were seasonal. Temporary foreign workers numbered 64,660 in 2022, up six percent in a year, making up 23.2 percent of all agricultural employees — most from Mexico, Guatemala and Jamaica.

Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada puts the figure at just over seventy thousand temporary foreign workers in primary agriculture by 2023, a thirty percent increase since 2017. Whatever young Canadians want, the work is currently being done in large part by people who come here for a season.

What the numbers do not tell you

There is no Statistics Canada survey asking young Canadians whether they would like to farm, so anybody who tells you that young people do or do not want to be farmers is offering an opinion. What the data shows is participation, not desire — and participation is falling while the price of entry rises.

There are two hopeful signs in the same data. Written succession plans rose from 8.4 percent to 12.0 percent in five years, an increase of nearly seven thousand farms. And the share of operators who are women keeps climbing, reaching 30.4 percent in 2021. Neither is a solution, but both are movement.

The 2026 Census of Agriculture began collecting in May 2026. When it reports, these will be the first numbers worth looking at.

Where these facts come from

Age, succession and operator figures come from the 2021 Census of Agriculture and Statistics Canada's own commentary on it. Farmland values come from Statistics Canada and from Farm Credit Canada, a federal Crown corporation. Labour figures come from Statistics Canada and Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada.

This page gives shares rather than head counts for operators under 35, because Statistics Canada publishes the share and not the count. And it does not use the often-quoted farm job vacancy number, because that figure covers agriculture, forestry, fishing and hunting together rather than farming alone.

Common questions

How old is the average Canadian farmer?

56.0 years at the 2021 census, up from 55.0 in 2016 and 49.9 in 2001. 60.5% of operators were 55 or older.

How many young farmers are there in Canada?

8.6% of farm operators were under 35 in 2021, down from 9.1% in 2016. Statistics Canada publishes the share rather than a head count.

Why don't more young people go into farming?

No survey asks them, so anyone claiming to know their wishes is guessing. What the data shows is cost: an average 809-acre farm at Ontario's 2024 land values is roughly $17 million before any equipment, against a maximum federal loan guarantee of $500,000.

What loans exist for young farmers?

The Canadian Agricultural Loans Act guarantees 95% of a lender's net loss up to $500,000 per farmer, and Farm Credit Canada offers a Young Farmer Loan of up to $2 million for producers under 40 with no loan processing fees.
