Prince Edward Island, from the fact box to the hard questions

Canada's smallest province, its most crowded one, a 12.9-kilometre bridge, and the room where Confederation was first discussed. Every figure comes from a government source, and they are all listed at the bottom.

The smallest province is also the most crowded one. Take the population and the land area from the two tables at the bottom of this page and work it out: Prince Edward Island has roughly 32 people for every square kilometre. Ontario, with eighty-nine times as many people, has about 18. Canada as a whole has about 5. That arithmetic is ours, not a published figure — but the two numbers it uses are both official, and you can check it yourself.

Where Prince Edward Island is

Prince Edward Island sits in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, off the coasts of New Brunswick and Nova Scotia. It is the only province that is entirely an island, and it is the only one you cannot drive to without either a bridge or a boat.

Since 1997 there has been a bridge: the Confederation Bridge, 12.9 kilometres long, connecting the island to New Brunswick. Its operator says it is the longest bridge in the world crossing ice-covered water.

What the land is like

The island is gentle: low rolling hills, no mountains, and famous red soil. The red comes from iron in the sandstone underneath, the same reason the beaches and the dirt roads are that colour.

Statistics Canada records no fresh water area for Prince Edward Island at all. There are streams and ponds, but nothing large enough to register on a national table — which is itself a fact worth putting in a project.

How big is that, really?

  • You could fit Prince Edward Island inside Manitoba more than one hundred times, and inside Nunavut more than three hundred and sixty times.
  • Ten of the other twelve provinces and territories hold more fresh water than Prince Edward Island has land. Only Nova Scotia and New Brunswick hold less.

How people make a living

Farming, fishing and tourism, in roughly that order of tradition. Potatoes are the crop the island is known for and they are grown at a scale that supplies far beyond the province. Lobster and shellfish come off the coast, and the summer brings visitors in numbers many times the resident population.

There is also a story in the coat of arms: it is supported by two silver foxes, because fur farming was once a major industry here.

How Prince Edward Island became a province

The island is Epekwitk in Mi'kmaq, and the Mi'kmaq have lived here for thousands of years. The French called it Île Saint-Jean and settled Acadian families here; Britain took it in 1763 and renamed it after a son of King George III.

In September 1864, Province House in Charlottetown hosted the first conference on joining the colonies together. Parks Canada's words: delegates from Prince Edward Island, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Canada met in the legislative council chamber to begin discussions, which led to Confederation in 1867.

Here is the twist that makes this the island's best story: Prince Edward Island hosted the meeting that created Canada and then did not join for six more years. It became the seventh province on 1 July 1873.

Things most people do not know

  • The island hosted the 1864 conference that started Confederation and then waited until 1873 to join Canada.
  • Its coat of arms is held up by two silver foxes, because fur farming was once a major island industry.
  • Statistics Canada lists no fresh water area for Prince Edward Island — the only province or territory with none on the national table.
  • The official tree, the red oak, was adopted in 1905 — earlier than most provinces chose any tree at all.
  • The provincial bird is the blue jay, a cousin of Steller's jay, which is British Columbia's bird at the other end of the country.
  • The island has an official soil, the Charlottetown series, adopted the same year as New Brunswick's — 1997.

Going deeper

This part is for older students and for teachers. Same material, with the complications left in.

Why a bridge and not a tunnel, and why it took until 1997

The Northumberland Strait freezes. Any fixed link had to survive sheets of moving sea ice grinding against it every winter for decades — which is why the bridge's own operator describes it as the longest in the world crossing ice-covered water, and why the piers are shaped to break ice rather than resist it.

A fixed link had been discussed since the 1800s. Islanders were genuinely divided about it, and not only over engineering: a bridge changes what it means to live on an island. A plebiscite was held before it was built.

What “most crowded province” does and does not mean

Density is people divided by area, and it hides as much as it shows. Prince Edward Island comes out as Canada's most densely populated province, but nobody would call it crowded — it has no city over about 40,000 people. What the number actually says is that the island has almost no empty land: nearly all of it is farmed or lived on.

Compare Ontario, where the density figure is dragged down by an enormous, almost unpopulated north. The lesson for a school project: an average over a whole province can describe a place that does not exist anywhere in it.
