🤝 Canada and the United States: 170 Years of Trade, Fights and Deals

Canada has spent 170 years torn between wanting the American market and fearing American power. Every single episode — 1866, 1911, 1988, 2025 — mixed money with sovereignty. Here is the whole story, up to what is happening this month.

Last checked 9 August 2026 — this page covers a fast-moving situation

Where things stand today. On 19 August 2026, ten days from now, a United States tariff of 50 percent is scheduled to hit roughly 18 to 20 billion dollars of Canadian goods — vehicles, alcohol and dairy. It is signed but not yet in force, and talks are under way in Washington. What makes it different from everything before is that CUSMA, the free trade agreement, does not shield goods from it. Energy, potash, fish and critical minerals are exempt.

1854: free trade, and the annexation scare that caused it

Before 1854 the British colonies traded inside the British Empire on preferential terms. Then Britain went free trade — repealing the Corn Laws in 1846 and the Navigation Acts in 1849 — and the colonies lost their guaranteed market overnight. The shock was real enough that in 1849 a group of prominent Montreal businessmen signed an Annexation Manifesto proposing that Canada join the United States.

The answer was the Reciprocity Treaty, signed in Washington in early June 1854 by Lord Elgin and the American Secretary of State. It allowed free trade in natural products — grain, timber, coal, livestock, fish — but not manufactured goods. The hinge of the deal was fish: American fishermen got access to the Atlantic coastal fisheries, and the trade terms were partly the price of settling that dispute.

The Americans cancelled it in 1866. Northern politicians were angry at British sympathy for the Confederacy during the Civil War, the Treasury needed tariff revenue, and some in Washington calculated that squeezing the colonies economically would push them into the Union. It had the opposite effect. Losing the American market became one of the strongest arguments for Confederation in 1867: if the north-south trade was gone, build an east-west country instead.

1879: the National Policy builds a wall

Sir John A. Macdonald campaigned on it in 1878, won, and put it in the 1879 budget. Three parts: protective tariffs on imported manufactured goods, a transcontinental railway, and western settlement. Duties on protected manufactures typically landed in the 20 to 30 percent range. The framework lasted, in some form, into the 1950s, and its logic was not fully reversed until 1989. Economists generally argue it raised prices and hurt efficiency; historians more often defend it as nation-building. Both readings have a case.

1911: the election Canada fought over free trade — and free trade lost

Wilfrid Laurier negotiated a reciprocity agreement with President Taft. The American Congress passed it. Canada refused. The Conservatives under Robert Borden won the election of 21 September 1911 with 134 seats to 87, ending fifteen years of Liberal government.

What is striking is that the campaign was barely about economics. It was about loyalty and sovereignty — that free trade would loosen imperial ties and Americanise Canada. Rudyard Kipling warned that Canada risked losing her own soul. The slogan was no truck nor trade with the Yankees. And American politicians helped: the Speaker of the House of Representatives said publicly that he looked forward to the day the American flag flew over all of North America. That single remark was worth seats to the Conservatives.

1935 and 1965: quiet progress

The Depression and the American Smoot-Hawley tariff of 1930 had set off a spiral of retaliation. Mackenzie King and Roosevelt signed a modest agreement on 15 November 1935 to start unwinding it, followed by a broader one in 1938. In 1948 both countries joined the GATT and tariff-cutting moved into the multilateral system. A footnote worth knowing: also in 1948 the two countries secretly negotiated a full free trade deal, and Mackenzie King personally killed it at the last minute, fearing the politics.

The Auto Pact of January 1965, signed by Lester Pearson and Lyndon Johnson, was not free trade — it was managed trade, and the distinction matters. Tariffs on vehicles came off, but only if the big carmakers kept building in Canada: production could not fall below 1964 levels, and they had to build roughly as many vehicles here as they sold here. The effect was dramatic. Vehicles built in Canada and shipped to the United States went from 7 percent in 1964 to 60 percent by 1968, and cars overtook pulp and paper as Canada's biggest industry. The weak spot was parts: between 1965 and 1982 Canada ran a 28 billion dollar surplus in assembled vehicles against a 40.5 billion dollar deficit in parts. The World Trade Organization eventually ruled the Canadian safeguards illegal, and the Auto Pact was terminated in February 2001.

1988: the free trade election

A royal commission chaired by a former Liberal finance minister recommended free trade with the United States in 1985, calling it a leap of faith. That a Liberal commission said it gave Brian Mulroney the cover he needed. Negotiations ran through 1986 and 1987 and nearly collapsed over dispute settlement — Canada's central demand was a panel system that could review American anti-dumping duties. The agreement was signed on 2 January 1988.

Then the Liberal-dominated Senate refused to pass it and demanded an election first. The campaign of autumn 1988 was one of the most intense in Canadian history. John Turner accused Mulroney of selling out the country, and a week after the leaders' debates the Liberals were ahead.

The detail everyone forgets. The Progressive Conservatives won 169 seats and free trade went ahead — but a majority of Canadians voted for the two parties opposing it. The Liberals and the NDP together outpolled the Conservatives; the anti-free-trade vote simply split between them, and our voting system did the rest. The agreement came into force on 1 January 1989.

1994 to 2020: NAFTA, then CUSMA

NAFTA added Mexico and came into force on 1 January 1994. It kept the panel system Canada had fought for and added investor-state dispute settlement, which allowed companies to sue governments — a provision Canada was sued under more than most.

CUSMA replaced it on 1 July 2020. Cars needed 75 percent North American content instead of 62.5, and a new rule required 40 to 45 percent of a vehicle to be built by workers earning at least 16 US dollars an hour. Canada kept supply management in dairy but gave American producers access to roughly 3.5 to 3.9 percent of the Canadian market. Investor-state dispute settlement between Canada and the United States was removed. And a sunset clause was added: the agreement runs sixteen years, to 1 July 2036, with a compulsory joint review at year six. That review is the reason 2026 matters.

Softwood lumber: the fight that never ends

This one has run more or less continuously since 1982, and the cause is structural. Most Canadian timber grows on Crown land, and governments set the fee for cutting it. Most American timber is on private land and sold at auction. American producers call the Canadian fee a subsidy; Canada disagrees and has repeatedly won at trade panels. There have been five rounds, three negotiated settlements, and in 2006 the United States returned about four billion of the five billion dollars it had collected. In 2026 the combined duty for a typical Canadian producer sits around 25 percent, with another 10 percent on top from a separate national-security tariff imposed in October 2025.

2025: elbows up

In November 2024, before taking office, the incoming American president threatened 25 percent tariffs on Canada and Mexico, tied to fentanyl and migration. He also began describing Canada as a prospective fifty-first state. It was the annexation talk, more than the tariffs, that changed Canadian public opinion.

Elbows up is a hockey phrase associated with Gordie Howe: stand your ground and push back. Mike Myers wore an Elbows Up shirt on Saturday Night Live in early 2025, Carney adopted the posture, and it became the shorthand for the Canadian response — boycotts of American goods, provinces pulling American alcohol off liquor store shelves, a collapse in travel bookings to the United States, and Buy Canadian campaigns everywhere.

2026: the Supreme Court steps in

On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court of the United States ruled six to three that the emergency law the president had used did not authorise him to impose tariffs at all. Congress had never given that power. The decision struck down the entire 25 and 35 percent structure on Canada. Tariffs imposed under national-security law survived — steel, aluminium, autos, copper, lumber, furniture.

What followed was a search for other legal authorities. A temporary 10 percent global surcharge was proclaimed in February, struck down by a trade court in May, and expired on its own in July. A 10 percent tariff tied to forced-labour rules took effect on 24 July, with CUSMA goods exempt. And then on 20 July the administration reached for Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — a provision from the Smoot-Hawley era that had never once been used in almost a century. That is the 50 percent measure scheduled for 19 August.

Meanwhile the compulsory CUSMA review took place on 1 July 2026, and the United States declined to renew the agreement in its current form. Canada and Mexico both wanted to extend. This does not end CUSMA — it remains fully in force, and the expiry date is still 1 July 2036. What it does is trigger a joint review every year from now until then. All three countries can still agree to extend at any time.

That last block is why energy was left off the August 2026 tariff list, and it is Canada's main source of leverage. Note also the first number moving: the share of Canadian exports going south has fallen to near a multi-decade low. Whatever else the tariffs achieved, they made Canada start selling elsewhere.

What to take away

The pattern is remarkably steady. Canada opens to the American market when it needs growth, then pulls back when openness starts to feel like a threat to independence, and every single time the argument is fought in the language of sovereignty rather than economics. 1866, 1911, 1988 and 2025 are the same argument in different clothes. What is new in 2026 is that an American court has limited the president's tariff powers, and that a measure has been designed specifically to punch through the free trade agreement that had been Canada's shield.
