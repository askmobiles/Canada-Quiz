What these exercises are actually training

Memory Chain is a version of the n-back task. A leaf lights up somewhere on the grid every couple of seconds and you say whether it matches the one from a few steps back. It is hard because you have to keep updating what you are holding in mind rather than just holding it still.

Word Trap is the Stroop task, first published by John Ridley Stroop in 1935. Reading is so automatic in a fluent reader that the word fights the colour, and the small delay while you push the reading aside is the thing being measured.

Number Span is digit span, one of the oldest measures in psychology. Forward span is straight short-term memory. Backward span asks you to hold the digits and rearrange them at the same time, which is why almost everyone scores lower on it.

Switch Trail is based on Trail Making Test B: alternate 1, A, 2, B, 3, C. The cost of jumping between two sequences is called the switch cost, and it is the reason doing two things at once feels slower than doing them one after the other.

An honest note

Practising a brain game reliably makes you better at that game. Whether it makes you sharper at anything else is much less certain — the research on transfer to everyday thinking is mixed at best, and the large commercial claims made for brain training in the past have not held up well. Play these because they are a good few minutes, not because a website promised you a bigger brain. If you want something with better evidence behind it, sleep, exercise, and learning a genuinely new skill all have a stronger record than any puzzle.

These exercises are not a medical or cognitive assessment. If you are worried about memory or concentration for yourself or someone in your family, that is a conversation for a doctor, not a website.

Common questions

Do brain training games really make you smarter?

Practice reliably makes you better at the exercise you practised. Whether that carries over to everyday thinking is much less certain, and the evidence for transfer is mixed. Play these because a few focused minutes are enjoyable, not because a website promised more.

What is the n-back exercise?

Something lights up one after another, and you say whether the current one matches the one from n steps earlier. It is demanding because you have to keep updating what you hold in mind, not simply hold it.

Why is it so hard to name the ink colour instead of the word?

Reading is automatic for a fluent reader, so the word is understood before you choose to look at the colour. The pause while you push the reading aside is the Stroop effect, described by John Ridley Stroop in 1935.
