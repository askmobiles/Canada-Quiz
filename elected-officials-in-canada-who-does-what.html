🗳️ Who does what: every elected office in Canada

Canadians vote for people at four or five levels: Parliament, the province or territory, the city or town, the school board, and in many First Nations, chief and council. Here is what each of them is responsible for, what they do not do, and who to contact about what.

Last checked 16 October 2026

Jump to: At a glance · Federal · Provinces and territories · Cities and towns · School trustees · First Nations · Not elected · Who to contact

A surprise for many people: nobody in Canada votes directly for the Prime Minister or a premier. You vote for one person to represent your area. The leader who can keep the support of most of those elected members becomes Prime Minister or premier.

Federal: Parliament in Ottawa

Member of Parliament (MP)

Canada is divided into 343 electoral districts, also called ridings. Each one elects one Member of Parliament to the House of Commons. To be a candidate you must be a Canadian citizen and at least 18 years old on election day.

What an MP does:

  • Represents everyone in the riding, including people who voted for someone else
  • Debates and votes on bills, which become federal laws
  • Votes on taxes and on how the government spends money
  • Works on committees that study bills and question experts
  • If in opposition, asks the government questions and holds it to account
  • Helps people in the riding with federal matters such as passports, Employment Insurance, pensions and immigration files

How long: federal elections are set by law for the third Monday in October in the fourth calendar year after the last one. An election can come earlier, but never more than five years after the previous one.

Prime Minister of Canada

After an election, the Governor General asks the leader who can keep the support of the House of Commons to form the government. That is normally the leader of the party with the most seats. The Prime Minister is almost always an elected MP, and keeps the job only as long as the government keeps the confidence of the House.

  • Leads the federal government and chairs Cabinet meetings
  • Chooses the ministers and gives each one a department
  • Sets the government's main goals and speaks for Canada abroad
  • Answers questions in the House of Commons

What the Prime Minister does not do: make laws alone. Every bill must pass the House of Commons and the Senate and receive royal assent. The Prime Minister is the head of government, not the head of state; the King is the head of state.

Federal Cabinet ministers

Ministers are chosen by the Prime Minister, usually from the elected MPs of the governing party, and formally appointed by the Governor General. Each minister is responsible for a department, such as Health, Finance or Immigration, and answers for it in the House. Ministers also share responsibility for every Cabinet decision: in public they support it, even if they argued against it in private.

Provinces and territories

MLA, MPP, MNA or MHA

Each province and territory elects its own legislature. The members have different titles in different places, but the same job:

WhereTitle
In OntarioMPP, Member of Provincial Parliament
In QuebecMNA, Member of the National Assembly
In Newfoundland and LabradorMHA, Member of the House of Assembly
In all other provinces and the territoriesMLA, Member of the Legislative Assembly
  • Represents the people of the riding at the provincial or territorial level
  • Debates, amends and votes on provincial or territorial laws
  • Holds the government to account, especially when in opposition
  • Helps people with provincial matters such as health cards, driver's licences, schools and highways

Most provinces have fixed-date election laws, usually every four years, but early elections still happen. The Charter of Rights and Freedoms says no legislature may go more than five years without an election.

Premier of a province or territory

In each province, the premier has a role much like the Prime Minister's: the premier is an elected member who leads the party that holds the confidence of the legislature, chooses the provincial ministers and leads the provincial government.

No parties in two territories. The Northwest Territories and Nunavut use consensus government. Every member is elected as an independent. After the election, the members themselves choose the premier and the ministers by secret ballot.

Provincial ministers

Provincial ministers work the same way as federal ones. The premier chooses them, they form the Executive Council (the provincial Cabinet), and each runs a department such as health, education or transportation.

Cities and towns

Municipalities are created by the provinces, so their rules come from provincial law and differ from province to province. The examples below quote Ontario's Municipal Act, 2001; other provinces have similar laws. Municipal terms are usually four years.

Mayor, reeve or warden: the head of council

The head of council has different titles: mayor in a city or town, reeve in some townships, warden in a county. Under section 225 of Ontario's Municipal Act, the head of council's role is to:

  • act as chief executive officer of the municipality
  • chair council meetings so its business is done efficiently
  • provide leadership to the council
  • represent the municipality at official functions

The mayor usually has one vote on council, like every councillor. In Ontario, many mayors now also have strong mayor powers: they can hire the chief administrative officer, propose the budget and veto some council decisions. Council can override a veto with a two-thirds vote.

Councillors

Ontario's Municipal Act (section 224) says the role of council is to:

  • represent the public and consider the well-being of the municipality
  • develop and evaluate the municipality's policies and programs
  • decide which services the municipality provides
  • make sure the municipality is run accountably and openly
  • keep the municipality's finances sound

What a councillor does not do: run the city day to day. Council decides; municipal staff carry it out. A councillor cannot personally order a pothole fixed or a permit approved, but can pass your concern to the right department and raise it at council.

In places with two tiers, such as Ontario's regions and counties, some councillors also sit on the upper-tier council. Since 2026 several Ontario regional chairs are appointed by the province instead of elected.

School trustees

School trustees sit on the board that runs the public schools in an area. In most provinces they are elected on the same day as the municipal council. Ontario's Education Act (section 218.1) says each trustee must, in short:

  • help the board carry out its duties
  • attend and take part in board meetings
  • consult parents, students and supporters on the board's multi-year plan
  • bring their concerns to the board
  • support a board decision once it is passed, even after voting against it
  • leave day-to-day management to staff, through the director of education
  • keep the focus on student achievement and well-being

What a trustee does not do: run a school, hire or discipline teachers, or set the curriculum, which comes from the province. Trustees represent all the students in their area, not one school.

  • Ontario: trustees are elected with the municipal council for a four-year term. In 2026 the province changed how its boards are run and gave the director of education a bigger role as the board's chief executive officer.
  • British Columbia: trustees are elected every four years, on the third Saturday in October, with the local elections.
  • Quebec: since 2020, French-language schools are run by school service centres, which have no general election. English-language school boards still elect commissioners every four years, on the first Sunday in November.

First Nations chief and council

Chief and council are responsible for making decisions on behalf of the First Nation and its members, such as housing, local services, band membership and community programs. First Nations choose their leaders in different ways:

  • under the Indian Act, with two-year terms
  • under the First Nations Elections Act, which a First Nation can choose to use, with four-year terms
  • under the community's own election code
  • under a self-government agreement and constitution

People often think these are elected, but they are not

  • Members of the Senate: 105 of them, appointed by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister. They serve until age 75.
  • The Governor General: the King's representative in Canada, appointed by the King on the advice of the Prime Minister, usually for about five years.
  • Lieutenant governors: the King's representatives in the provinces, appointed by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister. The territories have commissioners instead.
  • Cabinet ministers: elected as members, but appointed to Cabinet.
  • Judges: always appointed in Canada, never elected.
  • City managers, police chiefs and school principals: hired staff, not elected officials.

Health care, hospitals, driver's licences, highways, school funding, minimum wageMLA, MPP, MNA or MHA

Some things are shared. Immigration and agriculture are shared by the federal and provincial governments, and policing can be federal, provincial or municipal depending on where you live.
