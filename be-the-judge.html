⚖️ Be the Judge

You are a judge in a busy Canadian courthouse. Twelve cases come before you. Fairness, the law, the clock and the public's trust in the courts all pull against each other.

📖 How to play

Each case is brought to you by someone in the courtroom. Pick one of three answers. Every answer moves four things: fairness to the people in front of you, time (the court's waiting list), public confidence in the courts, and the law — getting it legally right. If any one reaches zero, your time on the bench ends early. At the end you will see which parts of the job you leaned on.

⚖️

    How a judge is expected to act

    • Be independent. Judges decide cases free from pressure by governments, politicians or anyone else. Secure tenure and secure pay protect that independence.
    • Be impartial. A judge steps aside from any case where a reasonable person would see a risk of bias, such as a family or business link.
    • Follow the ethical principles. The Canadian Judicial Council's Ethical Principles for Judges guide their conduct, and complaints about judges can be made to a judicial council.
    • Give reasons. Judges explain their decisions so people can understand them and appeal them if they disagree.

    How to treat a judge and the court

    • Show respect in court. Stand when the judge enters, speak politely and address the judge the way that court asks, often 'Your Honour'.
    • Obey court orders. Disobeying a court order is an offence under the Criminal Code and can also be contempt of court.
    • Never try to influence a judge or juror. Trying to bribe or pressure a juror or a judge is obstructing justice, a crime in the Criminal Code.
    • Disagree the proper way. If you think a decision is wrong, the way to challenge it is an appeal to a higher court.
    About this game

    Be the Judge puts you on the bench of a Canadian courthouse. Twelve cases come before you, you pick one of three answers, and four things move: fairness to the people in the courtroom, time on the court's waiting list, public confidence in the courts and getting the law right.

    Every case is small and everyday: a bike theft, a broken fence, a car repair, spray paint on a wall. Each one teaches a real rule of Canadian law, such as the presumption of innocence, the right to reasonable bail, the right to an interpreter and the right to be tried within a reasonable time.

    At the end the game shows which part of the work you leaned on and names jobs in Canada that use it. It is a game, not a career test.

    Tips for playing

    • Under the Charter, everyone charged with an offence is presumed innocent until proven guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.
    • In 2016 the Supreme Court set time limits for criminal cases: 18 months in provincial court and 30 months in superior court, from the charge to the end of the trial.
    • Anyone accused of a crime in Canada can ask for a trial in English or French, anywhere in the country.

    Common questions

    Are these real court cases?

    No. Every case is made up and names no real person, court or town. The rules the game teaches are real Canadian law.

    Can I lose?

    Yes. If fairness, time, public confidence or the law reaches zero, your session ends early — just as real decisions can be overturned on appeal or cases stopped for delay.

    Does this tell me what job I should do?

    No, and it says so on the result screen. It shows which kind of decision you enjoyed making today, and which jobs involve a lot of that.

    Is it good for a classroom?

    Yes. There is no signup and nothing is collected, it works on a phone or a projector, nothing in it is graphic, and every case teaches a real rule about rights and the courts in Canada.
