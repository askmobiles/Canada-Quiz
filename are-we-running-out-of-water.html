💧 Are we running out of water?

Earth has the same amount of water it had a thousand years ago, and will have the same amount a thousand years from now. That is true, and it is also the reason the water argument confuses people. This page separates the questions that keep getting mixed into one — and then asks the same question about the metals we dig up, because the answer turns out to have the same shape.

Last checked 20 September 2026 · every figure is sourced at the bottom

The short answer

On any timescale a human being lives through, the planet is not losing water. What changes is how much water is in a usable place at a usable time, and that is a completely different problem with a completely different solution.

Of all the water on Earth, 2.5 percent is fresh. Of that fresh water, more than two thirds is frozen and almost all of the rest is underground. Every lake and river on the planet put together comes to roughly a quarter of one percent of the fresh water. The total was never the constraint. The usable slice always was.

That distinction — total against usable — is the whole of this page, and it is worth holding on to, because the same test settles the other thing people worry about running out of. Metal does not leave Earth either. Neither does water. In both cases the total is fixed and the usable share is what can be spent, and in both cases the question worth asking is not how much is left but how much is somewhere we can still reach.

So when someone says we are running out of water, they cannot mean the planet is losing it. They mean a particular place is losing its supply, or that we are moving water somewhere it cannot easily come back from. Both of those are real. Neither is the end of the world's water.

Is the amount of water on Earth always the same?

For any purpose a person can have, yes. The United States Geological Survey puts the total water on, in and above the Earth at about 1.386 billion cubic kilometres. That figure does not move on any timescale that matters to a human being.

Earth does leak. About three kilograms of hydrogen escape to space every second, and volcanoes put water back through outgassing. Set that escape against an ocean weighing more than a billion billion tonnes and the loss is smaller than a rounding error over a million years. Across the whole history of the planet, sea level has varied by a few hundred metres against an average ocean depth of four kilometres.

One honest note. The rate of loss to space is measured. The volcanic return is real but is not well quantified in the scientific literature, and this page did not find a primary source giving a global annual figure for it. The conclusion does not depend on that number: nothing published claims Earth's water is measurably changing on human timescales.

Is it really 71 percent water and 29 percent land?

Roughly, and the roughness is the interesting part. The USGS says about 71 percent of Earth's surface is water-covered. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration says the ocean covers approximately 70 percent. Two agencies of the same government, two different numbers, both hedged with a word like about. It is a rounded figure, not a constant.

Over long stretches of time it moves a great deal. Twenty thousand years ago, at the height of the last ice age, glaciers covered 25 percent of Earth's land area and sea level stood more than 120 metres lower than today. Enormous areas of what is now seabed were dry ground. Today glaciers cover 11 percent of land area.

Over two hundred or five hundred years, no. Global mean sea level rose about 0.20 metres between 1901 and 2018. That is real and measurable, and it does not move the split between land and ocean in any way anyone has quantified. If you see a figure claiming the percentage has changed since the 1500s, ask where it came from.

The 2.5 percent that actually matters

This is the single most useful fact on this page. Almost all of Earth's water is salt. Of the small fresh share, almost all is either frozen solid or sitting deep underground. What is left over is everything humanity has ever drunk, farmed with or swum in.

Every river on Earth together holds six thousandths of one percent of the fresh water. Put another way, the world's rivers are about one part in twenty thousand of the planet's water. That is the tap. Everything else is salt, ice, or too deep to reach cheaply.

Can fresh water turn into salt water?

Yes, and this is the part of the worry that holds up. It happens two ways, and both are documented rather than predicted.

One: pumping groundwater into the sea

When water is pumped out of the ground faster than rain refills it, much of it ends up in the ocean, where it becomes salt water. This is measurable in sea level. One study puts groundwater depletion at about 0.40 millimetres of sea level rise per year in the 2000s. A later study argues that is too high, because roughly a fifth of pumped water evaporates and falls again on land, and puts the figure nearer 0.27 millimetres. Against a total rise of roughly four millimetres a year, either number means a noticeable share of the ocean's growth is water that used to be underground and fresh.

Two: the sea moving in underground

Pump a coastal aquifer hard enough and seawater moves in to take the space. The USGS states plainly that saltwater intrusion has occurred to some degree in many of the coastal aquifers of the United States, naming Florida, California, the Atlantic coast and Long Island. Once salt reaches an aquifer, that water can no longer irrigate crops or be drunk. This is local, driven mainly by over-pumping, with sea level rise making it worse. It is not the world's fresh water turning salty everywhere at once.

And some water does not come back

The High Plains aquifer under the American prairies, often called the Ogallala, has lost about ten percent of its recoverable stored water since farming began drawing on it around 1950. In Texas the average water level has dropped 44 feet, and in the worst wells more than 200 feet. NASA satellites found that of the world's 37 largest aquifers, 21 have passed the point where they are being used faster than they refill, and 13 receive almost no refill at all.

This is the honest shape of the water problem. Not a planet drying out, but particular aquifers, in particular places, being spent faster than they are refilled — and some of that water ending up in the sea, where getting it back costs energy.

What an AI data centre actually does with water

Computers make heat. A large data centre makes an enormous amount of it, and the cheapest way to carry heat away is to evaporate water. That is the whole mechanism. Nothing is drunk, nothing is poisoned, nothing is destroyed — water goes in cool and leaves as vapour.

So the water is not destroyed, and you were right to say so. Vapour stays in the atmosphere for about nine days on average and then falls as rain. But it does not fall where it left. Water evaporated in Phoenix does not return to the Phoenix aquifer; it may come down two thousand kilometres away, a week later, on somebody else's fields.

That is why water experts use two words where most news reports use one. The difference between them explains almost everything about this argument.

WordWhat it meansWhy it matters
WithdrawnWater taken out of a river, lake or aquiferBorrowed. Much of it comes back
ConsumedThe part that does not return to that sourceGone from that watershed, whatever happens later

A house consumes roughly 5 to 15 percent of the water it withdraws. The rest goes down the drain, through a treatment plant, and back to the river. A data centre using evaporative cooling consumes 70 to 90 percent of what it withdraws. Microsoft's own reporting shows the pattern plainly: in its 2025 financial year it withdrew 13,266 megalitres worldwide and consumed 8,170 of them, returning about 5,096.

That ratio, not the raw volume, is the real story. A data centre can be a small user by total litres and still be a heavy one for the town it sits in, because almost none of what it takes comes back to the neighbourhood.

There is a way out, and it is already being built. Closed-loop cooling fills the system once during construction and then circulates the same water indefinitely. Microsoft says this design will avoid more than 125 million litres a year per data centre and has applied it to every project designed since August 2024. Air cooling uses almost no water at all — but it needs 33 to 54 percent more electricity, and more electricity usually means more water consumed back at the power station. There is no free move here, only a choice about where the cost lands.

How big is it really?

Both sides of this argument exaggerate, in opposite directions. Here are the measured numbers. In 2023, data centres in the United States directly consumed about 66 billion litres of water. That is roughly 0.02 percent of all American freshwater withdrawals.

Two things in that chart are worth pausing on. The first is that growing food uses more than a hundred and fifty times what data centres consume on site. The second is that the electricity powering those data centres consumes roughly twelve times more water than the buildings themselves — at the power station, out of sight, and almost never counted in the headline.

For a sense of proportion that usually surprises people: golf courses in the United States applied about 547 billion gallons of water in 2020, roughly thirty times what American data centres consumed in 2023. The two figures are not measured the same way — one is water applied, the other water consumed — so this is a rough comparison rather than a precise one. It is still the right order of magnitude.

And now the part that is genuinely a problem

National totals are small. The distribution is not. A 2026 study of American public water systems found that data centre peak demand would exceed the available surplus capacity in 99 percent of the small and medium systems examined. Peak demand runs three to ten times the daily average, and it peaks in summer — the same weeks every lawn in town is being watered.

A rounding error nationally can be a binding constraint in one town, in one month of the year. Both of those sentences are true at the same time, and almost every argument about data centres and water is two people each holding one of them.

The 500 millilitre claim, and what it really says

You have probably seen it: ChatGPT drinks a 500 millilitre bottle of water per conversation. It comes from a real paper, by researchers at the University of California Riverside and the University of Texas at Arlington, later accepted by a peer-reviewed journal. It is not made up. But three things about it are almost always left out.

  • It is per 10 to 50 responses, not per message. The authors themselves widened the range from 20 to 50 in a later version of the paper.
  • About 87 percent of it is water at the power station, not at the data centre. In the paper's own training example, 4.7 of the 5.4 million litres are electricity-related.
  • It models GPT-3 as deployed in 2023. Systems and cooling have both changed since.

Google published a counter-figure in August 2025: 0.26 millilitres for the median text prompt to its Gemini apps, about five drops. That paper is a preprint, it was written by the company being measured, and it counts only on-site water while comparing itself against figures that counted power-station water too. One of the authors of the original 500 millilitre paper made exactly that criticism in public. His group's own like-for-like, on-site-only estimate for an average American data centre is about 2.2 millilitres per request.

So the honest answer is a range, not a number: somewhere between about 0.26 and 2.2 millilitres of on-site water per prompt, depending on the model, the building, the season and the hour of the day. Anyone quoting one confident figure — in either direction — is claiming to know more than the research does.

Canada: the same technology, two different outcomes

Canada has a real advantage here, and it is simply the weather. Cold outside air lets a building cool itself without evaporating anything, which the industry calls free cooling. The QScale campus at Lévis, Quebec runs on free cooling roughly 80 percent of the year because of the local climate, and pipes its waste heat into greenhouses that grow 80,000 tonnes of food a year. Heat that would have been thrown away grows vegetables instead.

The advantage is seasonal, not absolute. Summer still needs cooling, and summer is exactly when a municipal system is least able to spare the water. Placement matters more than climate. An investigation published in March 2026 found that of 38 proposed data centre campuses in Alberta, about three quarters are in areas classed as high or extremely high water stress — with only three planned for low-stress areas, even though such areas make up most of the province.

The largest of the Alberta proposals, in the Municipal District of Greenview, is planned to draw about 24 million cubic metres of water a year from the Smoky River, described as roughly 0.2 percent of the river's annual flow. For scale, that single project would draw more water than Google reported consuming across its entire global operation in 2025. The Sturgeon Lake Cree Nation has said the province approved the water permit without consulting them.

Two Canadian projects, the same decade, the same technology. One recovers its heat to grow food; the other is a water question that has not been settled with the people who live beside the river. The difference is not the technology and not the province. It is where a building was put and who was asked first.

And Canada's own water position is better than most and more complicated than it looks. Environment and Climate Change Canada states that Canada has some 20 percent of the world's total freshwater resources, but that less than half of it — about 7 percent of the global supply — is renewable. Most of the rest is held in lakes, aquifers and glaciers and is not replaced. More than half of the renewable share flows north, away from the southern band where almost every Canadian lives.

The same question, asked about metal

Everything above was one idea applied to water: the total is fixed, the usable share is small, and the argument is always really about the usable share. Metal is the same idea with the numbers changed, and it is worth two minutes because almost everyone gets the first half right and stops there.

Dig gold out of the ground and it does not leave the planet. Nothing does. Of roughly 222,600 tonnes of gold mined in the whole of human history, essentially all of it still exists somewhere. The physics is on your side and it is not in dispute.

But still on Earth is not the same as still usable, and that gap is the whole of the minerals problem. Metal gets alloyed with other metals, coated, glued into composites, buried in landfill, and spread through mine tailings at concentrations too low to be worth recovering. A United Nations panel studied about 60 metals and found that fewer than a third are recycled at better than 50 percent when they reach the end of their life, and that 34 of them are recycled at under 1 percent.

The split is stark, and anyone who repairs phones for a living will recognise it immediately.

MetalHow much comes backWhere it ends up
Steel80 to 90 percent in the United StatesStructural steel from buildings, 97 percent; cars, close to all of it
GoldAbout 60 percent of consumption, 2025Worth too much to throw away, so it keeps circulating
CopperAbout 30 percent of United States supplyWiring and pipe are easy to recover; thin coatings are not
AluminiumOld scrap is about 28 percent of consumptionCans and window frames return; foil and coatings mostly do not
Lithium and rare earths1 to 10 percent at end of lifeBatteries and screens, mostly still in drawers and landfill

So the honest version of your point is this. We are not running out of atoms. We are running out of cheap concentrated places to find them, while throwing away most of what we have already dug up. That is a better problem to have, because it can be solved with engineering and collection rather than luck and geology.

Canada is a useful test of the running-out story, and it fails the test. Natural Resources Canada has never published a date on which Canada runs out of anything. Between 1985 and 2011, while the country mined continuously, Canada's gold reserves rose by 149 percent. Reserves means what is economic to extract at today's prices with today's technology — so the figure goes up when prices rise or a new deposit is proven, and the phrase how many years left has never meant what people think it means.

Forests and rain: the part most people do not know

Here is the fact that makes your instinct about trees correct, and it surprises almost everyone. Between 40 and 50 percent of all the rain that falls on land did not come from the ocean. It evaporated off land and was breathed out by plants, then fell again further inland.

Three separate research groups, using different methods, put the figure at 40, 45 and 51 percent. The spread reflects the method rather than any disagreement about the phenomenon. Forests are not only users of water. They are part of the machinery that carries water inland from the coast, a stage at a time.

And it can be measured in reverse. A study published in the journal Nature in 2023 used satellite records to show that tropical deforestation reduces rainfall — robustly, at scales above about 50 kilometres, in the Amazon, the Congo and Southeast Asia. Until then, models had predicted the effect and observations had been unclear. Now it has been watched happening.

Canada's position is genuinely good news. Natural Resources Canada reports 369 million hectares of forest, close to 9 percent of the world's total, with forest area stable and a very small increase nationally. Canada holds 9 percent of the world's forest and is responsible for 0.37 percent of global deforestation since 1990.

One caveat, in fairness. Stable in area is not the same as everything is fine. Land that is harvested and regrows still counts as forest, so the area figure says nothing about the age of the trees, about fire, or about how much carbon the forest holds. The area is stable. That is the claim, and it is worth making, but it is only that claim.

Is global warming real?

Yes. This page hedges where scientists disagree, and it is not going to hedge here, because they do not. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change assessment published in 2021 is the work of hundreds of scientists reviewing thousands of published papers, and its findings are stated with high confidence.

The clearest single measurement is the sea. Global mean sea level rose 0.20 metres between 1901 and 2018. More telling than the total is the speed: the rate was 1.3 millimetres a year from 1901 to 1971, 1.9 from 1971 to 2006, and 3.7 from 2006 to 2018. Satellite work published in 2024 traces the rate from about 2.1 millimetres a year in 1993 to about 4.5 by 2024 — a doubling in thirty years. That is not a projection. It is a measurement, taken from orbit.

What remains genuinely uncertain is different from whether it is happening. How fast ice sheets will come apart, exactly how much the sea will rise by 2100, how much deforestation the Amazon can absorb before it changes character — these are argued about by people who agree completely on the warming itself. Confusing the two kinds of uncertainty is how most public arguments about climate go wrong.

What nobody knows

Every page like this one should have a section like this, and almost none do. These are questions this article could not answer from a primary source, and the honest thing is to say so rather than fill the gap.

  • How much water Canadian data centres use. There is no published national figure. Most provinces do not require these facilities to meter their water at all, which is itself the finding.
  • How much water volcanoes return to the surface each year. No primary source located publishes a global annual figure.
  • Whether the land and ocean percentages have shifted at all in the past 500 years. Nobody appears to have measured it.
  • How much water one AI query uses. The credible on-site estimates differ by a factor of eight and were produced by parties with opposite interests.
  • How much deforestation the Amazon can take before it stops making its own rain. The threshold is genuinely contested and this page will not invent one.

What actually helps

Nothing on this page argues for using less technology, and nothing argues for pretending there is no problem. The things that measurably help are smaller and more boring than either side usually wants.

  • Put the building in the right place. Cold and wet beats hot and dry, and a cold country has an advantage it did not have to earn.
  • Measure and publish. A facility that meters its water can be held to a number. One that does not cannot be discussed at all.
  • Use the heat. Waste heat from a data centre is a greenhouse, a swimming pool or a district heating loop that did not need to burn anything.
  • Ask the neighbours first. Most of the disputes in this article are not about engineering. They are about a permit granted before a conversation happened.
  • Keep the forests, and yes, keep planting. Between 40 and 50 percent of rain over land is made by land. That was your point and the research supports it.
  • Collect the metal we already dug up. A drawer full of old phones is a richer ore body than most mines.
The water on Earth is not going anywhere, and it never was. The question worth asking is narrower and more answerable: is this particular water, in this particular place, being spent faster than it comes back? That question has an answer, and where the answer is yes it can be fixed by where things are built and how they are cooled.
