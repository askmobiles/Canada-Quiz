What Nunavut publishes, and what it does not

Nunavut publishes less about its driving test than any other jurisdiction in Canada, and the honest place to start is with that. What the territory does say is clear enough. The examination has two parts taken at one sitting, sign identification and the rules of the road and safe driving practices, and if you fail one part you rewrite only that part. Where reading the paper is a barrier, the driver examiner may give the test aloud instead.

What the territory does not publish is the number of questions, the pass mark, the time limit or the fee. A commercial practice site states twenty questions, sixteen correct and eighty percent, along with a dollar figure, and cites nothing at all for any of it; another prints the minimum age as sixteen when the territory's own manual says fifteen. We are not going to repeat a number that decides whether somebody books a test. The twenty questions in each part, the fifteen right needed in each part and the forty minutes on our mock test are a clearly stated practice standard of our own.

The fee is the same story. Fees are set by regulation and no amount appears on any Government of Nunavut page, so we do not print one. Ask your local Motor Vehicle service provider or your Government Liaison Officer, or call the territorial enquiry line at 1-888-975-5999, and ask before you travel rather than after.

Two steps, and no graduated programme at all

Nunavut has no graduated licensing programme. There are no stages, no probationary period, no learner curfew, no passenger limit and no requirement to display an L sign, because none of those things appears in the territory's own manual. That is unusual in Canada and it deserves to be said plainly. We are not borrowing a neighbouring territory's stages to fill the gap, and you should be careful with any site that quietly does.

What the manual describes is a two-step licence. The Class 7 licence is the learner licence, the minimum age is fifteen, and it permits the operation of any vehicle in Class 5 or 6 while you are learning to operate it. To obtain it you pass the written knowledge test, meet the basic vision standard and show suitable identification, which means two acceptable pieces of proof of identity along with the application form and the remittance slip.

The person beside you must hold a valid driver's licence and be authorised under the Act, and the examiner may ask to see that licence. Nothing further about the accompanying driver is published anywhere: not a minimum age, not a number of years licensed, not a required licence class, not a seating position, not an alcohol limit of their own. Where the territory is silent, we say that it is silent rather than borrow a rule from somewhere else.

The licence itself, and what happens after you have it

Demerit points are published at two thresholds and no more. Twelve or more points brings a warning letter from the Registrar and an interview. Fifteen or more brings a suspension of the licence for one month. The value of each individual offence lives in the demerit point regulations, which we could not obtain through any channel that permits automated access, so we do not list per-offence points anywhere on this site.

A Nunavut licence is issued for a five-year period. The renewal window opens ninety days before it expires, online renewal is available for a valid licence that is not cancelled or suspended, and a licence that has been expired for more than twelve months cannot be renewed at all, which means being examined again. Online renewal is the only part of this that happens on a screen: there is no online knowledge test.

If you move to Nunavut you have thirty days to apply for a territorial licence, and any change of name or address has to be reported to the nearest Motor Vehicle service provider within fifteen days. A driver's medical examination is required at seventy-five, at eighty, and every two years after eighty. The vision test for a Class 7 licence covers colour perception and field of vision, and the territory publishes no numeric standard for either, only the words basic and higher.

Where to get the official information

Everything on this page comes from what Nunavut publishes for free. The Nunavut Driver's Manual is the book the knowledge test is written from, and it is free to download from the territory's own site. It is published in English only, as are the territory's three other driver manuals, even though the Government of Nunavut website itself is available in Inuktitut, Inuinnaqtun, English and French. That is why the English title appears in the French place on this site: there is no French edition to name. Read the manual alongside this practice, not instead of it.

This is an unofficial free practice site. It is not affiliated with the Motor Vehicles Division, the Department of Economic Development and Transportation or the Government of Nunavut, and it cannot issue a licence. Always check the official pages for the current rules before you book.

Sign Identification

6 of 30

  1. You come to this sign where two gravel streets meet in the community. What does it require?

    • A complete stop every time, then go when the way is clearCorrect answer
    • Slow down, look, then keep rolling
    • A stop only when another vehicle is in sight
    • A tap on the horn before crossing

    Stop means the wheels stop turning, whether or not anyone else is around. Stop first, look both ways, and move off only when nothing is coming.

  2. How may you drive on a street where this sign is posted?

    • Only in the direction the arrow pointsCorrect answer
    • In either direction, keeping right
    • In either direction if you are turning
    • Only when no other vehicle is present

    Every lane on a one-way street runs the same way. Once you have turned onto it you must be travelling the way the arrow points.

  3. This yellow sign appears on a gravel road. What should you expect just ahead?

    • A rough spot that can throw the vehicle off lineCorrect answer
    • A speed bump you may ignore
    • A change of surface to pavement
    • A stretch where passing is allowed

    Slow down before you reach it. Hitting a bump at speed can bounce the steering out of your hands, and on loose gravel that easily becomes a skid.

  4. What does this sign tell you about the area you are entering?

    • Children may run out without lookingCorrect answer
    • Parking is reserved for families
    • Children are not allowed near the road
    • The area is closed in winter

    Nunavut asks drivers to keep to the posted speed near playgrounds and schools, and to a reasonable and prudent speed where nothing is posted. Children judge distance and speed poorly.

  5. This sign warns that a person will be directing traffic ahead. Whose instructions do you follow there?

    • The person directing traffic, ahead of any signCorrect answer
    • Whichever sign was posted before the work started
    • Your own judgement about the safest path
    • The vehicle in front of you

    A traffic control person stands in the middle of a work area that changes by the hour and can see what the signs cannot. Their signals come first.

  6. Who may leave a vehicle in a space marked with this sign?

    • Only a vehicle displaying a valid accessible parking permitCorrect answer
    • Anyone, for a short stop
    • Delivery vehicles
    • Anyone outside business hours

    The space is wider than the others for a reason: somebody needs that room beside the vehicle to get out. Using it without a permit takes the room away.

Rules of the Road and Safe Driving

6 of 40

  1. What is the youngest age at which you may hold a Class 7 learner licence in Nunavut?

    • 15 years oldCorrect answer
    • 16 years old
    • 17 years old
    • 18 years old

    Nunavut sets 15 as the minimum age for the Class 7 learner licence. Some practice sites print 16, which is the minimum age for a full Class 5, not for the learner licence.

  2. Your community has no Motor Vehicles Office. How are driver services delivered there?

    • Through the Government Liaison Officer programme, which has an office in every communityCorrect answer
    • You must travel to Iqaluit for every service
    • Everything is handled by mail
    • Only during one yearly visit by an examiner

    Nunavut has Motor Vehicles Offices in Iqaluit, Rankin Inlet and Gjoa Haven, and Government Liaison Offices in all twenty-five communities support the rest of the territory. Some examining is also done by third-party examiners.

  3. Which animal does the Nunavut driver's manual tell you to watch for around your vehicle?

    • DogsCorrect answer
    • Caribou
    • Foxes
    • Ravens

    The manual names dogs. They are often close to parked vehicles and can move without warning, so look around the vehicle before you start off and stay alert for animals on the road.

  4. What belongs in the kit the manual says to carry in winter?

    • A scraper brush, booster cables, a shovel, a tow strap, a traction mat, sand and a flashlightCorrect answer
    • A spare windshield, an extinguisher and flares
    • A first aid kit and nothing else
    • A second set of keys and a road map

    Every item on that list is there to get a stuck or dead vehicle moving again, or to let you see and be seen while you work on it. Carrying it costs nothing until the day it matters.

  5. Besides how clearly you see, what else does the Nunavut vision test look at?

    • Colour perception and field of visionCorrect answer
    • How quickly you read a page
    • Your hearing
    • Your reaction time

    Seeing to the sides matters as much as seeing straight ahead, and colour perception matters for signals and signs. If the standard is not met, a vision specialist gives a further test; glasses and contact lenses are allowed and are noted on the licence.

  6. What is the speed limit for trucks outside Nunavut communities?

    • 80 km/h, day or nightCorrect answer
    • 90 km/h, the same as cars
    • 100 km/h
    • There is no separate limit

    Nunavut sets a separate truck limit of 80 km/h outside cities and towns, day or night, where cars may do 90.

Common questions

How many questions are on the real Nunavut knowledge test?

The territory does not publish a number. Commercial practice sites state one anyway, and none of them cites a government source. Our mock test uses a count of our own and says so.

What is the pass mark?

That is not published either, and neither is a time limit. The widely repeated eighty percent has no Government of Nunavut source behind it, so the pass mark on our mock test is our own practice standard.

How old do I have to be?

Fifteen for a Class 7 licence, which is what the territory's own manual says. Some practice sites print sixteen, which is the minimum age for a Class 5 licence, not for the learner licence.

Does Nunavut have graduated licensing?

No. The territory publishes no stages, no stage lengths and no probationary period. What the manual describes is a Class 7 learner licence, then a road test, then a Class 5 licence.

Who can sit beside me while I practise?

Somebody who holds a valid driver's licence and is authorised under the Act, and the examiner may ask to see that licence. Nothing further is published about the accompanying driver, and we are not going to borrow another jurisdiction's rule to fill that in.
