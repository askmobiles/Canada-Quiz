Could Canada Feed Every Canadian?

Canada sells the world a great deal of food and buys a surprising amount back. The answer turns on one number almost nobody knows.

Start with the trade balance

Canada is comfortably a net exporter of food. On Global Affairs Canada's consistent basis for 2025, Canada exported 58.4 billion dollars of farm, fishing and intermediate food products and imported 33.5 billion — a surplus of just under twenty-five billion.

On the wider basis that Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada uses, which includes processed food and seafood, exports reached a record 100.3 billion dollars in 2024, nearly double what they were in 2013. The whole sector is about 149.2 billion dollars, roughly seven percent of Canada's economy, and employs 2.3 million people — about one job in nine.

One correction while we are here. You will see Canada described as a top-five agricultural exporter. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's own briefing material for the incoming minister says Canada was ninth in 2024, behind the United States, Brazil, the Netherlands, Germany, China, France, Spain and Italy, and adds that Canada has not ranked higher than seventh since 2020. Ninth is the sourced number.

The number that decides the question

Canada is the second largest country on earth by area. Only about seven percent of it is farmland, and of the whole country only about five percent is dependable land suitable for long-term annual crop production — around 45.7 million hectares.

A further 72.4 million hectares can support grass, grazing or hay, but is marginal or unsuitable for annual crops. Everything else is too cold, too rocky, too steep, too wet or too far north. The vastness of Canada is mostly not the kind of vastness you can plant.

Those land capability figures come from a Statistics Canada environment publication using a 2006 reference year. Soil capability does not change quickly, but it is the oldest number on this page and worth treating as approximate.

What Canada grows far too much of

Grains, oilseeds and pulses, overwhelmingly. In the 2025 to 2026 crop year Canada produced about 40 million tonnes of wheat and exported roughly 28.7 million of it. Canola production was 21.8 million tonnes. Soybeans, dry peas and lentils are all exported in similar proportions, with well over half of each crop leaving the country.

Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada puts it simply: more than half the value of Canadian agricultural production is exported. Canada also produces enough wheat flour and eggs to supply itself, with close to a billion dozen eggs produced domestically.

And what it cannot supply itself

Fruit, most obviously. Fresh fruit availability was 77.9 kilograms per person in 2025, up 3.7 percent — but that rise came from imports growing 4.7 percent while Canadian production grew only 1.8 percent. Canadians are eating more fruit and it is increasingly not Canadian fruit.

Meat too, at the margin. Beef availability rose 3.2 percent in 2025, which Statistics Canada attributes primarily to a 21.7 percent jump in imports. Poultry availability rose 3.9 percent with poultry imports up 22.2 percent.

Fresh vegetables went the other way: down 3.3 percent per person in 2025, the seventh consecutive annual decline. This page does not give you a share of winter produce that is imported, because Statistics Canada does not publish one, and the honest description is the direction rather than a precise fraction.

Greenhouses are the part that is quietly growing

Canada had 35.9 million square metres of greenhouse area in 2025, up 1.4 percent, and greenhouse vegetable and fruit sales reached 3.1 billion dollars, up ten percent in a year. Ontario holds about sixty-five percent of the area, British Columbia seventeen and Quebec ten.

The fastest-growing category is strawberries, whose greenhouse area grew 45.5 percent in a single year. Greenhouse growing is the one realistic route to producing more of the food Canada currently flies and trucks in through the winter.

So what is the answer?

On calories, Canada already produces far more food than Canadians eat. On variety, it does not, and cannot, without heating a great deal of glass through winter. A country that grows forty million tonnes of wheat and imports most of its fruit is not short of food — it is short of the particular foods people want in February.

Worth noting, because it surprises people: Canada has no federal food self-sufficiency target. There is a Food Policy for Canada, launched in 2019, whose stated vision is that all people in Canada can access a sufficient amount of safe, nutritious and culturally diverse food, and whose language is about resilience rather than self-sufficiency. No percentage target exists to report.

One more thing that gets missed. Food and beverage processing is Canada's largest manufacturing sector by both output and employment, at about seventeen percent of manufacturing on each measure. Feeding a country is not only about growing things; it is also about the factories that turn a harvest into groceries.

Where these facts come from

Trade figures come from Global Affairs Canada and Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada, crop figures from AAFC's outlook for principal field crops, food availability and greenhouse figures from Statistics Canada, and the land capability figures from a Statistics Canada environment publication.

Two figures are missing on purpose: a share of imported winter produce, which Statistics Canada does not publish, and a federal self-sufficiency target, which does not exist.

Common questions

Is Canada a net exporter of food?

Yes, clearly. In 2025 Canada exported $58.4 billion of farm, fishing and intermediate food products and imported $33.5 billion — a surplus of nearly $25 billion.

How much of Canada can actually grow food?

About 7% of Canada's land area is farmland, and only about 5% of the country is dependable land suitable for long-term annual crop production. Canada is huge, but most of it cannot be planted.

Is Canada a top five agricultural exporter?

No. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's own briefing material says Canada ranked ninth in 2024 and has not ranked higher than seventh since 2020.

Does Canada have a food self-sufficiency target?

No. There is a Food Policy for Canada launched in 2019 focused on access and resilience, but no percentage self-sufficiency target exists.
