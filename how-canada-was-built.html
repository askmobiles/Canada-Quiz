🗺️ How Canada Was Built: Every Province and Territory, and the Deal That Made It

Canada started with four provinces and grew to thirteen. Not one of them simply appeared. Each was the end of an argument — a railway promised, a bankruptcy, an armed resistance, a referendum won by seven thousand votes.

1867: why only four?

The Charlottetown Conference of September 1864 was not called to create Canada. It was a meeting about uniting the Maritime colonies, and the Province of Canada invited itself and took over the agenda. Seven colonies were in play. Three walked away: Prince Edward Island wanted its land problem fixed, not a federal government; Newfoundland sent observers and then rejected the idea at the polls in 1869; British Columbia was too far away to be part of the conversation at all.

So four provinces came out of three colonies, because the Province of Canada was split back into Ontario and Quebec — which was the whole point for both of them. Canada West had grown larger than Canada East but had the same number of seats, so its governments collapsed every few months. Quebec, merged in 1840 in a deliberate attempt to assimilate its French-speaking majority, wanted a province it controlled. Federation solved both problems at once.

Nobody voted on it. There was no referendum on Confederation in any of the four founding provinces. In Nova Scotia, Premier Charles Tupper pushed it through the legislature in 1866 without an election, because he knew he would lose one. He was right: in the September 1867 elections anti-Confederates took 36 of 38 provincial seats and 18 of 19 federal seats. Tupper was the only pro-Confederation MP the province sent to Ottawa, and on 1 July 1868 Nova Scotia flew flags at half-mast. Joseph Howe sailed to London to demand the union be undone. Britain refused, and in 1869 Howe accepted better financial terms and joined the federal Cabinet instead. Nova Scotia remains the only province that formally tried to leave.

New Brunswick voted no, then yes

New Brunswick is the only province that rejected Confederation at a general election and then accepted it at the next one. Premier Leonard Tilley called a snap election in 1865 and was crushed; he lost his own seat. What turned it around was fear. In April 1866, Fenian raiders — Irish-American Civil War veterans hoping to pressure Britain over Ireland — attacked Campobello Island. Militarily it was a fiasco. Politically it was decisive: an isolated colony suddenly looked indefensible. An 1866 election returned Tilley, and the legislature endorsed Confederation 38 votes to 1.

1870: the biggest land deal in Canadian history

Rupert's Land was the entire drainage basin of Hudson Bay — about 3.9 million square kilometres, roughly 40 percent of modern Canada — granted by Charles II to the Hudson's Bay Company in 1670 and named for Prince Rupert of the Rhine, the king's cousin. In 1868 the British Parliament authorised the Company to surrender it. Canada paid £300,000, plus land around the trading posts and one-twentieth of the fertile belt. Everything that later became Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut came out of that transaction.

Nobody asked the roughly 12,000 people already living at Red River.

Manitoba: the province that negotiated itself into existence

Before the sale was even legal, Canada sent survey crews to lay the prairie out in square townships — straight across Métis river-lot farms, without asking anyone. On 11 October 1869 a group of Métis including Louis Riel physically stopped a survey party near Saint-Vital. They seized Upper Fort Garry, turned back the incoming lieutenant-governor at the American border, and on 8 December declared a provisional government.

This was not a rejection of Canada. It was a demand to negotiate the terms of joining. The provisional government wrote Lists of Rights asking for province status rather than territory status, land title guarantees, French in the legislature and courts, and denominational schools. Unable to take possession of land it had already paid for, Ottawa negotiated. The Manitoba Act received royal assent on 12 May 1870 and came into force on 15 July, granting almost everything on the list, including 1.4 million acres set aside for Métis children.

Two things poisoned it. The provisional government court-martialled and shot Thomas Scott, a belligerent Orangeman from Ontario, on 4 March 1870. Ontario wanted Riel hanged; Quebec saw him as a defender of French Catholic rights. Riel fled and never returned to public life in Manitoba; he was hanged at Regina in 1885. And of the 1.4 million acres promised to Métis children, only about 15 percent ever reached Métis hands. In 2013 the Supreme Court of Canada ruled that the Crown had failed to keep that promise honourably.

Manitoba entered as the postage stamp province — about 36,000 square kilometres, one-eighteenth of its present size. It grew fivefold in 1881 and reached Hudson Bay and the 60th parallel in 1912.

1871: British Columbia asked for a road and was offered a railway

By the late 1860s British Columbia was broke. The gold rushes were over, people were leaving, and the colony was carrying heavy debt from building the Cariboo Wagon Road. Three futures were argued over in Victoria: stay a crown colony, join Canada, or join the United States — and after the Americans bought Alaska in 1867, annexation had real support in the business community.

At the Yale Convention of September 1868, the pro-Canada delegates set out their terms and asked for a wagon road to the east. Then three of them travelled to Ottawa in 1870 — 24 days, via San Francisco and the brand-new American transcontinental railroad. Having just ridden one, they arrived asking for a railway instead. Macdonald's government offered more than they had asked for: begin a railway to the Pacific within two years, finish it within ten, and take on the colony's debt. British Columbia joined on 20 July 1871. One of the delegates, Dr. Helmcken, told the legislature plainly that he had no love for Canada — this was a business transaction.

1873: Prince Edward Island was bankrupted by a railway

Confederation was born in Charlottetown, and Prince Edward Island then refused to join it for nine years. The Island had its own reasons. Its economy was fine. Under the proposed terms it would get five members out of 194. And it had a much older grievance: on a single day in 1767 the whole island had been divided into 67 lots and handed out by lottery in London to absentee British landlords. A century later Islanders were tenants on their own island, and they believed London, not Ottawa, would eventually fix it.

Then the Island built a railway. In a decision of spectacular short-sightedness the contract paid per mile, so the line wandered, curving to reach every community that lobbied for it. A 120-mile island ended up with roughly 200 miles of track. Costs exploded, credit dried up, and by 1873 the colony was facing default. Ottawa waited, then offered terms it had never offered anyone: take over the railway debt, guarantee year-round steam and telegraph links to the mainland, and advance up to $800,000 to buy out the absentee landlords. The legislature accepted almost unanimously. The Island joined on 1 July 1873 — the Dominion's sixth birthday — and the 108-year-old land grievance was finally settled with Confederation money.

Prince Edward Island is the only province whose borders have never changed.

1898: Yukon was created to hold on to a gold rush

On 17 August 1896, Skookum Jim Mason, Dawson Charlie and George Carmack found coarse gold on Bonanza Creek. When the news reached San Francisco and Seattle in July 1897, roughly 100,000 people set out; perhaps 30,000 to 40,000 arrived. Dawson City went from empty riverbank to a town of about 25,000 in two years.

Ottawa's problem was not gold. It was sovereignty. The overwhelming majority of the stampeders were American, arriving through American ports, and the district was being governed from Regina, thousands of kilometres away. The North-West Mounted Police were rushed in — Sam Steele's men sat at the summits of the Chilkoot and White Passes enforcing the famous rule that no one crossed without a ton of supplies, and making it unmistakable whose law applied. The Yukon Territory Act followed on 13 June 1898, giving the district its own government close enough to the goldfields to actually govern them.

The bust matched the boom. Yukon's population fell from 27,219 in 1901 to 4,157 in 1921 — an 85 percent collapse in twenty years.

1905: the province that never was, called Buffalo

The territorial premier, Frederick Haultain, spent 1901 to 1903 campaigning for one single large western province, to be called Buffalo. His argument was that the region had one economy and one history, and that only a big province would give westerners real weight in Ottawa.

Wilfrid Laurier's government said no, and the reasons were partly administrative and largely political. One province a thousand miles wide was said to be unmanageable. More to the point, it would have been a rival power centre; two would be easier to handle. The line they chose runs north to south at roughly the 4th meridian. It follows no river, no mountain range and no cultural divide, and it split Conservative strength rather than concentrating it. Haultain, a Conservative sympathiser, was passed over for the premiership of either new province.

Both provinces were created on 1 September 1905 — and both were denied something every other province had. Ottawa kept ownership of their Crown lands and natural resources, so it could control settlement. That grievance defined Prairie politics for a generation and was corrected only in 1930, twenty-five years later, by the Natural Resources Transfer Acts. Manitoba got its resources back in the same year.

Alberta is named for Princess Louise Caroline Alberta, Queen Victoria's fourth daughter. Saskatchewan comes from the Cree name for the river, kisiskāciwani-sīpiy, meaning swift-flowing river.

1949: the country that voted itself into Canada by 7,000 votes

Newfoundland was Britain's oldest overseas colony and, from 1907, a self-governing dominion — legally the equal of Canada, Australia and New Zealand. The Depression destroyed its economy, and in 1933, facing default, the legislature voted itself out of existence. From February 1934 an unelected Commission of Government appointed from London ran the country. Newfoundland is the only democracy in the modern Commonwealth to have given up self-government voluntarily.

After the war the question was what to do next, and it took two referendums. The first, in June 1948, split three ways and produced no majority. The second, on 22 July 1948, was a straight choice between responsible government and Confederation with Canada. Confederation won with 78,323 votes against 71,334 — a margin of about seven thousand votes, or 52.3 percent to 47.7 percent. Joey Smallwood, who had begun the campaign with no seat, no party and no money, became the province's first premier and governed for 23 years.

31 March or 1 April? Both, and the reason is charming. Term 50 of the Terms of Union says the union takes effect immediately before the expiration of the thirty-first day of March, 1949 — one minute to midnight. Smallwood insisted on that wording so that nobody could ever say Newfoundland had joined Canada on April Fool's Day. Statistics Canada and The Canadian Encyclopedia use 31 March; many celebrations use 1 April. They describe the same moment.

One negotiated term is worth remembering: Newfoundland kept the right to make margarine, which was banned in Canada at the time to protect the dairy industry. Margarine was a household staple there, and it was a genuine sticking point. The province was called simply Newfoundland for 52 years, even though Labrador is more than twice its land area; the name became Newfoundland and Labrador by constitutional amendment on 6 December 2001.

1999: Nunavut, negotiated into existence over 23 years

Nunavut was not a colonial arrangement. It was an Inuit political project, and the key idea was subtle. In 1976 the Inuit Tapirisat of Canada proposed a new territory as part of settling their land claim — but not an ethnic self-government carve-out. They asked for an ordinary public government in a territory where Inuit would be about 85 percent of the population, so that self-determination would come through normal democracy.

It took a plebiscite on dividing the Northwest Territories in 1982, which passed with 56.5 percent; a second vote on the boundary itself in May 1992, which passed with about 54 percent; ratification of the land claim by about 85 percent of Inuit voters later that year; and the signing of the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement at Iqaluit on 25 May 1993 — the largest Indigenous land claim settlement in Canadian history. Parliament then set the creation date six years into the future so a government could actually be built. Nunavut opened on 1 April 1999, with about 25,000 people and roughly a fifth of Canada's land area. It was the first change to the map of Canada since 1949.

Nunavut means our land in Inuktitut. Its legislature uses consensus government, with no political parties — members elect the premier and cabinet from among themselves. The Northwest Territories works the same way.

NameOrigin
CanadaFrom the Iroquoian kanata, meaning village or settlement.
OntarioFrom the Iroquoian kanadario, sparkling or beautiful water.

New BrunswickFor King George III, who was also Duke of Brunswick.
