💰 Canada's trillion-dollar plan — what was actually promised

On 14 and 15 September 2026 Canada held its first investment summit and asked global investors for one trillion dollars over five years. Almost every headline since has reported that number as though it had arrived. It has not. This page separates what was asked for from what was announced from what actually exists.

Last checked 19 September 2026 — this page covers a fast-moving situation

The short answer

Canada held its first investment summit in Toronto on 14 and 15 September 2026, co-hosted with CPP Investments and PSP Investments. The government's stated objective is one trillion dollars of total investment in Canada over five years. That is an ambition. No trillion dollars has been pledged, signed or received.

The number behind the number. The Parliamentary Budget Officer examined the trillion in May 2026 and found that 285 billion dollars of it is planned federal spending. The remaining 715 billion is assumed to come from provincial cost-sharing and private investment that the federal spending is expected to attract. That distinction is the single most useful thing on this page.

What was actually announced

From the Prime Minister's own remarks on 15 September and the government's summit page. These are announcements, which is not the same as things that have happened.

  • The four largest airports — Toronto Pearson, Montréal-Trudeau, Calgary and Vancouver — to be opened to private investment. The government keeps the land and sells a concession to operate. In the Prime Minister's words: “We're selling a concession, not privatizing the airport.” Consultations were said to be coming in the following weeks.
  • Immediate expensing for most capital investment, which the government says gives Canada the lowest marginal effective tax rate in the G7 at 6.4 percent.
  • A Canada Strong Fund, described as a sovereign wealth fund taking Canadian stakes in projects. The Spring Economic Update gives it 25 billion dollars of seed capital over three years. We could find no governing legislation, mandate or start date.
  • Liquefied natural gas exports to double to 50 million tonnes a year by 2030, then double again.
  • 14 gigawatts of hydro, wind and storage in Newfoundland and Labrador; a Defence Industrial Strategy put at half a trillion dollars over a decade; more than 50 critical minerals agreements said to unlock 20 billion dollars.
A warning about the jobs figure. In April and May 2026 the federal government's own Spring Economic Update and the Parliamentary Budget Officer described 15 projects worth about 125 billion dollars supporting over 60,000 construction jobs. The summit materials in September describe 27 initiatives worth over 192 billion supporting more than 330,000 jobs. Four months apart, from the same government, with no published method behind either number. We give both, with their dates, and assert neither.

What is genuinely good about it

Canada is already selling less to the United States

This is the strongest verified part of the whole case, and it is not a forecast. In the first quarter of 2025 the United States took 71.6 percent of Canada's merchandise exports. By the first quarter of 2026 it took 64.1 percent — the lowest share since Statistics Canada began tracking it. Exports to everywhere else rose 13.0 percent in one quarter and 4.1 percent in the next.

Be careful with it, though. That shift was driven by tariffs and by gold shipments to the United Kingdom. None of the projects on the government's list is in service, so none of them caused it. It shows the direction is real; it does not show the plan is working.

Indigenous ownership that actually exists

In June 2026 seven Williams Treaties First Nations took a minority stake in the Darlington New Nuclear Project, backed by a 715 million dollar financing package — half through the federal Indigenous Loan Guarantee Program and half through Ontario. Finance Canada calls it the largest Indigenous loan guarantee ever issued in Canada. The programme itself is 10 billion dollars. This is money that moved, not a target.

One independent model says the upside is large

TD Economics published a scenario in August 2026 in which a sustained investment wave lifts Canadian growth to 2 to 2.5 percent a year and adds about 12,000 dollars of real output per person by 2035. It is worth reading, and it is worth reading its conditions: the authors say it requires regulatory reform, competitive tax and a much larger skilled workforce, and that “nibbling around the edges of past policies is unlikely to do the trick”. It models an investment wave in general, not this list of projects.

What it costs — the environment

Canada emitted 685 million tonnes of greenhouse gases in 2024. That is down 10 percent from 2005, and down 0.3 percent from 2023 — essentially flat. Oil and gas is the largest single sector at 208 million tonnes, 30 percent of the national total, and it is 5 percent higher than in 2005 even though it has come down from its 2014 peak.

Every independent projection found says Canada misses its 2030 target. The Parliamentary Budget Officer puts the gap at 49 to 102 million tonnes. The Canadian Climate Institute puts it at roughly 65 to 147 million tonnes and notes that the government's own progress report “does not directly acknowledge the large and growing gap”. The Canada Energy Regulator's current-measures scenario has emissions 21 percent below 2005 in 2030, against a target of 40 to 45 percent, and largely flat after 2035.

What the gas projects would add

Six operating British Columbia LNG facilities by 2035 would add just under 13 million tonnes a year — about 20 percent to British Columbia's emissions. If every proposed BC project proceeds, upstream emissions rise about 10 million tonnes a year. Set those against a national total of 685 and a gap to target already measured in the tens of millions.

The argument that Canadian gas lowers world emissions

This is a genuine dispute and it is worth understanding, because both sides agree on the part that is arithmetic and disagree only about what happens in the buying country.

  • For. A Public Policy Forum and Canadian Chamber report, modelled by Navius Research in December 2025, finds Canadian LNG at 47.6 million tonnes a year by 2035 could cut global emissions by 40 to 70 million tonnes a year by displacing dirtier supply. It puts British Columbia LNG at about 0.15 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per tonne against a global average near 0.35.
  • Against. The Canadian Climate Institute answers that extra LNG imports do not necessarily retire any coal plant, that other suppliers exist, and that official Canadian methane estimates are “almost certainly low”. The International Institute for Sustainable Development finds “little evidence” that Canadian LNG will displace coal abroad, and cites United States Department of Energy modelling finding that more American LNG exports would increase net global emissions, because new gas displaces renewables more than it displaces coal.

Both sides agree Canadian LNG raises Canadian emissions. The disagreement is entirely about the counterfactual in the importing country, which is a thing nobody can measure directly.

What it costs — consent

There is no single Indigenous position on these projects, and any article that suggests otherwise is selling something. The same law is being challenged in court by First Nations in Ontario while a modern-treaty nation in British Columbia leads one of the projects it enables.

One pattern is visible in all of it: opposition is strongest where a nation is affected but is not a partner, and strongest of all on marine oil transport. Support tracks equity ownership.

What the law says about consent. In Kebaowek First Nation, decided in 2025, the Federal Court held that free, prior and informed consent under Canada's UNDRIP legislation is “a right to a robust process” requiring a deep level of consultation — not a veto. That is the legal position as it stands, whatever either side would prefer.

What it costs — the risk of not being built

There are not enough workers

BuildForce Canada, in July 2026, projects that Canadian construction needs 306,200 workers by 2034 and will still be short as many as 34,300 even after recruiting some 271,900 new entrants under thirty. In the residential sector alone, 135,000 workers — more than a fifth of it — are expected to retire by 2035. BuildForce is tracking nearly 800 major projects worth more than 500 billion dollars, all competing for the same people.

This is not a Canadian failing. Bent Flyvbjerg's international study of megaprojects found that nine in ten go over budget, with rail averaging a 44.7 percent overrun and a 51.4 percent shortfall in the passengers who were supposed to turn up. It is simply what large projects do, and it should be priced in before anyone celebrates a headline total.

And the price may not hold

In September 2026 oil was expensive because of supply disruption — North Sea Dated averaged 91 US dollars a barrel in August and spiked above 113 on 9 September. The International Energy Agency expects supply to rebound by about 8 million barrels a day in 2027 while demand recovers only 2.6 million. On gas the picture is already loose: global LNG production is growing more than 7 percent in 2026, the fastest since 2019, with North America driving most of it. A project sanctioned at today's price arrives into that.

And the law may not help as much as advertised

The C.D. Howe Institute, in September 2026, argued that the Building Canada Act creates a new process for a subset of projects and centralises decisions in the federal cabinet rather than fixing the underlying problem, and that “attempting to circumvent current regulatory approval processes is more complicated and leads to greater uncertainty than simply removing the underlying problems”. It also notes the Act's definition of national interest is so broad it is hard to imagine a project that would not qualify.

And on the airports specifically, the objection came within a day. The Canadian Labour Congress warned privatisation would cost travellers and pressure workers. Unifor's Aviation Council protested over wages and conditions — its national president said airport workers know from experience what privatisation costs them. The Leader of the Opposition asked for details and warned against “sweetheart deals for corporate power brokers”. Note carefully that Canada's unions support the nation-building investment in general and oppose this particular mechanism; those are different positions and they are easy to confuse.

So what level does this put Canada at?

This is the question the page was asked, and it deserves a straight answer: nobody has modelled it. No study was found that estimates what changes if all eighteen referred projects and nine strategies are built. The C.D. Howe Institute's own ranking paper notes that cost-benefit analyses were not done for all the projects it assessed. The government's totals come with no published method. So here is where Canada stands today, and then what can honestly be said about the change.

What would measurably change

  • Gas exports, by far the most. Canada is a rounding error in world LNG today. Even partial success here is a genuine change of position.
  • Copper. The Red Chris expansion alone would raise Canada's copper production by more than 15 percent. Canada is not in the top five for copper today.
  • Indigenous ownership of infrastructure, which is already changing through instruments that exist and have been used.
The sharpest thing we found, and nobody seems to be saying it. The government's target is 50 million tonnes of LNG a year by 2030, then double again. Canada's own energy regulator, in Canada's Energy Future 2026, does not have Canadian LNG exports reaching 48 million tonnes until 2050 — and even its higher scenario reaches only 62 million tonnes by 2050. The 2030 target is roughly where Canada's own regulator expects the country to be at mid-century.

What would not change

  • Canada's rank by size of economy. 192 billion dollars spread over years, against an economy of roughly 2.5 trillion, does not move Canada past Italy or Brazil. No source claims it would.
  • Canada's share of world oil. The Canada Energy Regulator has Canadian crude peaking at 6.1 million barrels a day in 2042, against world supply already projected above 110 million in 2027. Canada stays a 5-to-6-percent producer and stays fourth.
  • Potash and uranium. Canada is already first and second. Nothing on the list changes that.
  • The 2030 emissions target. Every independent projection has Canada missing it. None of these projects closes that gap and several widen it.
  • Productivity, on its own. The Parliamentary Budget Officer records labour productivity growth as essentially flat from 2022 to 2025, against a historical average of 1.2 percent. TD's model says a sustained investment wave could change that; the PBO says the conditions for it are not yet met.

The honest summary

Almost everything in this file is a target, a referral or a claim. Three things are executed facts: the Darlington Indigenous loan guarantee, LNG Canada's first phase operating since June 2025, and the real shift in where Canada sells. Schedule 1 of the Building Canada Act — the legal list of national interest projects — was still empty at last check, which means the Act's unusual powers have not yet been used on anything.

Is it good for Canada? The direction — selling to more countries than one, owning more of what is built, producing minerals the world needs — is defensible on the evidence. The execution is unproven, the labour to do it does not yet exist, the emissions arithmetic does not currently work, and the consent is partial and contested. Anyone who tells you it is simply good, or simply bad, has not read the file.

What is not known

  • The method behind 192 billion dollars and 330,000 jobs. Nothing is published, there is no breakdown by project, and the figures moved a long way in four months.
  • What the Canada Strong Fund actually is. A seed figure exists; no legislation, mandate or start date was found.
  • What the government's specific LNG target would do to emissions. No modelling of that target was found; the available Canadian figures cover six British Columbia facilities to 2035.
  • Whether the North Coast oil tanker ban still stands. Sources we could reach conflict, so we say nothing about the West Coast pipeline's marine leg.
  • The quantum, defence and critical minerals figures from the summit are single-sourced to the government's own speech and campaign page, with no independent corroboration found.
