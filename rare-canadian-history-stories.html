🔎 15 True Canadian Stories Almost Nobody Knows

Not the last spike, not Vimy Ridge, not Terry Fox. Fifteen documented stories from Canadian history that most Canadians have never heard — some wonderful, some hard, all of them true and all of them sourced.

Last checked 9 August 2026

A note before you start. Some of these stories involve real injustice and real deaths. We have written them plainly rather than dramatically, named what governments actually did, and said clearly where an apology was later made and when. Where no apology has ever been made, we say that too. Every story links to an authoritative source at the end of the page.

1. The city that lost its sight, and the Christmas tree that never stopped coming

Halifax, 6 December 1917

Just after nine on a clear Thursday morning, a French munitions ship and a Norwegian relief ship collided in Halifax Harbour. The munitions ship caught fire and drifted to a pier, and thousands of people came to their windows to watch. Twenty minutes later it detonated — the largest human-made explosion in the world before the atomic bomb. About 2,000 people died, some 9,000 were injured, and roughly 6,000 lost their homes. A blizzard arrived the next day.

Boston heard by telegraph within the hour. A relief train of doctors, nurses and supplies left the city by ten that same night. Nova Scotia sent Boston a thank-you Christmas tree in 1918 and, since 1971, has sent one every single year. It is the tree that lights Boston Common each December.

The detail that stays with you: so many spectators were struck by flying window glass that 249 damaged eyes had to be surgically removed, and 16 people lost both. The disaster directly led to the founding of the Canadian National Institute for the Blind in 1918.

2. Canada's forgotten Titanic sank in fourteen minutes

St. Lawrence River, 29 May 1914

Two years after the Titanic, and ten weeks before the First World War began, the liner Empress of Ireland was steaming down the St. Lawrence toward Liverpool. In heavy fog near Pointe-au-Père, a Norwegian collier struck her. She rolled over and sank in fourteen minutes. Of 1,477 people aboard, 1,012 died — more passengers than were lost on the Titanic. It remains the worst maritime disaster in Canadian history.

The war swallowed the story. Newspapers moved on within weeks, and a Canadian ship, in Canadian waters, carrying mostly Canadian and British passengers, never got the fame the Titanic did.

The detail that stays with you: 138 children were aboard. Five survived. Most were in third class, deep in the hull, with almost no chance in fourteen minutes. Parks Canada estimates that around a million Canadians today descend from immigrants who arrived on this one ship between 1906 and 1914.

3. The largest forest fire in recorded North American history

Miramichi, New Brunswick, 7 October 1825

After a hot, bone-dry summer, fires burning in the New Brunswick woods merged and ran. In roughly a day and a half the Great Miramichi Fire burned about 16,000 square kilometres — a fifth of the province's forests. Newcastle and Douglastown were destroyed. At least 160 people died in Newcastle alone, and regional estimates run to about 500 out of a population of some 8,500. People, cattle and wild animals waded into the Miramichi River together and stood in the water all night while ships anchored around them burned.

The detail that stays with you: it raised roughly £50,000 in international relief — the largest disaster relief effort in Canada before Confederation — and then Canada essentially forgot about it. Until a 2020 book, the longest account ever written of the fire ran about nine pages. Parks Canada finally designated it a National Historic Event in 2025.

4. The Black Loyalists who were promised land, and sailed away to found a country

Nova Scotia, 1783 to 1792

During the American Revolution, Britain promised freedom to enslaved people who left rebel owners and joined the British side. When Britain lost, about 3,500 free Black Loyalists were evacuated to Nova Scotia — the largest free Black community anywhere in North America at the time. They were promised 100 acres per household plus 50 more per family member.

The promise was broken. Grants came late, tiny, on poor soil, or never. In July 1784, white Loyalists in Shelburne, angry that free Black labourers worked for lower wages, attacked Birchtown and destroyed about twenty homes over ten days. It is often called Canada's first race riot.

The detail that stays with you: Thomas Peters, a formerly enslaved veteran, sailed to London in 1790 carrying his community's petitions and came back with an offer. In January 1792, nearly 1,200 Black Nova Scotians boarded fifteen ships and left. They founded Freetown, Sierra Leone. Their descendants there still identify as Nova Scotians.

5. The massacre that sent the Mounties west

Cypress Hills, 1 June 1873

In the spring of 1873 a group of Nakoda people travelled roughly 500 kilometres to camp in the Cypress Hills, in what is now southwestern Saskatchewan, escaping starvation. A party of American wolf hunters arrived convinced that Indigenous people had stolen their horses. Fuelled by whisky and determined to punish someone, they attacked the camp. Accounts vary, but roughly twenty people were killed — Elders, warriors, women and children.

Canada had passed the act creating the North-West Mounted Police only days earlier, in May 1873. News of the massacre reached Ottawa that summer and turned a plan on paper into an urgent priority. The famous March West happened in 1874 in large part because of this.

The detail that stays with you: the Mounties pursued the killers for years and never got a conviction. The Americans were tried and acquitted and extradition failed. Parks Canada notes that the significance was not the outcome but the attempt — that Ottawa treated a crime against Indigenous people as a crime worth investigating was itself new.

6. A tax you paid for being Chinese, and a law that shut the door for 24 years

Canada, 1885 to 1947 — apology 22 June 2006

When the Canadian Pacific Railway was finished, Canada no longer wanted the Chinese labourers who had built its most dangerous sections. In 1885 Parliament imposed a head tax of $50 on almost every Chinese person entering the country. No other nationality paid it. It rose to $100 in 1900 and $500 in 1903 — roughly two years' wages for a labourer.

It did not stop immigration, so in 1923 Parliament banned Chinese immigration almost entirely. The Act took effect on 1 July — Dominion Day — and for years afterward Chinese Canadians marked that date as Humiliation Day and closed their businesses rather than celebrate. The ban stood until 1947. Between 81,000 and 97,000 people paid the head tax, and Ottawa collected about $23 million. Because men came alone and could not bring wives, the communities became bachelor societies; at one point men outnumbered women about 28 to 1.

The apology: on 22 June 2006 Prime Minister Stephen Harper apologised in the House of Commons. Surviving payers and surviving spouses received symbolic payments of $20,000, with $24 million for community projects and $10 million for education.

7. Two months in Vancouver harbour, and a warship to see them off

Vancouver, 23 May to 23 July 1914 — apology 18 May 2016

The Komagata Maru arrived off Vancouver carrying 376 passengers from India — 340 Sikhs, 24 Muslims and 12 Hindus. All were British subjects arriving in a country that was part of the same empire. Canada's 1908 continuous journey rule required immigrants to travel directly from their country of citizenship, and no shipping line ran direct from India. The rule was designed to be impossible to meet.

About twenty passengers with prior Canadian residency were allowed ashore. The rest were held aboard for two months in worsening conditions. They cabled the Governor General: many requests to the Immigration Department for water but useless, better order to shoot us than this miserable treatment. On 19 July, 160 police tried to storm the ship and were driven back. The government then sent the naval cruiser HMCS Rainbow, guns uncovered, with 204 militia aboard. On 23 July the ship was escorted out to sea.

The detail that stays with you: the story did not end at sea. When the ship reached Budge Budge near Calcutta on 26 September 1914, British authorities treated the passengers as political agitators. Shooting broke out and about 22 people were killed, including 16 passengers; more than 200 survivors were imprisoned. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau apologised in the House of Commons on 18 May 2016.

8. The men who built the road to Lake Louise were prisoners

Canada, 1914 to 1920 — no apology has ever been made

When the First World War began, Canada invoked the War Measures Act and declared people from Austria-Hungary, Germany and the Ottoman Empire enemy aliens. The largest group affected were Ukrainians, who had come from territory ruled by Austria-Hungary and many of whom Canada had actively recruited to settle the prairies only years earlier.

Some 8,579 people were interned in 24 camps and receiving stations. Another 80,000 had to carry papers and report regularly to police. Internees lost the vote, had property and savings confiscated, and were put to hard labour for 25 cents a day — far below the going wage — clearing land, cutting timber, mining, and building infrastructure in the national parks. At least 107 died in captivity.

The detail that stays with you: internees at the Castle Mountain and Cave and Basin camps in Banff built the road to Lake Louise in 1915. Millions of tourists have driven it since without knowing it was built by prisoners who had committed no crime. Parliament passed a recognition act in 2005 and the government created a $10 million commemoration endowment in 2008, but Canada has never apologised.

9. The battalion Black Canadians had to fight to be allowed to join

Pictou, Nova Scotia, 5 July 1916 — apology 9 July 2022

When war broke out in 1914, Black Canadians volunteered and were turned away at recruiting offices across the country. Some travelled from town to town trying to enlist. Under pressure the army compromised: instead of allowing Black men into regular units, it created a separate one.

No. 2 Construction Battalion was authorised on 5 July 1916. More than 600 men joined, from across Canada, the United States and the West Indies. It was the first and only all-Black battalion-sized unit in Canadian military history — and it was non-combatant. The men were not permitted to fight. They served in France with the Forestry Corps, felling timber, building roads and running the sawmills that supplied the trenches. Heavy, dangerous work, without the recognition given to combat troops. They came home to the same discrimination they had left.

The detail that stays with you: the battalion's chaplain, Honorary Captain William Andrew White, was the first Black man to serve as a chaplain in the British and Dominion forces. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau apologised to the descendants at Truro, Nova Scotia, on 9 July 2022.

10. The community Halifax put a dump beside, then bulldozed

Africville, Halifax, 1840s to 1970 — apology 24 February 2010

Africville was a Black community on the shore of Bedford Basin in north-end Halifax, settled from the 1830s and 1840s and home to generations of families. It had its own school from 1883, its own post office, its own stores, and at its heart the Seaview United Baptist Church.

Residents paid municipal taxes. In return, the City of Halifax refused them running water, sewers, paved roads and garbage collection. Instead the city surrounded them with what it did not want elsewhere: a prison, a slaughterhouse, an infectious diseases hospital, railway lines, and around 1955 the city's open garbage dump, placed within metres of homes. In 1958 the city declared the community a slum, citing conditions it had itself created. Between 1964 and 1970 residents were moved out and their homes demolished immediately. People who could not produce a deed to land their families had lived on for a century were given $500.

The detail that stays with you: the Seaview United Baptist Church, the spiritual centre of the community, was bulldozed in the middle of the night on 20 November 1967, without warning to the congregation. Halifax apologised on 24 February 2010. A replica church was rebuilt in 2011 and the Africville Museum opened in 2012. Former residents and their descendants still hold an annual reunion on the site.

11. The secret spy school on Lake Ontario

Whitby, Ontario, 6 December 1941 to 1944

On a 260-acre farm between Whitby and Oshawa, British intelligence built a school to teach ordinary people how to be secret agents. Officially it was Special Training School No. 103. Everyone called it Camp X. It opened on 6 December 1941 — the day before Pearl Harbor, and before the United States had a covert service of its own.

Roughly 500 agents passed through. They learned silent killing, sabotage, lock-picking, forgery, coded radio work and parachuting. Canadians were recruited for their European languages, including twelve French Canadians, four of whom went on to operate behind enemy lines in occupied France. Early recruits of the American OSS, forerunner of the CIA, trained there too.

The detail that stays with you: the real jewel was not the spy school but Hydra, a radio station relaying secret Allied traffic — including material from the Bletchley Park codebreakers — between Britain, Canada and the United States. By mid-1944 it was handling around 39,000 messages a day. And one myth worth correcting: Ian Fleming did not train at Camp X, despite what many websites say.

12. The clerk who walked out of the Soviet embassy with documents under his shirt

Ottawa, 5 to 7 September 1945

Igor Gouzenko was a 26-year-old cipher clerk at the Soviet legation in Ottawa. On the evening of 5 September 1945, three days after the Second World War formally ended, he walked out with a bundle of secret telegrams hidden under his clothes. They proved that Canada's wartime ally had been running spy networks inside Canada, Britain and the United States, targeting among other things the secrets of the atomic bomb.

Almost nobody would listen. The Ottawa Journal turned him away. The Justice Minister's office did nothing. He and his pregnant wife spent a terrifying night hiding with neighbours while Soviet officials broke into their apartment. Only on 7 September, after the break-in, did Canadian authorities take him seriously. A royal commission reported in 1946 that a spy ring had indeed been operating; twelve people were arrested and several convicted.

The detail that stays with you: Gouzenko lived the rest of his life in Canada under an assumed name. When he appeared in public or on television he wore a hood or a cloth bag over his head. Many Canadians knew his story without ever seeing his face. The affair is widely considered one of the opening events of the Cold War.

13. Japan bombed Canada with paper balloons, and Canadians were told to say nothing

November 1944 to spring 1945

Late in the Second World War, Japan launched between 9,000 and 10,000 balloons carrying incendiary and anti-personnel bombs into the jet stream, aimed at North America. Each was about ten metres across, made largely of paper laminated with a paste from konnyaku root, with a sandbag and altimeter ballast system to keep it aloft for the three-day Pacific crossing. It was the first intercontinental weapon in history.

Roughly a tenth reached North America. At least 80 were found in Canada. The first was discovered on 1 January 1945 near Stony Rapids, Saskatchewan, and on 12 January a nearly intact balloon came down at Minton, Saskatchewan, bombs and ballast still attached — a windfall for Allied intelligence. Governments imposed a news blackout, both to deny Japan any idea whether the campaign was working and to keep the public calm.

The detail that stays with you: the blackout had a cost. On 5 May 1945 near Bly, Oregon, a pregnant woman and five children on a Sunday school picnic found a downed balloon and pulled at it. All six were killed — the only people killed by enemy action on the North American mainland in the entire war. Remnants still turn up: a Canadian Armed Forces team destroyed one found in a British Columbia forest as recently as 2014. If you ever find wartime ordnance, do not touch it. Call the police.

14. The night the earth squeezed, and the world watched live

Springhill, Nova Scotia, 23 October 1958

Springhill sat on top of one of the deepest coal mines in the world, with a slope over four kilometres long reaching more than a kilometre below the surface. At that depth, mines suffer bumps: the rock, under crushing pressure, suddenly fails and slams shut. On the evening of 23 October 1958 Springhill suffered the most violent bump in North American mining history. Roof and floor came together. Seventy-four men were killed.

Then the world stopped. Rescue crews dug for days. On 29 October, six days in, they found twelve men alive. On 1 November, nine days in, they found seven more.

The detail that stays with you: one of the trapped men was Maurice Ruddick, a father of twelve and the only Black miner on shift. Badly injured himself, he kept the group alive for eight and a half days by leading them in song and prayer. One survivor's mother said flatly that if it were not for Maurice they would all have died. Ruddick was named Canadian Citizen of the Year. When the Governor of Georgia invited the survivors on a holiday and learned one was Black, he insisted Ruddick could only come on a segregated basis: the white miners went to a motel, and the Ruddick family was put in a trailer, apart. CBC's coverage from the pit head was the first live television broadcast of a disaster in Canada.

15. The island of wild horses that Canadian children saved by writing letters

Sable Island, June 1960

Sable Island is a crescent of sand 42 kilometres long and rarely more than 1.5 kilometres wide, sitting 290 kilometres out in the Atlantic southeast of Halifax. It has no trees and no harbour. More than 350 ships have wrecked on it, which is why it is called the Graveyard of the Atlantic. And it has horses — several hundred, living entirely wild, drinking from freshwater ponds in the dunes, descended from animals put ashore in the 1700s.

In May 1960 Canadians opened their newspapers to learn that the federal Crown Assets Disposal Corporation had listed every Sable Island horse for sale, with slaughter — including for pet food — the likely outcome.

The detail that stays with you: children saved them. Schoolchildren across Canada and the United States wrote letters to Prime Minister John Diefenbaker demanding the horses be left alone. On 2 June 1960 he intervened and protected them from all human interference, and in 1961 it became an offence to feed, touch or interfere with them without written permission. As the children had put it, the horses would stay as free as the wind. They are still there, still unmanaged, still legally untouchable — no vet care, no feeding, no rescue. Sable Island became a National Park Reserve in 2013.
