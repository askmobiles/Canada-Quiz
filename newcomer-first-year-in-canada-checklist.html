🧭 Your First Year in Canada: A Newcomer's Checklist

A practical, step-by-step checklist for your first year in Canada — SIN, health card, bank account, housing, schools, taxes and citizenship.

Arriving in a new country means a hundred small tasks, all of them urgent-looking at once. This checklist puts them in a sensible order, so you can do the important things first and stop worrying about the rest until later.

Week one: the documents that unlock everything

  • Social Insurance Number (SIN). A nine-digit number you need in order to work and to file taxes. Apply through Service Canada — online, by mail, or in person. It is free. Never give your SIN to anyone who is not an employer, a bank or the government.
  • Provincial health card. Public health coverage is run by each province, and some have a waiting period of up to three months before coverage begins. Apply immediately, and consider private interim insurance to cover the gap.
  • Bank account. Most major banks have newcomer packages with no monthly fee for the first year. Bring your passport and immigration documents. Ask about a secured credit card, which is the standard way to start building a Canadian credit history.
  • Phone plan. You will need a Canadian number for almost every other application. Prepaid plans are a good starting point until you have credit history.

First month: settling in

  • Housing. Learn your province's tenant rights before you sign anything. Understand what "utilities included" means, whether a deposit is legal in your province, and what notice period applies. Get every agreement in writing.
  • Register children for school. Public school is free, and children must be enrolled regardless of immigration status in most provinces. Bring proof of address, birth certificates and vaccination records. Ask about free language support programs for students.
  • Find a family doctor, or learn where your nearest walk-in clinic is. In many areas there are waiting lists for a family doctor, so get on one early.
  • Newcomer settlement services. Government-funded, free, and genuinely useful. They help with résumés, language classes, credential assessment and understanding local systems. Search for a settlement agency in your city.
  • Free language classes. LINC (English) and CLIC (French) classes are free for eligible permanent residents, and many locations offer free childcare while you study.

First three months: work and money

  • Get your credentials assessed if your profession is regulated — nursing, engineering, teaching, accounting, trades. This takes time, so start early. Some professions require additional Canadian exams or bridging programs.
  • Rewrite your résumé in the Canadian format. No photo, no age, no marital status, usually two pages maximum. Settlement agencies will help you do this for free.
  • Build credit carefully. Use a credit card for small purchases and pay the full balance every month. Credit history matters for renting, car loans and mortgages.
  • Understand your pay stub. Income tax, Canada Pension Plan and Employment Insurance are deducted automatically. Your take-home pay will be noticeably less than the advertised salary.

First year: the bigger things

  • File your taxes. The deadline is April 30 for the previous calendar year. File even if you earned nothing — benefits like the GST/HST credit and the Canada Child Benefit depend on it. Free tax clinics exist for people with modest incomes.
  • Apply for the Canada Child Benefit if you have children under 18. It is a monthly, tax-free payment.
  • Get a driver's licence, and ask whether your driving experience abroad can shorten the graduated licensing waiting period.
  • Prepare for your first winter before November, not during the first snowstorm.
  • Build a community. Libraries, community centres, places of worship and cultural associations are free and welcoming. Isolation is the hardest part of the first year, and it is the part people plan for least.

Looking ahead: citizenship

Permanent residents can generally apply for Canadian citizenship after being physically present in Canada for at least 1,095 days (three years) within the previous five years. Applicants between 18 and 54 must show language ability in English or French and pass the citizenship test — 20 questions, 15 correct to pass, all based on the official Discover Canada guide.

Start reading that guide long before you are eligible. Spreading the study over months is far easier than cramming, and much of it is genuinely interesting once you live here.
