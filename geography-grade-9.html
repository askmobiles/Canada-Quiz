📖 Canada's land and climate

Canada is huge, old and cold in places, shaped by ancient rock, ice sheets, mountains and water. Its landforms and climate decide where people can live and what they can do there.

Size

The second-largest country

Canada covers almost 10 million square kilometres, second only to Russia. It touches three oceans, the Pacific, the Atlantic and the Arctic, and has the longest coastline of any country, more than 240,000 km counting all its islands.

Landforms

Landform regions

Geographers divide Canada into landform regions: the Canadian Shield, the Western Cordillera, the Interior Plains, the Great Lakes–St. Lawrence Lowlands, the Appalachians, the Hudson Bay Lowlands and the Arctic lands. Each has its own rocks, soils and shapes.

The Shield

The Canadian Shield

The Shield covers about half of Canada, in a horseshoe around Hudson Bay. Its rock is among the oldest on Earth: rock about 300 km north of Yellowknife is about 4 billion years old. It has thin soil, countless lakes, forests and rich minerals.

Ice age

Shaped by ice

About 20,000 years ago, ice sheets up to about 3 kilometres thick covered almost all of Canada. As they moved and melted, they scraped the Shield bare, deepened the basins of the Great Lakes and left behind hills of gravel and thousands of lakes.

Mountains

The Western Cordillera

In the west, plates of the Earth's crust pushing together raised the Rocky Mountains and the Coast Mountains. Canada's highest peak, Mount Logan in Yukon, is 5,959 metres high. The coast of BC also has the most earthquakes in Canada.

Climate

What makes a climate

Climate depends on latitude, ocean currents, winds and air masses, elevation, mountains, and nearness to water. That is why Vancouver, by the Pacific, has mild, rainy winters, while Winnipeg, in the middle of the continent, has hot summers and very cold winters.

The North

Permafrost and tundra

Across much of the North the ground stays frozen for at least two years in a row; only a thin top layer thaws in summer. This is permafrost. North of the tree line is tundra, with low plants and no trees. As the climate warms, thawing permafrost can crack roads and buildings.

Water

Lakes, rivers and drainage basins

Canada has more lakes than any other country. Its rivers drain into five ocean drainage basins: the Pacific, the Arctic, the Atlantic, Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Mexico. The Mackenzie, flowing to the Arctic Ocean, is Canada's longest river.

Hazards

Natural hazards

Floods, wildfires, ice storms, droughts, hurricanes on the Atlantic coast and earthquakes in the west can all strike Canada. The 2023 wildfire season was the worst on record, and smoke from it drifted across North America.

📖 Resources and industries

Forests, fish, farmland, minerals, oil and rushing rivers built Canada's economy. Using them without using them up is one of the country's biggest questions.

Resources

Renewable or not

Renewable resources, such as forests, fish, water and wind, can grow back or be replaced if they are managed well. Non-renewable resources, such as oil, gas and minerals, run out once they are used up.

Jobs

Sectors of the economy

Primary industries take resources from nature: farming, fishing, forestry, mining. Secondary industries make things: factories and building. Tertiary industries provide services, from stores to hospitals. About four out of five Canadians work in services.

Forests

A forest country

Forests cover about 40% of Canada, and Canada holds about 9% of the world's forests. Most is the boreal forest, a wide band of spruce, pine and birch across the country. By law, forests harvested on public land must be regrown.

Energy

Oil and hydro

Canada has the third-largest proven oil reserves in the world, mostly in Alberta's oil sands. About 60% of Canada's electricity comes from hydro, from dams on big rivers in Quebec, BC, Manitoba, Ontario and Newfoundland and Labrador.

Fish

When the cod disappeared

For centuries the northern cod off Newfoundland seemed endless. Overfishing made the stock collapse, and in 1992 the government closed the fishery. About 30,000 people in Newfoundland and Labrador lost their jobs, the largest layoff in Canadian history. In 2024 a small commercial cod fishery reopened.

Farming

Farmland is rare

Only about 7% of Canada's land is farmland. The best is on the Prairies, where wheat and canola grow, and in southern Ontario and Quebec. Canola was developed by Canadian scientists in the 1970s; its name comes from "Canada" and "oil".

Mining

Minerals from the Shield

Mines in the Shield produce gold, nickel, copper and more. Around Sudbury, nickel is mined from a crater made by a meteorite about 1.8 billion years ago. On the Prairies, Saskatchewan has the world's largest potash deposits, used to make fertilizer.

Trade

Trade and responsible development

Almost three-quarters of Canada's exports go to the United States (72.5% in 2025). Before a big mine, pipeline or dam is approved, governments must study its effects on the environment, and must consult First Nations, Inuit or Métis communities whose rights could be affected.

📖 People and communities

Who lives in Canada, where, and how that is changing, and what makes a city or town a good place to live.

Population

Almost 42 million people

On July 1, 2026, Canada had 41,798,407 people, Statistics Canada estimates. With so much land, that is only about 4 people per square kilometre, one of the lowest population densities in the world.

Where we live

Close to the border, in cities

Two out of three Canadians live within 100 kilometres of the US border, on just 4% of the country's land (2016 census). Almost three out of four live in a large urban area, and Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver alone hold more than a third of all Canadians.

Immigration

Growth from immigration

Most of Canada's population growth comes from immigration, not births. In the 2021 census, about 23% of people in Canada were immigrants, the highest share in more than 150 years.

Ages

An aging population

Families have fewer children than in the past and people live longer, so Canada is getting older. In 2021 about 1 in 5 Canadians was 65 or older. Geographers show this with population pyramids, bar graphs of each age group.

Indigenous

First Nations, Inuit and Métis

In 2021, 1.8 million people in Canada were Indigenous, about 5% of the population. Indigenous people are younger on average than other Canadians, and many communities are working to protect their languages and their relationship with the land.

Cities

Sprawl or build up?

When cities spread outward onto farmland, it is called urban sprawl. Building more homes on land already in the city, close to transit, is called intensification. Ontario's Greenbelt, created in 2005, protects farms and forests around the Golden Horseshoe.

Getting around

Liveable communities

A liveable community has safe, affordable housing, clean air and water, parks, schools, jobs and shops close by, and ways to get around without a car, such as transit, bike lanes and sidewalks. Paved surfaces trap heat, so trees help cool cities in summer.

🗓️ Timeline

  • 1867 Confederation: four provinces form Canada
  • 1870 Manitoba and the Northwest Territories join Canada
  • 1871 British Columbia joins, with the promise of a railway
  • 1885 Banff's hot springs are protected, the start of Canada's national parks
  • 1898 Yukon becomes a territory during the gold rush
  • 1905 Alberta and Saskatchewan become provinces
  • 1947 Oil is struck at Leduc, Alberta
  • 1949 Newfoundland joins Canada
  • 1959 The St. Lawrence Seaway opens
  • 1962 The Trans-Canada Highway opens
  • 1992 The northern cod fishery is closed
  • 1997 The Confederation Bridge joins PEI to the mainland
  • 1999 Nunavut becomes a territory
  • 2005 Ontario creates the Greenbelt
  • 2023 Canada's worst wildfire season on record
