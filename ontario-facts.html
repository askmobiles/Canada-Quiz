Ontario, from the fact box to the hard questions

Ontario is two places. Nearly four in every ten Canadians live here, almost all of them in the southern strip along the Great Lakes. North of that strip is a vast area of forest, rock, rivers and small communities, reaching all the way to Hudson Bay — and it is most of the province by area. A project about Ontario that only describes the south has described a corner of it.

Where Ontario is

Ontario sits between Manitoba to the west and Quebec to the east. Hudson Bay and James Bay are to the north, and the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence River form most of the southern boundary.

Ottawa, the capital of Canada, is in Ontario, on the Quebec border. Toronto is the capital of the province. Having two capitals in one province confuses a lot of people, including adults.

What the land is like

Southern Ontario is low, worn-down land between the lakes, with deep soil and a long growing season — which is why so much of Canada's fruit and vegetable farming is here. Then the Canadian Shield begins: bare rock, thin soil, spruce and pine, and water everywhere.

Ontario's highest point is Ishpatina Ridge, according to Ontario Parks. The province's own about-Ontario page gives the highest elevation as 693 metres above sea level, in the Timiskaming area. No single official page gives the name and the height together, so this page gives you both, each with its own source.

How big is that, really?

  • Ontario's fresh water alone — 158,654 km² — covers more ground than New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island put together.
  • About 15% of the province is fresh water. Only Quebec and the Northwest Territories hold more of it by area — Ontario is third, just ahead of Nunavut.
  • Ontario is the fourth-largest province or territory but the most populous by a wide margin — more people than Quebec and British Columbia added together.

How people make a living

Ontario builds things and moves money. Car and truck plants, steel, food processing and machinery are concentrated in the southwest; banking, insurance, technology and media in and around Toronto; the federal public service in Ottawa. Mining and forestry carry much of the north.

Farming matters more than the province's reputation suggests. The strip along Lake Erie and the Niagara peninsula grows fruit, wine grapes and vegetables that would be hard to grow anywhere else in the country.

How Ontario became a province

Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee, Cree and other nations lived here long before any of the borders on today's map. Their trade routes ran along the same lakes and rivers the highways now follow, because water was the road.

The area was French, then British, then part of the Province of Canada with what is now Quebec. On 1 July 1867 Confederation split that province in two and Ontario became one of the four original provinces, alongside Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick.

Ontario's boundaries kept growing after that. The province did not always reach Hudson Bay; the far north was added later, which is part of why the north and south feel like different places with different histories.

Things most people do not know

  • Manitoulin Island, in Georgian Bay, is the world's largest freshwater island — 2,766 km², which is about half the size of Prince Edward Island.
  • Ontario's official gemstone is amethyst, adopted in 1975. Purple crystals come out of the rock near Thunder Bay.
  • Not all of Ontario is on the same clock. The province's own page and the federal time page agree that areas west of 90° west longitude are on Central Time, like Manitoba.
  • The provincial bird, the common loon, is the same bird on the Canadian one-dollar coin — which is why the coin is called a loonie.
  • The white trillium is protected by more than tradition: it has been Ontario's floral emblem since 1937, and it is a flower that takes years to reach blooming size.
  • Ontario has the capital of Canada inside it and is not itself Canada's capital — Ottawa belongs to the country, Toronto to the province.

Why the Great Lakes decided where the cities went

Before railways, heavy goods moved by water or not at all. The Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence form a navigable route from the Atlantic deep into the middle of the continent, and every major city in southern Ontario sits on that route or on a river feeding it.

The same route explains the border. Rather than a ruled line, the boundary with the United States runs through the middle of four of the five Great Lakes, because the lakes were the thing worth dividing.

Two figures that look like a contradiction

Ontario is the fourth-largest province or territory and the most populous. Nunavut is the largest and the least populous. Size and population have almost nothing to do with each other in Canada, and any project that treats them as the same measure will reach the wrong conclusion.

What they do track is growing season, soil and access to water transport. Ontario's population is concentrated exactly where all three are best, and thins out fast once any one of them fails.
