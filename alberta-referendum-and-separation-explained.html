🗳️ Alberta's 2026 referendum, explained

On 19 October 2026 Albertans voted on ten questions, and one of them was about separating from Canada. It does not ask what most people think it asks. Here is what is on the ballot, who put it there, what would actually have to happen, and what the treaties that cover the whole province have to do with it.

Last checked 21 October 2026. Albertans voted on 19 October 2026. The ballots are counted by hand: the unofficial results so far are just below, and the official results are set for 29 October 2026.

Jump to: What is on the ballot · The ten questions, one by one · The separation question · How it got here · What the polls say · How the vote works · What the law requires · The treaties · Who Albertans are

The result so far (unofficial). On Question 10, 75.1% voted for Alberta to remain a province of Canada (1,536,359 votes) and 24.9% voted to start the legal process toward a separation referendum (510,587 votes). That count covered 5,391 of the 5,575 voting areas and locations on the evening of 21 October 2026, in Elections Alberta's unofficial results. CBC News projected that Alberta voted to remain in Canada. Questions 1 to 9 are counted by hand after Question 10, and their counts were still incomplete on 21 October 2026, so they are not shown here yet. The official results are set for 29 October 2026.
The short version. Nobody voted to leave Canada on 19 October 2026. The separation question asks whether the Government of Alberta should begin the legal process toward a future binding referendum. It is a vote about whether to hold a vote. Nine other questions share the same ballot and have nothing to do with separation.

What is actually on the ballot

Ten questions. Nine were announced on 19 February 2026 and the tenth, on separation, was added by Premier Danielle Smith on 21 May 2026. The nine come in two kinds: five that Alberta could act on by itself, and four that would need the Constitution of Canada changed.

The five Alberta could act on itself

  • More provincial control over immigration.
  • Limiting provincially funded programs to citizens, permanent residents and people with approved status.
  • A twelve-month residency requirement before someone can claim social supports.
  • Charging non-permanent residents fees for health care and education.
  • Requiring proof of citizenship to vote.

The four that would need the Constitution changed

  • Alberta choosing its own King's Bench and Court of Appeal judges.
  • Abolishing the Senate.
  • Letting a province opt out of a federal program and receive the funding instead.
  • Giving provincial law priority over conflicting federal law in shared or provincial areas.

A referendum result does not change the Constitution. The four constitutional questions would each need agreement well beyond Alberta, and the amending formula decides how much.

The ten questions, one by one

Each question is on its own numbered ballot. Below is the official English wording as Elections Alberta publishes it, then what is true today and what would have to happen after a Yes. Elections Alberta does not interpret the questions; it sends questions about their meaning to the Government of Alberta.

Question 1 — more provincial control over immigration

“Do you support the Government of Alberta taking increased control over immigration for the purposes of decreasing immigration to more sustainable levels, prioritizing economic migration and giving Albertans first priority on new employment opportunities?”
  • Today: immigration is shared between Ottawa and the provinces, and where their laws conflict, federal law wins. Alberta nominates some newcomers through the Alberta Advantage Immigration Program, but the federal government makes the final permanent-residence decision.
  • After a Yes: no constitutional change is named, but a larger provincial role would most likely need an agreement with the federal government. Alberta cannot do it alone.

Question 2 — provincial programs only for some statuses

“Do you support the Government of Alberta introducing a law mandating that only Canadian citizens, permanent residents and individuals with an Alberta approved immigration status will be eligible for provincially-funded programs, such as health care, education and other social services?”
  • Today: eligibility already differs from program to program. Alberta health insurance, for example, covers some temporary residents with a work or study permit who plan to live in Alberta for at least 12 months.
  • After a Yes: Alberta would pass a provincial law. "Alberta approved immigration status" is not defined yet; the government has said it would define it after the vote. Lawyers have pointed to possible Charter and Canada Health Act questions.

Question 3 — a 12-month wait for social supports

“Assuming that all Canadian citizens and permanent residents continue to qualify for social support programs as they do now, do you support the Government of Alberta introducing a law requiring all individuals with a non-permanent legal immigration status to reside in Alberta for at least 12 months before qualifying for any provincially-funded social support programs?”
  • Today: some programs already exclude temporary residents. Health insurance asks whether you intend to stay 12 months; it is not a 12-month wait.
  • After a Yes: Alberta would pass a provincial law. If it reached health insurance, the Canada Health Act limits provincial waiting periods to three months.

Question 4 — a fee for health care and schools

“Assuming that all Canadian citizens and permanent residents continue to qualify for public health care and education as they do now, do you support the Government of Alberta charging a reasonable fee or premium to individuals with a non-permanent immigration status living in Alberta for their and their family's use of the healthcare and education systems?”
  • Today: there is no general fee of this kind. Emergency care cannot legally be refused to anyone.
  • After a Yes: the province would set the amounts by law or regulation. Charging insured patients for insured health services raises Canada Health Act questions.

Question 5 — proof of citizenship to vote

“Do you support the Government of Alberta introducing a law requiring individuals to provide proof of citizenship, such as a passport, birth certificate or citizenship card, to vote in an Alberta provincial election?”
  • Today: only Canadian citizens may vote. Voters prove who they are and where they live; a voter who is not yet registered signs a declaration that they are a citizen.
  • After a Yes: a change to Alberta's election law, which the province can make by itself, within the Charter's right to vote.

Question 6 — provinces choose superior court judges

“Do you support the Government of Alberta working with the governments of other willing provinces to amend the Canadian Constitution to have provincial governments, and not the federal government, select the justices appointed to provincial King's Bench and Appeal courts?”
  • Today: the federal government appoints these judges, under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, choosing from lists made by advisory committees that include provincial members.
  • After a Yes: a constitutional amendment under the general formula: Parliament plus at least seven provinces with at least half of Canada's population.

Question 7 — abolish the Senate

“Do you support the Government of Alberta working with the governments of other willing provinces to amend the Canadian Constitution to abolish the unelected federal Senate?”
  • Today: the Senate is one of the two houses of Parliament. Senators are appointed, not elected.
  • After a Yes: the hardest kind of amendment: Parliament and all ten provinces must agree. The Supreme Court said so in 2014.

Question 8 — opt out of federal programs and keep the money

“Do you support the Government of Alberta working with the governments of other willing provinces to amend the Canadian Constitution to allow provinces to opt out of federal programs that intrude on provincial jurisdiction such as health care, education, and social services, without a province losing any of the associated federal funding for use in its social programs?”
  • Today: Ottawa can attach conditions to the money it sends provinces and hold some back if they are not met. The Canada Health Act is the best-known example.
  • After a Yes: a constitutional amendment. The general formula (seven provinces with half the population) is the one most often cited.

Question 9 — provincial laws win in a conflict

“Do you support the Government of Alberta working with the governments of other willing provinces to amend the Canadian Constitution to better protect provincial rights from federal interference by giving a province's laws dealing with provincial or shared areas of constitutional jurisdiction priority over federal laws when the province's laws and federal laws conflict?”
  • Today: when a valid federal law and a provincial law truly conflict, the federal law wins. Courts call this federal paramountcy.
  • After a Yes: a constitutional amendment under the general formula: Parliament plus seven provinces with half the population.

Question 10 — remain, or start the process

The full wording is further down this page. On the ballot it is two options rather than Yes or No:

  • Option 1: “Alberta should remain a province of Canada.”
  • Option 2: “The Government of Alberta should commence the legal process required under the Canadian Constitution to hold a binding provincial referendum on whether or not Alberta should separate from Canada.”

Neither option changes Alberta's place in Canada on its own. Question 10 is counted first on voting night.

Which results are binding

  • Questions 1 to 5 and 10: the cabinet order that put them on the ballot says the results are not binding. They tell the government what voters think.
  • Questions 6 to 9: under Alberta's Referendum Act, a majority result binds the government that called the vote to take the steps within its own power. Alberta cannot change the Constitution alone, so a Yes obliges it to pursue an amendment, not to make one.
  • Since December 2025 the Act also says the government does not have to carry out a result that would break the Charter or Aboriginal and treaty rights.

The separation question, word for word

“Should Alberta remain a province of Canada, or should the Government of Alberta commence the legal process required under the Canadian Constitution to hold a binding provincial referendum on whether or not Alberta should separate from Canada?”

Read it twice. The choice is between remaining a province and starting a process — not between staying and leaving. Even on the second option, Alberta is still a province the next morning, and the binding referendum it points at does not yet exist.

How it got here

Alberta separatism is not new, and it has been measured before.

1905Alberta becomes a province. Treaties 6, 7 and 8 were already signed — they are older than the province.
1981The Western Canada Concept is founded, to take the four western provinces and the territories out of Canada.
1982Gordon Kesler wins the Olds-Didsbury by-election for the party. At the general election later that year it takes 11.8% of the vote and no seats, and Kesler loses his own.
May 2025Alberta lowers the bar for a citizen-initiated referendum petition to 10% of the votes cast at the previous election, down from 20% of eligible voters.
21 May 2026The government puts its own separation question on the ballot as the tenth.
24 May 2026The Court of King's Bench strikes down the separatist petition, finding the Chief Electoral Officer erred in letting it proceed. Consultation with Indigenous peoples is central to the ruling.
19 October 2026Albertans vote on all ten questions.

Two citizen petitions, neither on the ballot

The separation question is on the ballot because the government put it there, not because a petition succeeded. The separatist petition was struck down in May. A rival petition running the other way — Thomas Lukaszuk's Alberta Forever Canada, arguing for staying — was verified at 404,293 signatures and also did not make the ballot.

And the premier who put the question on the ballot says she wants Alberta to stay. Danielle Smith: “I support Alberta remaining in Canada … I also believe Albertans deserve a say.” She has also said there would be no second referendum if the remain side wins, for as long as she is premier.

How the vote works

Who can voteCanadian citizens, 18 or older on voting day, who ordinarily live in Alberta.
Advance voting13 to 17 October 2026, 9 a.m. to 8 p.m., at any advance location in your electoral division.
Voting dayMonday 19 October 2026, 9 a.m. to 8 p.m., at your assigned voting place.
IDOne government photo ID with your name and current address, or two pieces of ID with your name, one of them showing your address. You can register at the poll.
CountingBy hand, with Question 10 counted first. Elections Alberta posts unofficial results after the polls close; the official results are set for 29 October 2026.

What leaving Canada would actually require

There is no exit clause in the Constitution of Canada. Two documents govern what would happen, and both were written because of Quebec.

The Supreme Court's Secession Reference, 1998

  • A province cannot leave unilaterally, under Canadian law or international law.
  • If a province's people clearly express a will to leave, the rest of Canada comes under a duty to negotiate. A duty to negotiate is not a guarantee of a result.
  • Leaving would take a constitutional amendment, and the Constitution stays in force until the terms are agreed.
  • The Court listed what negotiations would have to cover — Indigenous peoples, minorities, the economy, the debt, and the borders.

The Clarity Act, 2000

  • The House of Commons decides, before a vote, whether the question is clear — and a question that does not refer solely to secession counts as unclear.
  • The House also decides afterwards whether a clear majority expressed itself. The Act sets no number.
  • Indigenous peoples are to be part of the negotiations, alongside every province.

The treaties — the part most coverage skips

Treaties 6, 7 and 8 cover essentially the whole of Alberta. Not a corner of it. And they were signed with the Crown before Alberta existed — the province was created in 1905.

After the court struck down the separatist petition in May 2026, Treaty 8 First Nations of Alberta set out the argument plainly: “Treaty rights were not created by Alberta” — they “flow from sacred agreements entered into with the Imperial Crown” and are protected by Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. Grand Chief Trevor Mercredi: “Consultation is not a courtesy. It is a legal duty and a matter of respect.”

The separatist side does not dispute that this is an obstacle. The Alberta Transition Council's own 214-page plan calls for consent-based negotiations with each First Nation individually, and accepts that some reserve land could remain Canadian territory.

Alberta has 45 First Nations and eight Métis Settlements covering 1.25 million acres.

What independence would mean in practice

  • Alberta has no coastline. Its oil reaches the sea through British Columbia. Today that is a matter between provinces; for a separate country every pipeline across British Columbia becomes an international agreement that the other side has to want to sign. The claim that international law guarantees a landlocked country access to tidewater is disputed, not settled.
  • Trade costs money. Economist Trevor Tombe's illustration: a 5% rise in trade costs would take roughly $20 billion off Alberta's economy.
  • Currency. The transition plan keeps the Canadian dollar at first, but access to Canadian payment systems and central-bank liquidity would have to be negotiated separately.
  • The debt. The plan proposes no final figure for Alberta's share of the federal debt, asking instead for a verified accounting of assets and liabilities.
  • Borders. Alberta would need its own customs and immigration service, and current trade access would not automatically carry over.

One number is worth setting beside the ancestry list. Among people who arrived in Alberta between 2016 and 2021, about one in four came from the Philippines and about one in six from India, then Nigeria at 5.1% and China at 4.9% — 63% from Asia and 18% from Africa. The ancestry table describes the Alberta that was built; the arrivals describe the one being built now.
