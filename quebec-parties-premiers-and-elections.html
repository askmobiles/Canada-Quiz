⚜️ Quebec's parties, premiers and elections

Quebec runs more of its own affairs than any other province, and it does it with a set of political parties that mostly exist nowhere else in Canada. Parties here go from government to nothing in a single election, and have done it three times. This is the reference: who the parties are, who has been premier, how every election since 1966 turned out, and what Quebec does differently.

How Quebec elects a government

The same basic machinery as every other province. Voters elect one member for each riding, the party that can command a majority of them forms the government, and its leader becomes premier.

Three differences are in the words used:

  • The chamber is the National Assembly, not a Legislative Assembly.
  • Its members are MNAs — members of the National Assembly — where Ontario says MPP and most provinces say MLA.
  • It had 127 seats at the election of 5 October 2026, up from 125. A majority is 64.

Quebec also abolished its appointed upper house, the Legislative Council, in 1968. Every province has a single chamber today; Quebec was the last to get rid of its second one.

The parties

Quebec's parties line up on two questions at once, not one. The first is the ordinary left-right question about taxes and services. The second is whether Quebec should be a country. A party can be anywhere on one and anywhere on the other, which is why the names here do not map onto federal politics.

Quebec Liberal Party (PLQ)

The oldest of them, and the only party to have contested every election since 1867 under essentially the same name. Federalist: it opposes Quebec independence. It has formed government more often than anyone else, and it is also the party that collapses hardest — 21 seats in 2022, back to 40 and Official Opposition in 2026.

A quirk worth knowing: Liberal support is concentrated in a compact set of Montreal-area ridings. In 2022 the party finished fourth in votes and second in seats. That is first-past-the-post doing what it does, and every province uses the same system.

Union Nationale (1935–1989)

Founded in 1935 as a coalition of the Conservative Party and the Action libérale nationale, led by Maurice Duplessis and Paul Gouin. It governed 1936–39, 1944–60 and 1966–70, and the long Duplessis years are a defining period in Quebec's history.

Then it vanished. It won 56 seats in 1966 and none in 1973. Support fell to about 4% by 1981 and about 1% by 1986, and the party was formally dissolved in 1989 after 54 years.

Parti Québécois (PQ, founded 1968)

Founded on 14 October 1968 from the merger of René Lévesque's Mouvement souveraineté-association and Gilles Grégoire's Ralliement national, with members of the dissolving Rassemblement pour l'indépendance nationale joining too.

Its purpose is Quebec independence — originally “sovereignty-association”, meaning independence while keeping an economic association with Canada. It is the party that held both referendums.

It has formed government six times: 1976, 1981, 1994, 1998, 2012 and 2026. Its seat count tells the clearest story on this page — 7 in 1970, then 71 and government in 1976; 3 in 2022, then 59 and government in 2026.

Action démocratique du Québec (ADQ, 1994–2012)

Won its first seat in 1994, spent a decade as a minor party, then broke through to 41 seats in 2007 and became the Official Opposition — pushing the Parti Québécois to third. It fell back to 7 seats in 2008 and merged into the Coalition Avenir Québec in January 2012, its four remaining members crossing over on 21 January after about 70% of its members approved.

Coalition Avenir Québec (CAQ, founded 2011)

Founded on 4 November 2011 by François Legault, a former Parti Québécois cabinet minister, and the businessman Charles Sirois. It absorbed the ADQ two months later.

Nationalist but not sovereignist: it argues for more autonomy for Quebec inside Canada and rules out holding a referendum. It won 19 seats in 2012, 22 in 2014, 74 and government in 2018, and 90 in 2022 — the largest majority in decades. At the election of 5 October 2026 it won none, on roughly one vote in eight by the preliminary count. From 90 seats to zero in one election.

Québec solidaire (QS)

Left-wing and sovereignist. It won its first seat in 2008, then 2, 3, 10, 11, and 9 at the election of 5 October 2026. Small, but in a legislature where no party holds a majority, nine seats can decide what passes.

Conservative Party of Quebec (PCQ)

Quebec had a Conservative party in the 1800s; the modern one is a separate revival. For years it took votes without winning anything — 12.91% of the vote and zero seats in 2022.

At the election of 5 October 2026 it won 19 seats, and its leader Éric Duhaime took Bellechasse. It was the first time a Conservative had been elected to Quebec's legislature since 1935 — 91 years.

Every premier of Quebec since 1867

The National Assembly's own list runs to 38 premierships since 1867, held by 33 different people. Five people served two separate, non-consecutive terms: Boucher de Boucherville, Taillon, Godbout, Duplessis and Bourassa.

Why lists disagree about “the 33rd premier”. That gap between 38 and 33 is the whole problem: count premierships and you get one number, count people and you get another. Canada settles the same question federally by counting people — Sir John A. Macdonald served two non-consecutive terms and is counted once, which is why Mark Carney is Canada's 24th prime minister and not the 25th. Counting people the same way, the premier elected in April 2026 was Quebec's 33rd and the premier elected on 5 October 2026 is its 34th.

What the columns show, read down rather than across:

  • Quebec changes governments completely, not gradually. The Liberals went from 23 seats to 99 in 1985. The CAQ went from 22 to 74 in 2018. The Parti Québécois went from 3 to 59 in 2026. Landslides are the normal shape here.
  • Parties die. The Union Nationale governed in 1966 and held nothing by 1973. The ADQ was the Official Opposition in 2007 and gone by 2012. The CAQ held 90 seats in 2022 and none in 2026.
  • Minority governments are rare, and short. Three of the seventeen elections in this table produced one: 2007, 2012 and 2026. The legislature elected in 2007 lasted 20 months and the one elected in 2012 lasted 18.
  • Votes and seats come apart badly. In 2022 the Conservatives took 12.91% of the vote and no seats, while the Liberals took 14.37% and 21 seats.
About the 2026 figures. The seat counts settled on election night and are given above. Where this page gives a 2026 percentage it says it comes from the preliminary count and rounds it. The official figures go on the Élections Québec page linked at the end.

What Quebec runs for itself

The voting is ordinary. What is unusual is how much Quebec runs for itself.

  • A different kind of law. Quebec's private law — contracts, property, family matters, wills — runs on the Civil Code of Québec, inherited from the French tradition. Every other province and territory uses common law, built from accumulated court decisions. Criminal law is federal and the same everywhere.
  • Its own income tax. Quebec residents are the only Canadians who file two income tax returns, one with Revenu Québec and one with the Canada Revenue Agency. The split dates from 14 January 1954, when Maurice Duplessis introduced the bill creating a provincial income tax after Quebec had left that field to Ottawa since 1945.
  • Its own pension plan. Workers pay into the Québec Pension Plan rather than the Canada Pension Plan. It is compulsory for workers 18 and over earning more than $3,500 a year.
  • Its own immigration selection. Under the Canada–Québec Accord, Quebec picks its own economic immigrants, the refugees it resettles from abroad, and humanitarian cases. Ottawa keeps family sponsorship, protected persons, and the admissibility checks for everyone. Quebec is the only province that receives an annual federal grant for settling newcomers — $726.7 million in 2023–24.
  • Its own parental insurance. The Québec Parental Insurance Plan replaces the federal maternity and parental benefits. It generally starts paying sooner and covers self-employed parents.
  • Compulsory drug coverage. Everyone permanently settled in Quebec must have prescription drug insurance at all times. If a workplace plan is available you must take it; only people with no private option join the public RAMQ plan. No other province requires this.
One thing that is often described wrongly: the 16.5% abatement. Quebec filers get a 16.5-percentage-point reduction of their basic federal income tax that exists nowhere else. It is easy to read that as a discount, and it is not. Finance Canada's own page explains it: in the 1960s Ottawa offered provinces the choice of running certain federal programs themselves, Quebec was the only one to take it, and so Ottawa abated 13.5 points of federal tax while Quebec raised its own tax by the same amount. A further 3 points came from the Youth Allowances program in 1964. Quebec gets tax room instead of cash transfers — the money does not disappear, it changes which government collects it.

How Montreal governs itself

Montreal is not run the way most Canadian cities are. It has three layers, not one.

  • Nineteen boroughs, each with its own mayor and council, handle what residents notice daily: local planning, garbage, parks, culture, housing, fire prevention.
  • City council — the mayor and 64 councillors, directly elected for four years — handles public security, the environment, intergovernmental relations and urban regeneration.
  • The agglomeration council covers the whole Island of Montreal: the city plus 15 separate municipalities such as Westmount, Côte Saint-Luc, Dorval and Pointe-Claire. It runs what cannot stop at a city line — police and fire, public transport, water, arterial roads, property assessment, municipal courts, social housing. Votes are weighted by population: about 87% Montreal, 13% everyone else.

The reason is history. The island's municipalities were merged into one city in 2002; 16 were reconstituted on 1 January 2006 after residents voted to leave. The shared services stayed shared, and the agglomeration council is what was left standing.

Montreal also votes separately from the province, on its own four-year cycle. At the municipal election of 2 November 2025, Soraya Martinez Ferrada of Ensemble Montréal was elected mayor with 43.40% of the vote, on a turnout of 37.07% — well below what a provincial election draws.

What Quebecers get, and what it costs

Set out plainly, with both halves of the trade.

  • Childcare at a set daily rate. Subsidized educational childcare costs $9.65 a day, indexed every 1 January, the same whoever you are and wherever in the province you live. Places are limited.
  • Parental leave on its own terms, through the Québec Parental Insurance Plan, including for self-employed parents.
  • Guaranteed prescription drug coverage, because it is compulsory and the public plan takes everyone with no private option.
  • A say over who settles in the province, plus provincially run French courses, credential recognition and job-market support for newcomers.
  • And the other side. Quebec's provincial income tax runs from 14% to 25.75%, among the highest in the country, and the 16.5-point federal abatement exists because Quebec collects that tax itself rather than receiving the money from Ottawa. The programs above are paid for by the people who use them.

Whether that is a good trade is a question Quebecers answer at the ballot box, and the table above shows they have answered it differently in different decades.
