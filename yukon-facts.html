Yukon, from the fact box to the hard questions

Canada's highest mountain, the biggest icefields outside the poles, a gold rush that brought fifty thousand people, and a territory that stopped changing its clocks. Every figure comes from a government source, and they are all listed at the bottom.

Yukon gave up daylight saving and never went back. The territory's own words: “In the Yukon, we do not ‘spring forward’ or ‘fall back’.” Since 2020 it has stayed on Yukon Standard Time, UTC−7, all year. For part of the year that puts Yukon on the same clock as British Columbia, and for the rest of it on the same clock as Alberta — without moving a single hand.

Where Yukon is

Yukon is the northwest corner of Canada. Alaska is to the west, British Columbia to the south, and the Northwest Territories to the east. The Beaufort Sea, part of the Arctic Ocean, touches a short stretch of its northern edge.

Its shape is a rough triangle, and almost the whole population lives in or near Whitehorse, in the south.

What the land is like

Yukon is mountains and plateau. The St. Elias Mountains in the southwest hold the highest ground in Canada, including Mount Logan at 5,959 metres. Parks Canada says Kluane holds the world's largest non-polar icefields — ice on a scale you otherwise have to go to Greenland or Antarctica to find.

The Yukon River runs through the middle of the territory and gave it its name. Much of Yukon escaped the last ice age: the ice sheets did not cover the whole of it, which is why some of the oldest evidence of people in North America has been found here.

How big is that, really?

  • Yukon is bigger than Newfoundland and Labrador, and about eighty-five times the size of Prince Edward Island.
  • Only about 1.7% of Yukon is fresh water, the smallest share of any province or territory except Prince Edward Island — because the water here is frozen into icefields rather than sitting in lakes.

How people make a living

Mining is still the biggest industry, as it has been since the gold rush — gold, silver, zinc, copper. Government is the other large employer: a territory this size with this few people needs the same schools, roads, courts and hospitals as anywhere else, and that work is mostly public.

Tourism is the third leg. Kluane's mountains, the Dempster Highway, the northern lights and the Klondike history bring visitors in numbers larger than the resident population.

How Yukon became a territory

First Nations have lived here for thousands of years — among them the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, the Champagne and Aishihik First Nations, the Vuntut Gwitchin, the Kluane First Nation, the Teslin Tlingit and others. Yukon is notable today for having settled modern self-government agreements with most of its First Nations, which is unusual in Canada.

In 1896 gold was found in the Klondike. Parks Canada describes the Klondike Gold Rush of 1897–8, when some 50,000 gold-seekers stampeded north, and says more than $500 million worth of gold was ultimately taken from the frozen ground. Dawson City went from almost nothing to one of the largest towns west of Winnipeg, and then back down again.

Yukon was made a separate territory on 13 June 1898, in the middle of the rush — it existed because of the gold, and because Canada wanted a government in place before fifty thousand strangers organised one themselves.

Things most people do not know

  • Mount Logan, at 5,959 metres, is the highest point in Canada — and it is in Yukon, not in the Rockies.
  • Parks Canada says Kluane National Park holds the world's largest non-polar icefields.
  • Yukon's clocks have not changed since 2020. It is the only place in Canada that switched to permanent daylight-style time and stayed there.
  • The official bird is the raven — chosen in 1985, and a bird that stays all winter when most others leave.
  • Yukon has more people than the Northwest Territories, even though the Northwest Territories is nearly three times as big.
  • Yukon has no official motto. Neither do the Northwest Territories. Every province does.

Why a territory and not a province

Provinces get their powers directly from the Constitution. Territories get theirs from federal laws that Parliament could, in principle, change. In practice Yukon has been handed control of most things a province runs — schools, health, land and resources — through a process called devolution, so the day-to-day difference is much smaller than the legal one.

The other half of the answer is self-government agreements with Yukon First Nations, which created governments that are neither federal nor territorial. Yukon is one of the places in Canada where that arrangement is furthest along, and a project on Yukon government that describes only the territorial legislature is describing part of the picture.

The part of Canada the ice missed

During the last ice age most of Canada lay under kilometres of ice. Parts of Yukon did not, because the mountains to the south and west caught the moisture that would have fed an ice sheet. That unglaciated ground is called Beringia, and it connected to Alaska and Siberia across land that is now under the sea.

Because the ice never scraped it clean, Yukon preserves things the rest of the country lost: ancient bones, old volcanic ash layers useful for dating, and some of the earliest traces of people in North America. Geology decided what history survived.
