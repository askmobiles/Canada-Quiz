The driving test in Canada, province by province

Driving is run by each province and territory, not by Canada, so the name of your licence, the age you can start, and what the written test even looks like all change when you cross a border. This page puts all thirteen side by side.

Why is it Class 5 in one province and Class 7 in another?

This is the question newcomers ask most, and the answer is simpler than it looks. In western Canada, licences are numbered by the kind of vehicle you are allowed to drive. Class 1 is a tractor-trailer. Class 2 is a bus. Class 3 is a large truck. Class 4 is a taxi or an ambulance. Class 5 is an ordinary car. Class 6 is a motorcycle. So Class 5 is not a stage of anything: it is simply the licence for the car in your driveway.

Class 7 is different. It is not a seventh kind of vehicle. It is an administrative label that British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan and several eastern provinces invented for the learner stage of driving a Class 5 car. Holding a Class 7 means you are learning to drive the same vehicle a Class 5 driver drives, with a supervisor beside you and a list of restrictions.

Three places never adopted that idea. Manitoba keeps the number 5 the whole way and adds a letter instead: 5L while you learn, 5I in the middle, 5F when you are finished. Newfoundland and Labrador does something similar with Class 5 Level I and Level II. Ontario ignores numbers altogether for car drivers and uses G1, G2 and G. Quebec uses words rather than codes: a learner's licence, then a probationary licence, then a Class 5 licence.

So if a friend tells you they passed their Class 7 and you are being sent to write a 5L or a G1, you are both doing the same thing. Different provinces, different names, one idea: a supervised beginner licence that you earn by passing a written test on road signs and the rules of the road.

Six of the thirteen do not publish how many questions they ask. That is not a gap in our research: those governments genuinely do not say. Every practice site that prints a confident number for Saskatchewan, Manitoba, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, Yukon, the Northwest Territories or Nunavut took it from somewhere other than the government.

What is the same everywhere

Every province and territory uses graduated licensing, and it always has the same shape. You pass a written test on signs and rules, and you get a beginner licence that only works with an experienced driver beside you. After a set number of months you pass a road test and you may drive alone, still with restrictions. After more months and a harder road test, the restrictions end.

Zero alcohol is universal for beginners. Not under the legal limit, zero. So is a vision test before your first licence, and so are the road signs themselves: signs are standard across Canada, which is why studying signs is the part of your work that travels with you if you move.

A licence from another country matters too. Most provinces will exchange a full licence from a country they have an agreement with, and will give credit for your experience even where there is no agreement, which can move you straight to the road test. Ask before you start at the bottom: people lose a year by not asking.

What is different where you live

One paragraph each on the thing that catches people out in that province, and the official book the real test is written from.

Ontario

Ontario is the one province that never used the class numbers for new drivers. G1 means learner, G2 means you may drive alone with limits, and G is the full licence. The knowledge test is not timed at all, and DriveTest does not publish how many questions it asks. Your accompanying driver must have held a full licence for four years and must be the only other person in the front seat.

How the test is split: Two or three sections. You rewrite only the sections you failed.

Official MTO Driver's Handbook

Quebec

Quebec is the one place where you do not go to a government office to sit the written test. It is Module 5 of the mandatory driving course and you take it at the driving school. The zero-alcohol rule for a learner or probationary licence holder never expires with age: it applies for as long as you hold that permit, whether you are 17 or 47.

How the test is split: Three parts, each with its own pass mark.

Guide de la route (SAAQ)

Practise for this test

British Columbia

British Columbia publishes more about its test than anywhere else: fifty questions, forty to pass, forty-five minutes. Two rules change on 19 October 2026. The minimum age for a supervising driver drops from 25 to 22, and the passenger limit for novice drivers loosens. If you read an older guide, check the date on it.

How the test is split: One test.

Learn to Drive Smart (ICBC)

Practise for this test

Alberta

Alberta lets you sit the knowledge test at fourteen, younger than anywhere else in Canada. The stage after the learner licence keeps the number 5 but carries the tag GDL, and it lasts two years.

How the test is split: One test.

Driver's Guide to Operation, Safety and Licensing

Practise for this test

Saskatchewan

SGI publishes none of the three numbers people most want: not the question count, not the pass mark, not a time limit. What it does publish is more useful than it sounds. The test is two tests, they are marked separately, and a fail on one means you rewrite only that one. You may sit it once a day, and you are allowed to ask the examiner what a question means.

How the test is split: Two separate tests, Basic and Sign, marked separately. You rewrite only the one you failed.

Saskatchewan Driver's Handbook (SGI)

Practise for this test

Manitoba

Manitoba is the province that never invented a Class 7 at all. The learner keeps the number 5 and gains a letter: 5L for learner, 5I for intermediate, 5F for full. Manitoba Public Insurance publishes a thirty-minute time limit but not a question count or a pass mark.

How the test is split: One test.

Manitoba Driver's Handbook (MPI)

Practise for this test

Nova Scotia

Nova Scotia has a third stage that almost nobody else has. After the novice period comes a Restricted Individual stage, marked Condition 47 on the licence, that lasts at least two more years. The written test can be taken online.

How the test is split: Two tests, rules and signs, taken and paid for separately.

Nova Scotia Driver's Handbook

Practise for this test

New Brunswick

New Brunswick was the first province to put the whole written test online. There is a catch worth planning around: after you pass online you must still wait at least four business days before you can do the vision test in a Service New Brunswick office.

How the test is split: Separate sign recognition and rules of the road exams.

New Brunswick Driver's Handbook

Prince Edward Island

Prince Edward Island runs three stages of a year each, so the full programme is three years unless you take a certified course. Your supervising driver needs four years of experience, the highest bar in Atlantic Canada alongside Newfoundland. Driving schools may give the knowledge exam themselves.

How the test is split: Not published as separate sections.

PEI Driver's Handbook

Newfoundland and Labrador

Newfoundland and Labrador asks for 85 percent, the highest published pass mark in the country. It is also the only province that publishes a pass mark but not a question count. The test is available online in English, and in person in French and Ukrainian.

How the test is split: One knowledge test covering rules, safe driving and signs.

Road Users Guide (Service NL)

Yukon

Yukon starts youngest in the country at fifteen, along with the other two territories, and it is stricter than most once you have the licence: driving only between 5 a.m. and midnight, an L sign on the vehicle, no phone at all even hands-free, and fifty logged hours of practice before the road test. There are eleven Motor Vehicles offices across the territory.

How the test is split: A sign test and a knowledge test.

Yukon Driver's Basic Handbook

Northwest Territories

The Northwest Territories marks its middle stage with a P for probationary, so the licence reads Class 5P. A learner may not drive between 11 p.m. and 6 a.m. and may carry no passenger other than the supervising driver.

How the test is split: One exam covering safe driving, the law and road signs.

NWT Basic Manual

Nunavut

Nunavut is the one place with a restricted licence built for people who never drive outside the territory: a B endorsement for Nunavut only, or a G endorsement for within 100 kilometres of your home community. Testing is by appointment at Motor Vehicles offices in Iqaluit, Rankin Inlet and Gjoa Haven, with a liaison officer programme for other communities.

How the test is split: Rules of the road and safe driving, plus sign identification.

Nunavut Driver's Manual

Why some cells say “not published”

A number on this page decides whether somebody books a test, takes a day off work and pays a fee. We are not willing to invent one. When we could not find a question count, a pass mark or a time limit on the government's own pages, the cell says so.

Ontario is the clearest example. Almost every practice site states a question count for the G1 test. Neither ontario.ca nor DriveTest publishes one. What DriveTest does publish is that you need 80 percent overall, that the test is split into two or three sections, that you rewrite only the sections you failed, and that the test is not timed at all.

Where our own practice tests use a question count and a timer, it is our standard, not a copy of the real one, and the page says which is which. Practising against a clock is useful even when the real test does not have one.

Rules change. British Columbia's supervising driver age drops from 25 to 22 on 19 October 2026, and there will be others. Check the official book for your province before you book, and treat this page as the map rather than the law.
