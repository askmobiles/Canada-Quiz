🎖️ Remembrance Day Quiz

Why Canadians stop at 11 o'clock on November 11: a short lesson for classes and families, with a quiz, endless practice, a challenge round, and printable worksheets with answer sheets. No accounts, nothing to download.

For classes: made for the days before November 11, from about Grade 4 to Grade 10. It fits the Canadian history taught in those grades, the First and Second World Wars in particular. 📖 The First World War, 1914–1918

Canada was a young country of eight million people when the war began. More than 650,000 Canadians and Newfoundlanders served, and more than 66,000 never came home.

1914

Canada goes to war

When Britain declared war on Germany on August 4, 1914, Canada was at war too, as part of the British Empire. Thousands of volunteers lined up to join.

1915

In Flanders Fields

In May 1915, near Ypres in Belgium, John McCrae, an army doctor from Guelph, Ontario, wrote the poem In Flanders Fields after a friend was killed. Red poppies grew among the soldiers' graves.

1916

Beaumont-Hamel

On July 1, 1916, the Newfoundland Regiment attacked at Beaumont-Hamel in France. Of about 800 men who went forward, only 68 answered the roll call the next morning. Newfoundland and Labrador still marks July 1 as Memorial Day.

1916

No. 2 Construction Battalion

Many Black Canadians who tried to enlist were turned away. In July 1916 the No. 2 Construction Battalion was formed in Nova Scotia, the only mostly Black battalion in Canada's history. Canada apologized for their treatment in 2022.

1917

Vimy Ridge

From April 9 to 12, 1917, all four divisions of the Canadian Corps attacked together for the first time and captured Vimy Ridge in France. Nearly 3,600 Canadians were killed.

1914–1918

Francis Pegahmagabow

An Anishinaabe soldier from Wasauksing First Nation in Ontario, he won the Military Medal three times and is one of the most highly decorated Indigenous soldiers in Canadian history.

1914–1918

Nursing sisters

Thousands of Canadian women served as nursing sisters, caring for the wounded in hospitals and in medical units, some close to the front lines.

1918

The armistice

The fighting stopped at 11 a.m. on November 11, 1918: the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. That is why Canadians are silent at 11 o'clock.

📖 The Second World War and after

More than one million Canadians served in the Second World War. Since then, Canadians have served in Korea, as peacekeepers around the world, and in Afghanistan.

1939

Canada decides for itself

This time Canada declared war on its own, on September 10, 1939, a week after Britain. More than one million Canadians served, and more than 45,000 died.

1942

Dieppe

On August 19, 1942, nearly 5,000 Canadian soldiers took part in a raid on the French port of Dieppe. More than 900 were killed and nearly 2,000 were taken prisoner.

1944

D-Day

On June 6, 1944, about 14,000 Canadians landed on Juno Beach in Normandy, France, one of five beaches in the largest landing from the sea in history.

1945

Tulips for Canada

Canadian soldiers played a leading part in freeing the Netherlands in 1945. The Dutch royal family sent tulips to Ottawa to say thank you, and tulips still arrive every year.

1940s–1950s

Tommy Prince

An Ojibwe soldier from Manitoba, Tommy Prince served in the Second World War and in Korea and became one of Canada's most decorated Indigenous soldiers.

1950–1953

The Korean War

More than 26,000 Canadians served in the Korean War, and 516 died.

1956

Peacekeeping

During the Suez Crisis, Lester B. Pearson proposed a United Nations force to keep the peace. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1957, and Canadians have served as peacekeepers in many countries since.

2001–2014

Afghanistan

More than 40,000 members of the Canadian Armed Forces served in Afghanistan, and 158 of them died.

📖 How Canada remembers

The poppy, the two minutes of silence and the ceremonies at every cenotaph honour everyone who has served Canada, and the more than 118,000 who died in service.

1931

From Armistice Day to Remembrance Day

Canada first marked Armistice Day in 1919. In 1931 Parliament fixed the day on November 11 and named it Remembrance Day.

1921

The poppy

Canada adopted the poppy as its flower of remembrance in 1921. The Royal Canadian Legion runs the poppy campaign, and the donations help veterans. It is worn on the left side, close to the heart.

November 11

Two minutes of silence

At ceremonies, a bugle plays the Last Post, then everyone keeps two minutes of silence at 11 o'clock, and Reveille ends the silence. Wreaths are laid at the cenotaph.

Every year

The Silver Cross Mother

Each year the Royal Canadian Legion chooses a mother whose child died serving Canada. She lays a wreath at the national ceremony in Ottawa for all Canadian mothers who lost a child.

2000

The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

In 2000 an unknown Canadian soldier of the First World War was brought home from a cemetery near Vimy Ridge and buried at the National War Memorial in Ottawa.

Every year

The Books of Remembrance

In the Peace Tower on Parliament Hill, the Books of Remembrance list more than 118,000 Canadians who died in military service. A page is turned every day.

1994

Indigenous Veterans Day

Thousands of First Nations, Inuit and Métis people served Canada in war. Since 1994, November 8 has been a day to honour them.

About this quiz

This page explains Remembrance Day for students and families: the First World War and the armistice, In Flanders Fields and the poppy, Vimy Ridge and Beaumont-Hamel, the Second World War, Korea, peacekeeping and Afghanistan, and how Canada remembers today. It includes Black and Indigenous soldiers and the nursing sisters, and every number comes from Veterans Affairs Canada.

It works six ways. Read the lesson and its timeline, take the ten-question quiz, practise for as long as you like with no score, try the harder challenge round, then print a worksheet and its answer sheet. There are three versions, lettered A, B and C, and the answer sheet with the same letter matches its worksheet exactly.

Good to know

  • The fighting of the First World War stopped at 11 a.m. on November 11, 1918, which is why Canadians keep two minutes of silence at 11 o'clock.
  • The Books of Remembrance on Parliament Hill list more than 118,000 Canadians who died in military service.
