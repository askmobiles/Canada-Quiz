🗣️ Canadian Slang: Words and Sayings You Will Hear in Canada

Toque, double-double, loonie, keener, chirping and more — a friendly guide to Canadian slang for newcomers, students and visitors.

Canadian English mostly sounds familiar, and then somebody asks if you want a double-double and you have no idea what just happened. Here is the everyday vocabulary that catches newcomers out, gathered in one place.

Clothing and weather

  • Toque (rhymes with "duke") — a knitted winter hat. This is the classic one. Nobody in Canada says "beanie".
  • Runners — running shoes or sneakers.
  • Bunny hug — a hooded sweatshirt, but only in Saskatchewan. Everywhere else it is a hoodie, and Saskatchewan finds this hilarious.
  • Snowbird — a Canadian, usually retired, who spends the winter somewhere warm like Florida or Arizona.
  • Freezing rain — rain that turns to ice the moment it lands. It is as bad as it sounds.

Food and drink

  • Double-double — a coffee with two creams and two sugars. Usually said at a drive-through window at high speed.
  • Timmies — Tim Hortons, the coffee and doughnut chain that is everywhere.
  • Timbits — small round doughnut holes, sold by the box for meetings and kids' sports.
  • Pop — a soft drink or soda.
  • Mickey and two-four — a small bottle of liquor, and a case of 24 beers. The Victoria Day long weekend in May is nicknamed the May two-four.
  • Poutine — fries, cheese curds and gravy. Non-negotiable.

Money and shopping

  • Loonie — the one-dollar coin, named after the loon on it.
  • Toonie — the two-dollar coin.
  • The Bay — the Hudson's Bay Company department store.
  • Canadian Tire money — coupon-like bills from Canadian Tire that many families still keep in a kitchen drawer.
  • Homo milk — homogenized whole milk. It is written that way on the carton and it surprises absolutely everyone once.

People and attitude

  • Keener — someone who is very eager, especially a student who always does extra work.
  • Hoser — a gently mocking word for a clumsy, unsophisticated person.
  • Chirping — teasing or trash-talking, especially in hockey.
  • Give'r — to go all out, put in full effort.
  • Beauty — used as praise, for a person or a thing. "He's a beauty."
  • The 6ix — Toronto.

Places and getting around

  • Out east / out west / up north / down south — Atlantic Canada, the western provinces, the territories, and the United States.
  • The Rock — Newfoundland. Someone from there is a Newfoundlander.
  • Klick — a kilometre.
  • Washroom — the bathroom or restroom. This is the polite, standard word in Canada.
  • Hydro — electricity, and your electricity bill, because so much Canadian power comes from hydroelectric dams.

And yes, "eh"

It is real, though not as constant as television suggests. It usually turns a statement into a gentle question or invites agreement: "Cold out today, eh?" It is friendly, informal and completely harmless. Nobody expects you to use it.

One last note about politeness

The reputation for apologizing is largely deserved. Canadians say sorry when someone bumps into them. It usually means "excuse me" or "that was awkward", not an admission of fault. Ontario even has a law clarifying that an apology is not automatically an admission of legal liability — which tells you something about how often it happens.
