🚗 Why Ontario car insurance costs what it costs

Two people can have the same car, the same clean record and the same years licensed, and pay hundreds of dollars a year apart. Sometimes they live on the same street. And a driver with ten clean years can still open a renewal that is higher than last year's. All of that has a published explanation. Here it is, with the numbers and the dates.

Last checked 29 September 2026 — insurance rules and rates change every few months, so check the official pages linked at the end before you act on this

What you are actually buying

In Ontario the policy sits on the vehicle, not on the person. Everyone in the household who drives it is listed on it, and the price is built from all of them together — plus the address where the car is kept overnight.

That is not an Ontario invention. Insurance is sold this way across Canada. Four provinces — British Columbia, Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Quebec — run part or all of their auto insurance through a government body. The rest, Ontario included, buy from private companies. Either way, the address the car sleeps at is part of the price.

What the law actually asks

No insurance company in Ontario can simply decide what to charge you. Each one files its rates with the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) and cannot use them until they are approved.

The test is in the Automobile Insurance Rate Stabilization Act, section 3(5). An approved rate must be just and reasonable in the circumstances, reasonably predictive of risk, able to fairly distinguish between risks, and not so low that the company cannot pay its claims.

The second line is the whole story. The law asks whether a factor predicts what a group will cost. It does not ask whether the factor is something you chose, something you can change, or something you are responsible for.

There are limits. Under the Insurance Act, section 238(4), an insurer's rules cannot be subjective, arbitrary, unrelated to the risk, or contrary to public policy. Credit information is not allowed for auto insurance in Ontario. Nothing that is a prohibited ground under the Ontario Human Rights Code may be used, and FSRA's guidance goes further, telling insurers not to use data that acts as a stand-in for those grounds either. Where you live is not on the banned list.

Why one side of a street can cost more than the other

Insurers do not price every postal code separately. They group them into territories, and the rules for drawing those groups come from a FSRA bulletin dated 31 January 2005:

  • no more than 55 territories in the province, and no more than 10 inside Toronto
  • a new territory needs at least three years of the company's own data and at least 2,500 vehicles a year
  • territories must be contiguous — one connected piece of ground
  • a newly split territory's rate cannot sit more than 10 per cent away from the territory beside it

A territory has to end somewhere. Wherever that line falls, there are houses on both sides of it.

FSRA's chief executive, Mark White, said so plainly in 2024: people “move literally across the street from one postal code territory to another, and they can see a significant increase in rates.” He also said FSRA would not ban the practice, because “location is one of the primary determinants of your risk as an insured driver,” and because removing it shifts cost onto drivers in cheaper areas.

How big the gap is

FSRA publishes the average premium actually paid. As of October 2025:

Where the car is keptAverage annual premium
Greater Toronto Area$2,810
Other urban Ontario$2,076
Rural Ontario$1,740
Ontario overall$2,164

The distance between the GTA line and the rural line is $1,070 a year — about $89 a month — before anything personal about the driver is counted. Those are averages of everyone, so gaps between two specific addresses run both wider and narrower.

Claim costs in dense areas are higher for countable reasons: more cars in less space, higher repair and towing costs, and theft. The Insurance Bureau of Canada reported Ontario auto theft claims at $485 million in 2025, down from $723 million in 2024. Toronto, Brampton and Mississauga were the three costliest cities for auto theft in both years. That figure cuts both ways, and it is worth holding onto: the cost driving GTA rates fell by roughly a third in a single year.

The Auditor General of Ontario put the same driver and the same vehicle through two cities and published what came back.

Nearly three times, for one person and one car. The audit also recorded that the territorial framework had not been updated since 2005, and that the average premium rose almost 14 per cent between 2017 and 2021, to $1,642.

What a premium measures, and what it does not

A high territory rate is often read as a verdict on the people who live there. It is worth being precise about what the number is actually counting, because a premium is a claim-cost score, not a driving-skill score, and the two come apart in ways that are measurable.

Take the most-discussed example. In 2021 Brampton's claim frequency ran about 25 per cent above the provincial average and claim severity about 30 per cent above. By 2023 the average premium there was $2,707 against $1,744 province-wide. Those gaps are real. What produced them is the interesting part.

A claim is not always a crash

Brampton was the second costliest city in Ontario for auto theft in both 2024 and 2025. A car taken from a driveway at three in the morning is a claim, it is priced into the territory, and there is no driver in it at all. A meaningful part of the territory's cost is that, and it says nothing about how anyone drives.

How far people drive is not in the price the way you would expect

Insurers rate on claims per vehicle, not per kilometre. So a place where people commute long distances, with limited transit and 400-series highways on every side, will show more claims per vehicle than a place where people drive a third as far — even if every driver in both places is equally capable. The industry's own account of Brampton leans on exactly this: commute distance, the share of driving done at highway speed, and the cost of the injury claims that result.

Severity works the same way. The same moment of inattention costs far more at 100 km/h on a highway than at 50 on a residential street. That is a fact about where the driving happens, not about who is doing it.

What cannot be tested, and what can

A common explanation for a fast-growing city's rates is that newer arrivals or students are driving the numbers. That cannot be tested from any published data. No Ontario collision dataset and no insurance dataset records immigration status, years in Canada, or whether someone is a student. It is not collected, so it cannot be shown — and insurers are not pricing it, because they do not have it either.

What insurers do use, and what is measurable, is years licensed. Someone who passed their road test in 2023 is priced as a new driver whether they were born in the city or arrived last year, and a Canadian-born nineteen-year-old with three years on a licence is priced the same way. A city that grew from 656,480 people in 2021 to roughly 791,486 by 2025 will naturally hold more recently licensed drivers. That is the real, countable version of the intuition, and the factor is licence history — not origin.

The gap between a territory's premium and its collision record is filled by theft, by distance driven, and by the cost of injury claims. A premium is what claims cost in a place. It is not a score for the people who live there.

Why the price rises even when your driving improves

A driver gets a year older, adds another clean year, changes nothing — and the renewal is higher. Then the reasons given sound like they contradict each other: the car is too new, the car is too old, parts cost more, sensors cost more, the postal code, the city. None of those are lies. They are also not really about you.

There are two dials, and only one is yours

Your dial is everything personal: record, years licensed, years claims-free, age band, the vehicle. Over a clean year it usually moves in your favour. The company's dial is its whole rate level, filed with FSRA and applied across every driver it insures. It moves for repair costs, parts supply, theft, court awards, reinsurance and weather — none of which are about how you drove.

If the company's dial moves up seven per cent and yours moves down two, your bill goes up five. Both facts are true at the same time. The reasons you were given describe the pool you are in, not the driver you are.

The number in the news is an average, not your number

When a rate change is approved, it is an average across that company's whole book of drivers. A company approved for an average of five per cent may be raising one group fifteen and lowering another three, because it re-priced some segments and not others. That is why your increase can be far larger than the figure you read, without anyone having done anything improper.

Your car's rate group moves even though your car did not

Canadian insurers rate vehicles through CLEAR — Canadian Loss Experience Automobile Rating. It scores a specific model-year by how often that exact vehicle actually gets into claims, what those claims cost, and how often it is stolen. The data is deliberately normalised to strip out territory and driving record, so it measures the car and nothing else.

  • It is updated every year. The same car, unchanged in your driveway, can move to a different rate group because last year's claims experience for that model-year came in differently. Year-to-year movement is capped, so it drifts rather than jumps.
  • It is model-year and trim specific. Two cars with the same name on the back can sit in different groups.

So “your car is new” and “your car is old” can both be real explanations, in different years, for the same car — because neither is actually about age. Both are shorthand for where that vehicle landed in CLEAR this year.

Why the same car costs a different amount for two people

  • Different insurer. Each files its own rate tables and draws its own territories inside FSRA's limits, so two companies can place the same address in different groups.
  • Different trim or engine, which means a different CLEAR group.
  • Different discounts — bundling home and auto, telematics, employer, alumni and group plans.
  • Different deductibles and optional coverages. A cheaper policy is sometimes a smaller one.
  • Different people on the policy. Everyone in the household who drives is counted, not just the person paying.

And getting older stops helping at some point. The steep drops come through the twenties. After that the curve flattens, and in the older age bands it can turn back up.

What the numbers say right now

The 2026 picture is genuinely mixed, and both halves matter. Applied Systems measured Canadian personal auto premiums up 21.8 per cent year over year in the second quarter of 2026 — Alberta +22.6%, Atlantic +9.4%, Ontario +7.0%, Quebec +0.9%. At the same time, FSRA approved rate reductions at nine Ontario insurers effective between August and November 2026, ranging from 0.23 to 5.3 per cent.

Both are real. Approved rate levels and what lands on a renewal are not the same number: the amount you pay also moves with vehicle rate groups, coverage changes and re-segmentation, and nine insurers are not the whole market.

Why Quebec drivers pay about half

This is the comparison people reach for, and the gap is real. Quebec does not do the same thing more cheaply. Quebec does a different thing, and the difference is written in one sentence of law.

The split

Quebec runs a blended plan. Private insurers cover property damage — your vehicle, and your liability for damage to someone else's. The Société de l'assurance automobile du Québec (SAAQ) covers bodily injury, on a no-fault basis, funded not by a premium but by charges built into the driver's licence and the vehicle registration.

The sentence that does the work

Section 83.57 of Quebec's Automobile Insurance Act: “Compensation under this title stands in lieu of all rights and remedies by reason of bodily injury and no action in that respect shall be admitted before any court of justice.”

In Quebec you cannot sue for bodily injury from a car accident. The Supreme Court of Canada confirmed how far that reaches in Godbout v. Pagé (2017), holding that it can bar claims against third parties such as a hospital or police where there is a plausible, logical and sufficiently close link to the accident.

Bodily injury is the biggest, slowest and most contested part of a car insurance claim. Quebec took it out of the private market and out of the courts at the same time. That is the source of the gap.

What it adds up to

A Quebec driver pays a private premium for property damage, plus two SAAQ contributions. An Ontario driver pays one premium for everything.

What is paidAmount
Quebec — private premium, property damage (2025 average)$1,067
Quebec — SAAQ contribution on a class 5 licence, no demerit points, 2026 (75% rebate applied)$21.54
Quebec — SAAQ contribution on a passenger vehicle registration, 2026$66.01
Quebec — roughly, all in$1,155
Ontario — one premium, everything (2025 average)$2,117

The extra that Quebec adds to the licence and the registration is small — under $90 a year for a clean-record driver in 2026. It does not close the gap, and it is not meant to.

And Quebec's injury coverage is not the smaller one

This is the part that surprises people who assume cheaper must mean thinner. The SAAQ income replacement indemnity pays 90 per cent of net income, on maximum insurable earnings of $94,000 (2024 onward), and can continue to age 68 in some circumstances.

Ontario's standard income replacement benefit pays 70 per cent of gross income to a maximum of $400 a week, for up to 104 weeks — and since 1 July 2026 it is optional, so a driver who does not select it has none at all.

So the Quebec system is cheaper and, on this particular benefit, larger. What it costs the Quebec driver is the right to go to court. Whether that is a good trade is the question, and it is a question about what people want, not about arithmetic.

Has anyone tried to change the Ontario system

Yes — repeatedly, from more than one party, and it has not carried.

  • October 2018. Bill 42, the Ending Discrimination in Automobile Insurance Act, introduced by Parm Gill, would have stopped insurers using factors primarily tied to postal code or telephone area code, rescinded the 2005 territory bulletin, and required FSRA to write new rules. It passed second reading on division on 21 March 2019 and was referred to committee. It did not come back.
  • 2018 election. Ending “postal code discrimination” was an election promise.
  • August 2022. NDP MPP Tom Rakocevic introduced a bill to base rates on driving record rather than location, co-sponsored by Doly Begum and Marit Stiles — the third time that party had brought it forward. It passed first reading and went no further.

So it is not that nobody raises it. It is raised, it gets a sympathetic hearing, and it stops. One reason it stops is that the arithmetic does not disappear when the rule changes: money not collected from higher-claim areas has to be collected from drivers in lower-claim ones. That trade, and who would be told about it, is the hard part to legislate.

What is moving is the method rather than the principle. FSRA opened a Territories Test and Learn Environment, letting insurers try different ways of drawing and grouping Greater Toronto Area territories, including outside data such as traffic and weather, with fairness checks against discrimination. Territory changes under it began on or after 2 July 2024, it runs a minimum of two years, and FSRA has approved 25 applications covering more than 90 per cent of Ontario's private passenger market.

What changed on 1 July 2026

Premiums are one number. What the policy pays for is another, and the two have moved in opposite directions. In 2013 the provincial budget targeted an average 15 per cent cut in auto rates by August 2015; by November 2014 the figure reached was 6 per cent.

On 1 July 2026 Ontario changed what a standard policy must include. Medical, rehabilitation and attendant care benefits remain mandatory. These are the benefits that moved out of the mandatory set:

  • income replacement
  • non-earner benefits
  • caregiver benefits
  • lost educational expenses (up to $15,000)
  • expenses of visitors
  • housekeeping and home maintenance
  • damage to personal items
  • death benefits and funeral benefits
If you already had a policy, nothing was taken away from you on 1 July. FSRA is explicit: “existing policies must be renewed with the same coverages and limits as the expiring policy unless the customer agrees to a change in writing.” A renewal carries your old coverage forward until you sign something that changes it.

Renewing an existing policy

FSRA's wording is that “all existing policies will renew with the same coverage as they had prior to July 1, 2026, until the customer elects to make changes.” So the benefits above do not vanish from your policy on their own. What changes is that they can now be dropped — and dropping them takes your written agreement. If your renewal came back cheaper, that is the thing to check: read what you agreed to, not just the price.

Taking out a new policy

A new policy does not inherit anything, because there is nothing to inherit. The optional benefits are selected, not assumed. FSRA requires the quote to identify clearly which coverages are optional and to explain each one in plain language — but what you end up with is what you chose.

There is a second change inside that one. Optional benefits, where bought, now cover the named insured, their spouse, dependants and listed drivers — not passengers, pedestrians or cyclists outside those categories.

Declining the optional benefits is estimated to save roughly $10 a month, about 5 per cent of a premium. The rate reductions approved alongside run from 0.23 to 5.3 per cent at nine insurers. Those three figures are the trade, set beside each other.

How profitable is it

This is the question most often asked and most often answered badly, in both directions. The published figures move a great deal, and the way they are calculated is genuinely disputed.

The Auditor General reported that in 2020, for every dollar of premium, insurers made 27.6 cents of profit — more than five times the roughly five cents a rate filing is permitted to build in. Statistics Canada puts industry return on equity at 18.2 per cent in 2021.

Two things have to be said beside those numbers. The first is that 2020 and 2021 were the pandemic years: traffic collapsed, collisions collapsed with it, and premiums had already been set. The second is what happened next. Statistics Canada records return on equity falling to 9.1 per cent in 2023 and recovering to 12.6 per cent in 2024, with the claims ratio reaching 90.4 per cent in the third quarter of 2024 and the combined ratio above 100 per cent in several quarters — the level at which the insurance itself loses money before investment income.

Why the answer is contested

Measuring the profit of one product line in one province means splitting company-wide costs and capital between products and provinces, and there is no agreed method. For 2018 the official industry data showed a return on equity of 3.3 per cent; a researcher working for the trial lawyers' association calculated between 5.3 and 15.9 per cent for the same year, depending on the expense assumptions used. As one academic put it, deciding how much of a chief executive's time belongs to Ontario auto rather than Alberta auto is unavoidably artificial.

It is worth knowing who is arguing. The figures showing high profits are most often advanced by the trial lawyers' association, whose members are paid out of the right to sue that a Quebec-style system would remove. The figures showing thin margins come from the industry that files the rates. Both sets are calculated from real data, and both parties have something to lose.

What you can actually check

FSRA publishes every approved rate change — company, percentage, effective date. Look up your own insurer. If your renewal rose far more than your insurer's approved change, that is a specific question with a number attached, and it deserves a specific answer.

Four things worth asking for, in writing:

  • Which rating factors changed between my last term and this one — listed.
  • Which rating territory my address is in — not the postal code, the territory.
  • What my vehicle's rate group was last term, and what it is this term.
  • What changed in my coverage, including any accident benefits that became optional on 1 July 2026.

Then compare. Because every insurer draws its own territories and files its own tables, the same driver, same car and same address can be priced very differently by two companies. That is the one part of this system a driver has direct control over.
