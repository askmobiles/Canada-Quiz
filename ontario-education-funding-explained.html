🏫 How Ontario pays for school

Two million children, 72 school boards, and a budget of more than thirty billion dollars a year. Every spring the same budget is announced as record funding and described as a cut, by people who are both looking at real numbers. This page explains how the money actually reaches a classroom, why no two school boards get the same amount, and how to tell those two headlines apart — then follows the same questions into college and university.

Last checked 2 October 2026 · every figure is sourced at the bottom

The short answer

Ontario runs the largest school system in Canada: about 2.1 million students in 4,861 publicly funded schools, taught under 72 school boards. For the 2026-27 school year the province has set aside just over 30.5 billion dollars for those schools.

Two things about that number are true at the same time, and almost every argument about education funding comes from people holding one of them and ignoring the other.

  • The total goes up nearly every year. Thirty point five billion is more dollars than the province has ever put into schools.
  • Measured against inflation, the amount behind each student has been falling. The province's own independent financial office has published this, in constant dollars, for several years running.
These do not contradict each other. A budget can grow in dollars and shrink in purchasing power at the same time, and that is exactly what happens when the dollars grow more slowly than prices. Understanding that one sentence is most of what anyone needs to read an education funding story without being misled.

How the money actually reaches a classroom

Ontario does not hand each school a budget. The province sends money to school boards, and the boards run the schools. What the province sends is worked out by a formula, and the formula has two halves that behave very differently.

The half that is the same everywhere

Every board is paid the same base amount for a student of a given age. A Grade 5 student in Brampton and a Grade 5 student in Kenora generate exactly the same base dollar. The biggest of those flat amounts is the Classroom Staffing Fund, which pays for teachers and classroom staff. These are its published rates for 2026-27, for each student counted in a board's average daily enrolment:

GradeClassroom Staffing Fund, per student
Kindergarten$7,034.82
Grades 1 to 3$6,308.79
Grades 4 to 8$5,154.69
Secondary school$5,776.63

Those amounts are far lower than the roughly 14,900 dollars per student quoted later on this page, and that is not a contradiction. This is one fund out of six — the part that follows the child. Everything else, and it is most of the money, arrives through the second half. Do not compare these rates with a total.

The half that is different for every board

On top of the base, the formula pays for circumstances. Not for the child — for what it costs to deliver school to that child, where that child lives:

  • special education support
  • French-language education, and support for students learning English
  • school buses, and the distances they cover
  • small schools, and schools a long way from the next one
  • remote and northern locations
  • Indigenous education programs
  • heating, repairs and keeping the buildings standing
  • falling enrolment, which leaves a board with the same building and fewer students
This is the single most useful thing to know about the whole system: the base rate is flat and the top-ups are not. Almost every confusing number in an education funding story comes from the top-ups, not the base.

Why Ontario has 72 school boards, and four kinds

People often ask why one province needs four separate school systems. The answer is not administrative. It is constitutional, and it goes back to the founding of the country.

Type of boardHow many
English public31
English Catholic29
French Catholic8
French public4
District school boards72
School authorities (hospital and remote schools)10

The Catholic system is protected by section 93 of the Constitution Act, 1867 — one of the original terms of Confederation. The French-language boards come from section 23 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, added in 1982. Neither can simply be merged away by a provincial decision.

That is not only an Ontario fact, and the proof is how hard it was elsewhere. Newfoundland and Labrador ended its denominational school system in 1998, and it took two constitutional amendments to do it: the first, in 1997, reduced those rights but left the system standing, so a second was needed. Quebec replaced its Catholic and Protestant boards with language boards on 1 July 1998, after an amendment proclaimed in December 1997 added a clause stating that most of section 93 no longer applies to Quebec.

Does every school board get the same amount per student? No.

The Financial Accountability Office of Ontario — an independent officer of the Legislature, not part of the government — measured provincial funding per student at every board. The spread is wider than most people expect.

Three times as much. Most of that difference is explained by fixed costs, geography, school size and how far the buses travel.

The Conseil scolaire de district catholique des Aurores boréales is a small French Catholic board in northwestern Ontario. Its students are spread across a territory larger than many countries. A school with forty children still needs a principal, a roof, a heating bill and a bus. Divide those fixed costs by forty instead of by eight hundred and the figure for each student rises steeply. Halton Catholic, in the dense suburbs west of Toronto, has full schools close together, so the same fixed costs spread thin.

The honest one-line version: Ontario funds the cost of delivering school, not just the number of children. A dense urban board is inexpensive per student. A remote board is expensive per student. Neither is being rewarded and neither is being punished.

Why you cannot get a clean chart back to 2000

This page was going to open with a year-by-year table from 2000. It does not, and the reason is worth a section of its own, because anyone who hands you such a table has quietly made choices you cannot see.

First, the older records are hard to reach. Ontario's education funding pages from before about 2016 now redirect to the provincial archives rather than showing the figures.

Second, and more seriously, the system itself was replaced. In April 2024 the province announced that the Grants for Student Needs — eighteen separate grants, used since the late 1990s — would become Core Education Funding, organised into six funds. It took effect with the 2024-25 school year.

Here is what that break does to a chart. For the single school year 2023-24, three different totals are in circulation: 27.08 billion dollars in the province's own technical paper, about 28.19 billion implied by the following year's announcement, and 27.9 billion used by a teachers' federation. All three come from people reading official documents. They are counting different things.

A graph that runs smoothly from 2000 to 2026 is not showing you more information than one that stops. It is showing you someone's decision about how to join two systems that do not join — without telling you which decision they made.

What can be shown honestly is the recent series, which uses one definition throughout:

School yearCore Education FundingHow the province labels itStudents (average daily enrolment)
2024-25$29.37 billionactual—
2025-26$30.26 billionrevised estimate2,062,813
2026-27$30.58 billionprojection2,049,821
Notice the third column. Only one of those three years is a final number. The most recent year is a projection, the year before it is still being revised, and only the oldest is settled. This is why the same year can be quoted at two different values a year apart — and why a figure announced in the spring is rarely the figure that ends up in the books.

Why one budget is called a record and a cut on the same day

Now the central trick, and it is not really a trick — it is two rulers.

A figure in today's dollars is the actual cheque. You do not have to work it out yourself — the province publishes it. Average Core Education Funding per pupil is 14,916 dollars for 2026-27, up 248 dollars or 1.7 percent on the revised 2025-26 figure of 14,669 dollars. Those are real numbers and the province can fairly call them the largest ever.

A figure in constant dollars restates every year into one year's money, so that years can be compared fairly. That is what the Financial Accountability Office publishes. Its numbers look bigger because they are measured with a different ruler — the cheque has not changed.

Here is that office's published series, restated into 2026-27 dollars:

School yearPer student, in constant dollars
2025-26$15,140
2026-27$15,074
2027-28$14,850
2028-29$14,775

Read down that column and the dollars are planned to rise each year while the line falls. That is the whole disagreement, in one table. The same office reported that in 2024-25, real provincial operating funding per student was at its lowest level in ten years.

Neither headline is false. If a story gives you a per-student figure without saying which ruler it used, it has left out the thing that decides the answer.

Who says what, and what each one is counting

Ask how much per-student funding has changed since 2018 and you get four answers from four organisations, all working from government documents.

WhoWhat they areTheir figure
Financial Accountability Officeindependent officer of the Legislature−$358 per student, 2015-16 to 2024-25
Ontario Public School Boards' Associationthe boards' own association−$404 per student since 2018
Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives, 2024progressive research organisation−$1,500 per student since 2018
The same organisation, 2026same authors, later end year−$220 per student since 2018

That last pair is the most instructive line on this page. The same researchers, looking at the same province, published minus fifteen hundred dollars and later minus two hundred and twenty. Two things separate them. The later figure runs to a more recent year, and the organisation says funding hit its low point in 2022-23 and has partly recovered since. The two studies also appear to treat differently a pool of money the province holds back and has not yet allocated. Neither number is dishonest; a per-student figure simply depends on where you stop counting and what you count.

A market-oriented research institute, using a different window of years, reported that Ontario's inflation-adjusted per-student spending fell 1.7 percent between 2013-14 and 2022-23, while earlier work from the same institute emphasised large real increases across the 2000s. Both can be true, because they cover different decades.

The practical rule: when two credible groups give very different answers to the same question, the gap is usually in the method, not in the facts. Ask what period they chose and what they counted, and the disagreement usually explains itself.

The same argument, written as one big number

The figure most people actually meet is not a per-student amount at all. It is $6.5 billion, and it arrives in advertising. It comes from the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives, published in 2026, and it is not money removed from a budget. It is their estimate of the cumulative gap since 2018-19 between what boards received and what they would have received had funding kept pace with inflation and with the number of students. Across those same years the province's own projections file shows the total going up: $29.4 billion in 2024-25, $30.3 billion in 2025-26, and $30.6 billion projected for 2026-27.

Both of those are true at the same time, which is this whole subject in one line. The word that fits the $6.5 billion is behind. A headline that says cut is describing something else — money taken away — and that is not what the research says. The same report puts per-pupil funding up 1.1 per cent against forecast inflation of 2.1 per cent, and calls the difference a one per cent cut in real terms. That is a careful sentence, and it does not survive being shortened.

The third answer is the table a few paragraphs above: the Financial Accountability Office's four-year series, dollars rising and the line falling. It is worth saying once who that office is. It is an independent officer of the Legislature, it reports to every party, and it is the only organisation in this argument with no case to make. When its figures and a campaign's point the same way, that is worth more than either on its own.

Which organisation is which

  • Financial Accountability Office — an independent officer of the Legislature. It reports to every party, and it is the only one on this list with no side to be on.
  • Ontario Public School Boards' Association — the boards themselves. They are the employers, which is what makes their figure interesting.
  • Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives — an outside research organisation with a stated point of view. It does the arithmetic the headlines quote.
  • ETFO, OSSTF, OECTA and AEFO — the four teacher unions: public elementary, public secondary, English Catholic, and French-language. They mostly quote the figures above rather than publish their own.
  • The Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan is none of these. It is the pension fund, one of the largest investors in the world, and it has nothing to do with how schools are funded.
A number is usually calculated by one organisation and repeated by another, and the wording changes on the way. By the time it reaches a screen it may have lost the date it was measured to, the years it covered, and the difference between behind and cut. Checking the word costs nothing, and it is the fastest way to tell a finding from a slogan.

Forty years in four lines

For the long view there is one consistent series. The Financial Accountability Office tracked spending from 1990 to 2023 in constant dollars, per child aged 0 to 17. Two caveats matter and are stated here rather than buried. This measure bundles schools together with child care, and it counts every child including infants and preschoolers, so it is broader than the school funding figures above in both directions.

PeriodChange per year, after inflation

Down sharply, then up sharply, then levelling off. That span covers governments formed by three different political parties, and the pattern does not line up neatly with any one of them. Whatever explains the turns, the long view is a more useful thing to hold than any single year's headline.

After high school: where Ontario sits in Canada

Colleges and universities are funded separately, and here the picture is clearer because everyone measures it the same way.

The Financial Accountability Office compares provincial funding per full-time domestic student across Canada. Its most recent published year is 2023-24:

Ontario was lowest in the country on both. In the same year its domestic undergraduate tuition was the fourth highest, at 8,271 dollars against a national average of 7,152. A year earlier, in 2022-23, the same office had Ontario lowest on both measures again and its tuition third highest — so the ranking on funding has not moved, and the tuition ranking has slipped by one place.

The lowest public funding per student in Canada, alongside the third-highest tuition. That is not an advocacy claim — it comes from the Legislature's own independent financial office, and a panel appointed by the province reached the same conclusion in 2023.

The tuition decade: a cut, a long freeze, and 2026

In January 2019 Ontario cut domestic tuition by ten percent and then held it there. The freeze lasted six consecutive years.

YearDomestic tuition
2019-20cut by 10 percent
2020-21 to 2025-26frozen
2026-27freeze lifted, up to 2 percent

A tuition cut is money students keep, and it is also money institutions do not receive. News reporting in January 2019 projected the first-year effect at roughly 360 million dollars for universities and 80 million for colleges; another outlet put the combined figure at about 250 million. No government or audit body published a measured number, so treat all of these as estimates made before the fact.

The same announcement changed student aid. Grants that had covered full tuition for the lowest-income students became a mix that included loans; the income threshold for grant eligibility was lowered; and the six-month interest-free period after graduation was removed from the provincial portion of the loan, so interest begins accruing as soon as a student leaves full-time study.

A panel appointed by the province reported in November 2023 that the sector was not financially sustainable, and recommended both higher grants and a tuition increase. In February 2024 the province announced nearly 1.3 billion dollars over three years and declined the increase for students from Ontario, extending their freeze instead — while allowing fees for domestic students from other provinces to rise up to 5 percent.

In February 2026 the position changed. The province announced 6.4 billion dollars over four years — taking annual operating funding to roughly 7 billion, which it described as the highest level in Ontario's history — funded 70,000 additional student places, and allowed tuition to rise by up to 2 percent from the autumn of 2026. In the same package, student aid shifted further toward loans: from that autumn, grants make up at most a quarter of an assessed award.

Both halves of that belong in the same sentence. Ontario's per-student grants have been the lowest in Canada for years, and the province has just announced its largest postsecondary funding package. A reader given only one of those has been given half a page.

What happened when the international students stopped coming

None of the college story makes sense without this part. Because provincial grants were low and domestic tuition was frozen, Ontario colleges came to depend heavily on international student fees, which are not regulated the same way.

The Auditor General of Ontario reported in 2021 that in the 2020-21 year, 68 percent of all college tuition revenue in the province — about 1.7 billion dollars — came from international students, and that at some smaller colleges the share was above 90 percent. International tuition had come to exceed the provincial grant.

In January 2024 the federal government capped new study permits nationally. Statistics Canada estimated the result in a study published in May 2026. Compared with 2023-24, Ontario's international student numbers were down about 36 percent by 2025-26 — roughly 92,000 fewer people — and Ontario's share of Canada's international students fell from 60 percent to 54 percent.

Two numbers from that same study get quoted as Ontario figures and are not. Across Canada, college international enrolment fell about 42 percent over those two years, and new college students fell about 75 percent — a loss of roughly 102,000 people nationally. Because Ontario held more than half the country's international students, a large share of that national fall happened here, but Statistics Canada did not publish an Ontario-only college figure and neither will this page.

One caution on all four of those numbers. They come from a Statistics Canada feasibility study built from administrative records, and the agency labels the two most recent years as preliminary. Its own wording is “appears to be” and “may represent”, not “was”. They are the best available estimates, and they are estimates.

What followed is counted by two bodies and neither count is audited. The colleges' own sector association reported more than 600 programs suspended and more than 8,000 positions eliminated. The union representing college staff reported about 600 programs in July 2025 and more than 650 since, along with about 10,000 jobs — a figure that, in the union's own words, covers staff who “have been or will be” laid off.

Notice where they actually differ. On programs the two are close, around 600 to 650. On jobs they are 2,000 apart. No government body publishes an official count of either, so the gap cannot be settled from a neutral source — which is itself worth knowing before quoting either number.

Campuses went too. Algonquin College's board voted to close its Perth campus, which ceases operations in August 2026 with its programs moved to Ottawa. Centennial College is moving the programs at its Story Arts Centre in Toronto to its Progress campus for the autumn of 2026; the college calls this a relocation rather than a closure. Both are decisions already taken, not proposals.

What nobody can tell you

A page like this is more useful if it marks its own edges.

  • Whether more money produces better results. No one has shown a clean link between a funding figure and a test score, in either direction. Anyone who tells you the answer is simple is selling something.
  • A final figure for the current year. The province does publish an average per pupil every year, but the newest one is always a projection and the year before it is still a revised estimate. Only the oldest year in any table is settled.
  • An official count of college programs closed or jobs lost. The sector association and the union each keep a list. Neither is audited and the two do not match.
  • What the next few years hold. Every figure past the current year is a plan, and plans change with every budget.

How to read any education funding headline

If you remember nothing else from this page, four questions will get you through almost any story about school money, in any province.

  • Total, or per student? A total can rise while the amount behind each child falls, simply because there are more children.
  • Today's dollars, or constant dollars? This one decides more arguments than any other, and it is the one most often left out.
  • Which years? Choosing where a comparison starts can turn the same data from a rise into a fall.
  • Who is counting, and what do they gain? A government, a board association, a union and a research institute can all be honest and still reach different numbers.
Every number on this page came from a document anyone can open. The links are below. Reading the source rather than the headline is the single most reliable defence against being told a half-truth — about education funding or anything else.
