Saskatchewan mostly ignores daylight saving time. The federal government's own time page says most of the province uses Central Standard Time all year round. The exception is the area around Lloydminster, a city that sits on the Alberta border — it keeps Mountain Time and changes its clocks with Alberta, so that the two halves of one city agree with each other.

Where Saskatchewan is

Saskatchewan is the middle Prairie province. Alberta is to the west, Manitoba to the east, the Northwest Territories to the north, and two American states — Montana and North Dakota — along the south. Like Alberta, it has no ocean coast.

Its shape is almost a rectangle, because three of its four borders are ruled lines of latitude and longitude rather than rivers or mountains.

What the land is like

The picture most people carry of Saskatchewan — flat fields to the horizon — is accurate for the south and completely wrong for the north. The southern third is grain country. Above it comes parkland, and then the whole northern half is boreal forest, rock and water: thousands of lakes, sitting on the Canadian Shield.

  • Southern third: open prairie and farmland, plus the Cypress Hills in the southwest corner and badlands along some river valleys.
  • Middle: aspen parkland, where the fields start breaking up into bush.
  • Northern half: boreal forest and Canadian Shield, with far more lake than land in places and almost no roads.

How big is that, really?

  • Saskatchewan is slightly smaller than Alberta overall — 651,036 km² against 661,848 km² — but it has three times as much fresh water: 59,366 km² against 19,531.
  • Saskatchewan holds more fresh water than British Columbia, Alberta, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick put together — 59,366 km² against their combined 42,484 km².

How people make a living

Saskatchewan feeds people and supplies materials. It is Canada's biggest wheat province, and it also grows canola, lentils, peas and durum in large quantities. Underground it has potash, which goes into fertiliser used around the world, and uranium, and oil in the southeast.

That combination — food and fertiliser and fuel — means Saskatchewan's economy is unusually exposed to what is happening in other countries. A harvest failure on the other side of the world changes prices here.

How Saskatchewan became a province

Cree, Saulteaux, Dakota, Nakota, Lakota, Dene and Assiniboine nations lived across this land, and the Métis built communities along the rivers. Treaties 2, 4, 5, 6, 8 and 10 cover what is now Saskatchewan, signed between 1871 and 1906.

In 1885 the North-West Resistance was fought largely on this ground, at Batoche and elsewhere, over land, food and broken promises. Twenty years later the same territory had filled with homesteaders, and on 1 September 1905 Saskatchewan and Alberta became provinces together.

One more piece of Saskatchewan history reaches every Canadian. In 1962 the province brought in North America's first universal public hospital and medical insurance plan, over a doctors' strike. The federal programme that became medicare followed it.

Things most people do not know

  • Saskatchewan's official sport is curling, adopted in 2001, and its official fruit is the saskatoon berry, adopted in 2005. The berry and the city share a name, and the name came from the Cree word for the berry.
  • Most of the province never changes its clocks, so for half the year Saskatchewan has the same time as Alberta, and for the other half the same time as Manitoba.
  • The province holds three times as much fresh water as Alberta, even though almost nobody pictures Saskatchewan as a place with lakes.
  • The motto, “From many peoples, strength”, is unusual: most provincial mottoes are about loyalty, glory or God. This one is about immigration.
  • Saskatoon is the bigger city and Regina is the capital — the same split Alberta has with Calgary and Edmonton.
  • Public medicare in Canada started here, in 1962, before it existed anywhere else in North America.

Why Lloydminster gets an exception

Lloydminster sits astride the Alberta–Saskatchewan border. If the Saskatchewan half followed the rest of its province and the Alberta half followed Alberta, then for half the year the two sides of the same main street would be an hour apart — schools, shifts, buses and appointments all out of step within one town.

So the federal time page records the Lloydminster area as Mountain Time, changing with Alberta. It is a small reminder that time zones are a decision people make for practical reasons, not a fact about the sky.

Potash, and why a landlocked province cares about ports

Potash is a potassium salt mined from ancient seabeds buried under southern Saskatchewan, and it is used as fertiliser. Almost none of it is used in the province. It travels by rail to ports in British Columbia and on the Atlantic, and from there to farms in other countries.

This is why a province with no coastline follows port and railway news closely. A rail strike, a bridge closure or a congested terminal on either coast is a Saskatchewan problem, even though the nearest salt water is over a thousand kilometres away.
