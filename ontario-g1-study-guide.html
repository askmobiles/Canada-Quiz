What the G1 Test Is

The G1 test is the first step to a driver's licence in Ontario. It is a knowledge test: you sit at a screen and answer questions. You do not drive a car. You only show that you understand road signs and the rules every driver must follow.

Anyone who wants to drive a car, van or small truck in Ontario starts here. You must be at least 16 years old. The same day, you also do a short eyesight check.

This guide is free, unofficial study help written in plain words. We are not part of the Government of Ontario and are not connected to any government office.

How Graduated Licensing Works in Ontario

Ontario uses graduated licensing. "Graduated" means step by step: you learn in three stages, and each stage gives you more freedom. The stages are G1, G2 and G.

Stage One: G1

You get your G1 after passing the knowledge test and the eyesight check. A G1 driver is a learner and may never drive alone. A fully licensed driver must sit in the front passenger seat, with at least four years of experience, sober enough to take control.

As a G1 driver you must have zero alcohol in your body, and you must not drive after using any drug that changes how your brain works. Every person needs a working seatbelt, so you may never carry more passengers than seatbelts.

You must not drive between midnight and five in the morning. You must not drive on the fastest highways, such as the 400-series highways or other high-speed expressways. One exception: if a licensed driving instructor is teaching you, those roads are allowed.

Most people stay at G1 about one year before the first road test. With an approved course at a recognised driving school, the wait is shorter, about eight months.

Stage Two: G2

You move to G2 when you pass your first road test, a real drive with an examiner. Now you may drive alone, on any road including the fast highways, at any hour.

Some rules stay. You still must have zero alcohol, and everyone still needs a seatbelt. Younger G2 drivers have limits on how many teenage passengers they may carry late at night. Those limits ease after six months, and family members count differently from friends.

You normally stay at the G2 stage for at least one year before you may take the second road test.

Stage Three: Full G

Passing the second road test gives you a full G licence, and the beginner rules end. The normal laws still apply, and drivers aged 21 or younger must still have zero alcohol in their body.

From your first day as a G1 to a full G takes at least about twenty months if you never fail. You get five years in total to finish both road tests, or you start again from the beginning, including the knowledge test.

The Test Format and the Pass Mark

DriveTest says the knowledge test comes in two or three sections, and that you rewrite only the sections you fail. It does not publish how many questions it asks, and neither does ontario.ca — so treat any site that prints an exact number, including the forty in our own mock test, as that site's practice format rather than the real one. What the sections cover is clear enough:

  • Road signs. A sign is shown or described, and you choose what it means or what you must do.
  • The rules of the road. These cover safe driving, sharing the road, speed, alcohol, licences and emergencies.

The pass mark is 80 percent. On a section of twenty questions that would mean sixteen right, which is the mark our mock test uses. The two sections are marked separately, so a strong score on signs will not rescue a weak score on rules — you would go back and rewrite the rules half. How many questions the test asks is not published on any government page, so this site does not state one, and the test is not timed.

The two halves are marked separately, and this surprises many people. A high score on one half does not rescue a low score on the other. If you get 20 out of 20 on signs but 14 on rules, you have not passed, and you come back to repeat the rules half. You usually repeat only the failed half. Many people fail on signs because they never studied the shapes, so do not skip that part.

Road Signs: Read the Shape First

Here is the most useful trick in the guide. Every road sign gives its message twice: once by shape, once by colour. Learn both and you can work out a sign you have never seen before. That is exactly what the test checks.

Learn these shapes:

  • Octagon (eight sides). Used for one sign only: STOP. Nothing else in Canada is an octagon. Come to a complete stop, wheels no longer turning, behind the white line, and go only when it is safe.
  • Triangle pointing down. This is the yield sign. Yield means give way. Slow down, be ready to stop, and let the other traffic and any pedestrians go first. You only continue when the way is clear.
  • Diamond (a square standing on one corner). This is a warning sign. It tells you about a possible danger ahead: a sharp curve, a hill, a bump, a narrow bridge, a slippery road, a hidden side road, or animals that cross. It does not order you to do anything. It asks you to think and slow down.
  • Square or rectangle. The everyday signs. Taller than wide usually means a rule you must obey: speed limit, no parking, no left turn, one way. Wider than tall usually means information: a road name, the distance to a town, or where to find a hospital.
  • Pentagon (five sides, like a simple house). This shape means school. You are near a school zone or a place where children cross. Slow down and watch, because children do not always look before stepping out.
  • Crossbuck and advance railway sign. A crossbuck is the big white X of two crossed boards standing at the railway crossing itself. Before it, a yellow diamond warns you the crossing is coming: it draws the road you are on crossed by a line of rails and ties, at the same angle the real tracks cross the real road. Never stop on the tracks, and never try to beat a train.

Road Signs: Then Read the Colour

Colour gives the mood of the sign and finishes the message the shape started.

  • Red means stop or forbidden. A red circle with a line through a picture means "do not do this": no left turn, no parking. Red is the strongest colour on the road.
  • Green means allowed, or gives directions. A green circle around a picture means the action is permitted, such as parking allowed here. Large green highway signs give exits, place names and distances.
  • Blue means services for travellers: parking, rest stops, food, fuel, hospitals and tourist information.
  • Yellow means warning. Danger ahead, be careful. Most diamond warning signs are yellow.
  • Orange means road work. You are entering a construction zone, where there may be workers, machines, loose gravel, a lane that ends, or a person holding a stop paddle. Fines are usually higher here, and workers stand very close to moving traffic.
  • Bright yellow-green is used for signs about people: children, school crossings, pedestrians and cyclists. The colour is deliberately loud.
  • White with black letters is used for rules, most famously the speed limit sign. Black on white means law.

The Rules of the Road

Safe and Responsible Driving

Good driving is mostly about seeing problems early. Look far ahead, not only at the car in front, and check your mirrors often. Before changing lanes: mirror, signal, then a look over your shoulder. That space beside you is the blind spot, because mirrors do not show it.

Signal every turn and lane change early enough for others to react. Keep right unless you are passing. The posted speed is a maximum for perfect conditions, not a target. In rain, snow or heavy traffic, the safe speed is lower than the sign.

Sharing the Road

Pedestrians. People on foot deserve extra care, especially children and older adults. At a pedestrian crossover, or a school crossing where a guard is helping, wait until every person has crossed completely before you move.

Cyclists. A bicycle is a vehicle and may use the road. Give a cyclist plenty of room when passing. Before opening your door on the street side, look behind you, or you may knock a cyclist off the bike.

Large trucks and buses. Big vehicles need far more distance to stop and have huge blind spots, especially on the right and directly behind. If you cannot see the driver's face in the truck's mirror, the driver cannot see you. Never cut in front of a truck and then brake.

School buses. When a school bus has its upper red lights flashing and its stop arm out, traffic must stop, well back. On a road with no raised median, traffic in both directions stops. Move again only when the lights stop flashing.

Streetcars. In Toronto, streetcars run in the middle of the street. When one stops to let people on or off and there is no raised safety island, stop behind its rear doors and wait until the passengers reach the sidewalk.

Emergency vehicles. When a fire truck, ambulance or police car approaches with lights and siren, pull to the right, stop, and wait until it passes. If an emergency vehicle or tow truck is already stopped at the roadside with lights flashing, slow right down, and move into another lane when it is safe if the road has two or more lanes your way.

Driving in Difficult Conditions

Rain. Water makes the road slippery, and the first rain after a dry spell is worst because it lifts oil off the surface. Slow down and leave more space. If your tires ride up on a film of water and lose grip, lift your foot off the gas and hold the wheel steady until they touch the road again.

Snow and ice. Everything takes longer on snow: starting, turning and above all stopping. Be gentle with pedals and steering. Watch for black ice, a thin clear layer that looks like a wet patch. Bridges freeze before ordinary roads, and winter tires help a lot.

Fog. Slow down and use low beams. High beams bounce off the fog and make seeing harder. If the fog is too thick, leave the road at a safe place and wait.

Night. You see only as far as your headlights reach, so drive slowly enough to stop inside that distance. Dim your high beams for oncoming cars and when you come up behind another vehicle. If a car does not dim its lights, look toward the right edge of the road instead of into the glare.

Skidding. A skid means the tires have lost grip. Do not brake hard and do not panic. Ease off the gas and steer gently where you want to go, then straighten out as the car comes back under control.

Keeping Your Licence

Ontario records serious driving mistakes as demerit points. You start with none. Points are added when you are convicted of offences such as speeding, following too closely, careless driving or failing to stop for a school bus. The more dangerous the offence, the more points.

Points stay on your record for two years from the date of the offence, then drop off. Collect too many and you may be called to an interview, and your licence can be suspended, meaning you may not drive at all for a time. G1 and G2 drivers are suspended at a lower number of points than fully licensed drivers.

Some things suspend a licence immediately: alcohol in the body of a new driver, driving far above the limit in a way the law treats as stunt driving, or driving while already suspended. Insurance also becomes much more expensive after a conviction.

Impaired and Distracted Driving

Impaired means your ability to drive is reduced by alcohol, cannabis, other drugs, some medicines, or extreme tiredness. Alcohol slows your reactions while making you feel more confident, a dangerous combination. Coffee, cold showers and fresh air do not remove alcohol faster. Only time does.

For G1 and G2 drivers the rule is simple: zero alcohol. The same rule applies to every driver aged 21 or under. No small amount is acceptable. If you plan to drink, decide how you will get home before the first drink.

Distracted driving means anything that takes your eyes, hands or mind away from driving. In Ontario it is against the law to hold or use a phone while driving, even at a red light. Set your route and music before you move. Eating, arguing and turning to talk to the back seat are distractions too. Looking away for two seconds at highway speed means travelling a long way blind.

Your Vehicle

The driver is responsible for the condition of the car. Check your tires often: correct pressure and enough tread are what keep you gripping the road. Make sure every light works, front and back, brake lights and signals included.

Keep your windshield clear. Scrape the snow and ice off every window, the mirrors, the roof and the lights, not just a small hole in front of your face. Use washer fluid that will not freeze. Get strange brake noises checked early. Your car needs valid insurance, and you must carry your licence, ownership document and proof of insurance.

Emergencies and Collisions

If you are in a collision, stop. Driving away is a serious crime. Turn on your hazard lights and check whether anyone is hurt. Call for emergency help if there are injuries, if the damage looks serious, or if a driver seems impaired.

Exchange names, addresses, phone numbers, licence and plate numbers and insurance details. Write down what happened while it is fresh and get the names of witnesses. Do not argue about fault at the roadside. If the vehicles still run and nobody is hurt, move them off the travelled road. Damage above a certain amount must be reported to the police or at a collision reporting centre.

If your car breaks down on a highway, steer it well onto the right shoulder and turn on your hazard lights. Usually the safest choice is to stay inside with your seatbelt on and wait for help, because standing near fast traffic is very dangerous. If a tire goes flat suddenly, hold the wheel firmly, do not slam the brakes, let the car slow, and steer off the road.

Numbers to Memorise

Test questions love numbers. These are worth learning by heart.

  • 16 is the minimum age to apply for a G1 licence.
  • 80 percent is the pass mark DriveTest publishes. The number of questions is not published.
  • 50 km/h is the normal limit in cities, towns and built-up areas, unless a sign says otherwise.
  • 80 km/h is the normal limit on roads outside built-up areas, unless a sign says otherwise.
  • 100 km/h is the usual limit on 400-series highways. A few sections are posted at 110 km/h. Always follow the posted sign.
  • School zones and community safety zones are often lower, such as 40 or 30 km/h, with higher fines.
  • Two seconds is the smallest safe following distance in good weather. Pick a fixed object, and if you reach it before counting to two, you are too close. In rain, snow, fog or behind a big truck, make it four seconds or more.
  • 20 metres is how far back you must stop for a school bus with its upper red lights flashing.
  • Zero alcohol is the rule for every G1 and G2 driver, and for every driver aged 21 or under.
  • 0.08 is the blood alcohol level at which a fully licensed adult driver commits a criminal offence. Between 0.05 and 0.08 there are still immediate penalties, so do not treat 0.05 as safe.
  • Seatbelts for everyone, every seat, every trip. The driver is responsible for passengers under 16.
  • Child seats step up by size: rear-facing for the smallest babies, then forward-facing with a tether strap, then a booster. A booster can normally stop at about 145 cm tall, or 36 kg, or 8 years old.
  • 3 metres is how close you may not park to a fire hydrant.
  • 1 metre of space is the minimum you should leave when you drive past a cyclist.
  • 2 metres behind the rear doors is where you stop for a streetcar that is loading passengers with no safety island.
  • For a stopped emergency vehicle or tow truck with flashing lights: slow down, and move over one lane if there are two or more lanes in your direction and it is safe.
  • Two years is how long demerit points stay on your record.
  • 12 months at G1 before the first road test, or about 8 months with an approved course, then 12 months at G2, with 5 years in total to finish.

Tips for Test Day

  • Bring identification that proves who you are, your age, and that you are in Canada legally. Check the official requirements ahead of time, and bring originals, not photocopies.
  • Arrive early. Rushing raises your stress, and there is often a line.
  • Expect a touch screen. You tap your answer with a finger. There is nothing to write.
  • The test is usually offered in many languages, and translation help may be available. Ask when you arrive if English is hard for you.
  • Read every question to the end. Words like "not" or "except" change everything, and missing one turns a right answer into a wrong one.
  • Take your time. You are not usually rushed, so there is no prize for finishing first.
  • If a question confuses you, picture a real road with real cars. The safest, most polite choice is almost always correct.
  • You will also do a vision check, so bring your glasses or contact lenses if you wear them.
  • Sleep well the night before. A tired brain forgets what it knows.

Before You Go

This page explains the ideas quickly, in simple language. It does not replace the free official driver's handbook published by Ontario's Ministry of Transportation. That handbook is the source the test is built from, and it costs nothing to read. Read it once slowly, then come back to a summary like this one.

Then practise. Practice questions are the fastest way to find gaps in your knowledge and to get used to how real questions are written. Use our free practice tests as often as you like, repeat the road signs until shapes and colours feel automatic, and walk into the test centre calm and ready. Good luck.

Common questions

Is this the official Ontario driver's handbook?

No. This is a free unofficial summary written in our own words to explain the ideas quickly. The official handbook is published by Ontario's Ministry of Transportation and costs nothing to read, and we are not connected to that ministry, to ServiceOntario or to DriveTest.

How long does the whole guide take to read?

Most people get through it in well under an hour at a steady pace. Reading it once from top to bottom gives you the whole map, and after that you can jump straight back to a single section whenever a practice question catches you out.

Should I read this before or after doing practice questions?

Read it first. Questions teach you much more once the ideas already have somewhere to land, and the guide is written to be read in one sitting. After that, alternate between practising and coming back here for whatever keeps tripping you up.

Does the guide cover both halves of the test?

Yes. Road signs and the rules of the road each get their own sections, which matters because the test scores the two halves separately. You have to reach sixteen out of twenty on each side on its own.

Do I need an account to read this page?

No. There is no sign-up, no email address and nothing to download. The whole guide opens in your browser, so you can read it on a phone during a bus ride or on a laptop at home.
