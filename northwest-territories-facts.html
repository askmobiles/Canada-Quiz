The Northwest Territories, from the fact box to the hard questions

Eleven official languages, Canada's longest river, the biggest lake entirely inside the country, and a place bigger than Ontario with fewer people than a small town. Every figure comes from a government source, and they are all listed at the bottom.

The Official Languages Act of the Northwest Territories lists them: Chipewyan, Cree, English, French, Gwich'in, Inuinnaqtun, Inuktitut, Inuvialuktun, North Slavey, South Slavey and Tłı̨chǫ. Nine of the eleven are Indigenous languages. No province has more than two.

Where Northwest Territories is

The Northwest Territories sits between Yukon to the west and Nunavut to the east, with British Columbia, Alberta and Saskatchewan along the south and the Arctic Ocean to the north. Its northern edge includes islands in the Arctic Archipelago.

The capital, Yellowknife, is on the north shore of Great Slave Lake. Most communities are on water — a river or a lake — because for most of the territory's history water was the only practical road.

What the land is like

This is where the Canadian Shield, the western plains and the Arctic meet. The Mackenzie River — Canada's longest — runs northwest from Great Slave Lake to the Beaufort Sea, and its valley is the territory's spine. Great Bear Lake, further north, is the largest lake entirely inside Canada.

Forest gives way to tundra as you go north, and the line between them — the treeline — runs diagonally across the territory rather than straight east to west.

How big is that, really?

  • The Northwest Territories is bigger than Ontario, and has about one person for every 350 people in Ontario.
  • About 12% of the territory is fresh water — 163,021 km², which is more than any province except Quebec.
  • Put the three territories together and they are nearly 40% of Canada's area, with about a third of one percent of its people.

How people make a living

Diamonds and other mining carry most of the private economy — the territory made the diamond its official gemstone in 1999 for good reason. Oil and gas, government and the work of running scattered communities make up much of the rest.

Traditional harvesting is not a museum piece here. Hunting, fishing and trapping are part of how households actually eat, in a place where food shipped from the south is expensive.

How the Northwest Territories came to be

Dene and Inuvialuit peoples have lived across this land for thousands of years, and Métis communities grew up along the rivers. Treaties 8 and 11 were signed here, in 1899 and 1921, and what those treaties meant has been argued over ever since.

The Northwest Territories was created on 15 July 1870, the same day Manitoba joined Canada, out of land transferred from the Hudson's Bay Company. It was enormous — most of what is now western and northern Canada — and it has been shrinking ever since, as provinces and territories were cut out of it.

The last and largest piece to leave was Nunavut, on 1 April 1999. Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta, Yukon and parts of Ontario and Quebec were all carved out of the Northwest Territories at one time or another.

Things most people do not know

  • Eleven official languages — Chipewyan, Cree, English, French, Gwich'in, Inuinnaqtun, Inuktitut, Inuvialuktun, North Slavey, South Slavey and Tłı̨chǫ. Nine are Indigenous.
  • The territory's own government also prints several of those language names the way their own speakers write them — Dene Kǝdǝ́, Dëne Sųłıné, Dene Zhatıé, Dinjii Zhuʼ Ginjik, nēhiyawēwin, Tłı̨chǫ.
  • At the 2021 Census, 49.6% of people in the Northwest Territories were Indigenous — 20,035 people. At the 2016 Census it had been 50.7%, so the territory crossed from a majority to just under one between the two counts.
  • Great Bear Lake is the largest lake entirely inside Canada, and the Mackenzie is Canada's longest river. Both are here.
  • The official tree changed. The jack pine was replaced by the tamarack larch in 1999 — very few places have ever swapped an official tree.
  • Almost every other province and territory in western and northern Canada was once part of the Northwest Territories.

What eleven official languages actually costs and buys

Every official language creates obligations: documents, interpretation in the legislature, services, teaching materials, trained speakers in government offices. Doing that for eleven languages, for a population of about 46,000 spread over an area bigger than Ontario, is a genuinely hard problem, and the territory does not pretend otherwise.

What it buys is the survival of languages with small numbers of speakers. A language with no official status tends to disappear from schools, then from workplaces, then from homes. Official status does not guarantee survival, but it is the difference between a language the government must use and one it may ignore.

A territory that keeps getting smaller

In 1870 the Northwest Territories was nearly everything Canada owned west of Ontario and north of the 49th parallel. Manitoba was cut out the same year, Yukon in 1898, Alberta and Saskatchewan in 1905, and Nunavut in 1999, while Ontario, Quebec and Manitoba all took pieces as their boundaries were pushed north.

So the name is a leftover. It describes where the land was relative to the Canada of 1870, not where it is relative to the Canada of today — which is why a territory in the far north is called the north-west of anything.
