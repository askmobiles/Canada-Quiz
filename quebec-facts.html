Quebec, from the fact box to the hard questions

The biggest province by land, the French language, the maple syrup numbers done honestly, and the 72% of Quebec almost nobody ever sees. Every figure comes from a government source, and they are all listed at the bottom.

Most of Quebec is north. The Quebec government's own figure: the territory north of the 49th parallel is 72% of the province's area. Almost everything people picture when they think of Quebec — Montréal, Quebec City, the St. Lawrence valley, the sugar bush — is in the remaining 28%. The same page adds that the Canadian Shield covers 90% of Quebec.

Where Quebec is

Quebec is Canada's largest province. Ontario is to the west, Newfoundland and Labrador to the northeast, and New Brunswick to the southeast. Four American states border it in the south. Hudson Bay, James Bay, Ungava Bay and the Gulf of St. Lawrence all touch it, so Quebec has salt water on three sides.

The capital is Quebec City, on the St. Lawrence, where the river narrows — which is exactly why a fort was built there. Montréal, the larger city, is upriver on an island.

What the land is like

The St. Lawrence lowlands are a narrow band of good farmland on both sides of the river, and that band holds nearly all of Quebec's people. North and east of it the Canadian Shield takes over — the government's figure is 90% of the province — and keeps going, through boreal forest, then taiga, then tundra.

All that Shield holds an extraordinary amount of water. Quebec has more fresh water inside its borders than any other province or territory, and that water is the reason for the province's enormous hydroelectric system.

How big is that, really?

  • Quebec is about 15% of Canada's total area — one province, a sixth of the country.
  • Quebec and Ontario together are about a quarter of Canada's area and hold about three in every five Canadians.
  • Its 176,928 km² of fresh water is more than the whole of New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island put together — 133,852 km² for those three provinces.

How people make a living

Water is Quebec's biggest industrial asset. Hydroelectricity powers the province and is sold outside it, and cheap power is why energy-hungry industries like aluminium smelting settled here. Forestry, mining, aerospace, software and a very large cultural industry — television, music, games, circus — do the rest.

And maple. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada reports that Canada accounts for about 78% of world maple syrup production, and that 92% of the Canadian production comes from Quebec. Those are two separate figures from one federal report, and this page leaves them separate on purpose — multiplying them together to make a single world-share number would be our arithmetic, not anybody's published figure.

How Quebec became a province

The St. Lawrence valley was Haudenosaunee and Algonquin country, and further north and east it was and is Innu, Atikamekw, Cree, Naskapi and Inuit land. Eleven Indigenous nations are recognised in Quebec today.

The French founded Quebec City in 1608 and built a colony along the river. Britain took it in 1763. The Quebec Act of 1774 then did something unusual for its time: it protected French civil law and the Catholic religion, rather than replacing them. That decision is a large part of why French survived here.

Quebec became one of the four original provinces on 1 July 1867. In 1977 the National Assembly adopted the Charter of the French language, whose first section states that French is the official language of Québec and that only French has that status.

Things most people do not know

  • Quebec holds more fresh water than any other province or territory — more, in area, than New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island put together.
  • The provincial bird is the snowy owl, a bird of the far north. Quebec chose it in 1987, and it is a reminder that most of the province is northern.
  • Not all of Quebec is on the same clock. The federal time page says some areas east of 63° west longitude use Atlantic Time for all or part of the year.
  • The motto, “Je me souviens”, is the only provincial motto that is a plain sentence in an everyday language rather than Latin.
  • Quebec is Canada's largest province by area, but the Northwest Territories and Nunavut are both bigger — they are territories, not provinces, and the distinction is about how they are governed, not how big they are.
  • Quebec City's old town sits where the St. Lawrence narrows sharply. The city's name comes from an Algonquin word meaning roughly “where the river narrows”.

Going deeper

Why French survived here and almost nowhere else in North America

When Britain took the colony in 1763 it had a choice: absorb the population or accommodate it. The Quebec Act of 1774 chose accommodation — French civil law and the Catholic religion were protected by statute. That was not generosity; Britain had thirteen restless colonies to the south and no appetite for a revolt in a fourteenth.

The effect lasted. Language survives when the institutions that use it survive — courts, parishes, schools, notaries — and in 1774 those institutions kept operating in French. By the time anyone might have tried to change that, there were far too many French speakers for it to be practical.

The north, the water and the electricity

Quebec's hydroelectric capacity exists because of geography that looks useless at first glance: an enormous area of hard rock, high ground and heavy runoff, with almost nobody living on it. Rivers running off the Shield towards James Bay drop a long way, and that drop is the power.

Those projects were built on land used by Cree and Inuit communities, and the agreements that followed — beginning with the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement in 1975 — changed how large projects on Indigenous land are negotiated across the country. The electricity and the agreements are the same story.
