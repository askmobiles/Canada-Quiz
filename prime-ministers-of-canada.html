🍁 Every Prime Minister of Canada, 1st to Today

Twenty-four people have led Canada since 1867. Here they are in order, with the dates they actually held office, and one thing about each that you probably did not know.

Last checked 9 August 2026

Canada has had 24 prime ministers. That number surprises people, because you will also see 29 or 30 quoted. The difference is what you count. Four people served more than once with a gap in between, so there have been 30 separate ministries but only 24 individuals. The Government of Canada counts people, which is why Mark Carney is the 24th prime minister and not the 29th.

A note on the dates. You will find slightly different dates on different websites, and both can be right. A prime minister's term ends the day they resign or die; the next one's begins the day they are sworn in. Those are often not the same day. After Sir John A. Macdonald died on 6 June 1891, Canada had no prime minister for ten days. We use swearing-in dates throughout, so you will see small gaps. They are real.

One thing you probably did not know about each of them

Sir John A. Macdonald. In January 1867, while in London negotiating Confederation, he fell asleep and his candle set his chair alight. He was burned, and refused to miss a single session.

Alexander Mackenzie. A stonemason by trade, he refused a knighthood three times. Touring Fort Henry, he told a soldier that he had personally built the wall they were standing beside.

Sir John Abbott. He was the great-grandfather of the actor Christopher Plummer, and reportedly said he had been chosen because he was the least loathsome of the available options.

Sir John Thompson. He is the only Canadian prime minister to die outside Canada, at Windsor Castle, minutes after lunch with Queen Victoria.

Sir Mackenzie Bowell. Despite fifty years in Parliament, no one wrote a full biography of him until 2017 — a century after his death.

Sir Charles Tupper. The only medical doctor ever to be prime minister. He earned his MD at Edinburgh in 1843 and had delivered 116 babies by the age of 22.

Sir Wilfrid Laurier. He sat in the House of Commons for about 45 years, still a record for that chamber.

Sir Robert Borden. He led Canada through the First World War, and in 1917 the federal vote was extended to some women for the first time — the step that led to the wider franchise of 1918.

Arthur Meighen. He held the record for the longest retirement of any prime minister — 33 years — until Joe Clark passed him in 2014.

William Lyon Mackenzie King. Canada's longest-serving prime minister secretly consulted mediums and believed he spoke with the dead, above all his mother. None of it was public in his lifetime.

R. B. Bennett. The only Canadian prime minister made a lord, and the only one not buried in Canada. He died in his bath in Surrey, England, in 1947.

Louis St-Laurent. In 1949 his government ended appeals to Britain's Privy Council, making the Supreme Court of Canada the country's final court for the first time.

John Diefenbaker. He appointed the first woman to Cabinet and the first Indigenous senator, and in 1960 gave all First Nations people the federal vote without making them give up their status.

Lester B. Pearson. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1957, played semi-professional baseball, and won the first Spengler Cup with Oxford's hockey team in 1923.

Pierre Elliott Trudeau. He was the first world leader to meet John Lennon and Yoko Ono on their 1969 peace tour. Lennon said afterwards that if all politicians were like him there would be world peace.

Joe Clark. His finance minister said the nine-month government lasted long enough to conceive, but not long enough to deliver.

John Turner. He held the Canadian men's record in the 100-yard dash and qualified for the 1948 London Olympics. A bad knee kept him from going.

Brian Mulroney. He failed the Quebec bar exam twice before passing in 1965. His firm kept him on anyway.

Kim Campbell. In August 1993 she was the most popular prime minister in thirty years. Ten weeks later she lost her own seat and her party was reduced to two.

Jean Chrétien. When a 1993 attack ad appeared to mock the facial paralysis left by childhood Bell's palsy, he answered that God had given him a physical defect and he had accepted it as a child. The ad was pulled within days.

Paul Martin. He caught polio at eight years old in 1946. His father had caught it in 1907 — a rare father-and-son pair of survivors in Canadian politics.

Stephen Harper. He was the only Reform Party MP to vote in favour of creating the federal long-gun registry at second reading in 1995, before voting against it later.

Justin Trudeau. He is the only child of a prime minister to become prime minister. He first registered nationally at 28, delivering the eulogy at his father's state funeral in 2000.

Mark Carney. He is the only person to have run two G7 central banks — the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England — and the first Canadian prime minister who had never been elected to anything before taking the job.
