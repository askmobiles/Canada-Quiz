Where Winter Tires Are the Law in Canada

One province requires them everywhere. One requires them on certain roads. The rest only recommend them — however often you hear otherwise.

The short version

Transport Canada puts it plainly: Quebec is the only jurisdiction in Canada where winter tires are mandatory province-wide during the winter driving season. British Columbia is the other place with a real legal requirement, but it applies to signed routes rather than the whole province.

Everywhere else in Canada, winter tires are strongly recommended by the government and required by nobody. That does not make them optional in any sensible way — it just means the reason to fit them is physics, not a ticket.

Quebec, where the marking matters

From the first of December to the fifteenth of March, every tire on a vehicle registered in Quebec must be a winter tire. Rental cars are included. Heavy vehicles, tool vehicles and farm machinery are outside the rule, and there are narrow exemptions for a spare, for the week after buying from a dealer, for the week before a lease ends, for dealer plates and for motor homes.

The important detail is which tires count. Since December 2014 the tire must carry the pictogram of a mountain with three peaks and a six-pointed snowflake, at least fifteen millimetres across, or be legally studded. A tire marked only M and S — mud and snow, which most all-season tires carry — does not satisfy the Quebec law.

Quebec is also the one province with a clear rule about snow on the vehicle itself. Windows and windshield must be cleared of anything that reduces the driver's view, and it is an offence to drive a vehicle covered in ice or snow that may come off and endanger others.

British Columbia, where the road decides

British Columbia's requirement follows signs, not borders. It covers all highways in the North and the Interior, the Sea to Sky Highway, the Malahat, and Highways 4, 14 and 28 on Vancouver Island. Much of the Lower Mainland is not covered at all, which surprises people who assume the rule is provincial.

The period runs from the first of October to the thirtieth of April on most designated routes, ending on the thirty-first of March on some routes that do not cross mountain passes or high-snowfall areas. Studded tires are allowed over the same October to April window.

Here British Columbia and Quebec genuinely disagree. British Columbia accepts a tire marked M and S as the minimum legal requirement, or the mountain and snowflake symbol, and requires at least three and a half millimetres of tread. The fine for a passenger vehicle is one hundred and twenty-one dollars. A commercial driver who bypasses a chain-up area faces five hundred and ninety-eight.

What the two symbols actually mean

Transport Canada says that tires carrying the three-peaked mountain and snowflake symbol meet specific snow traction performance requirements and are designed for severe snow conditions. It has tested more than sixty tire models carrying that symbol since 2014 and found one that did not comply, which was recalled.

The M and S marking has no equivalent federal performance test behind it. British Columbia's own words are that it is the minimum legal requirement, while the mountain and snowflake tire gives better traction on snow and ice. Quebec simply does not accept M and S at all.

Two more figures from Transport Canada worth carrying in your head: below seven degrees, all-season and summer tires begin to lose elasticity and grip, and you should not be on snow-covered roads with tread worn close to four millimetres.

The Ontario discount is real, and it is compulsory on the insurer

Ontario does not require winter tires. What it requires is that every insurance company offer you a discount for having them. The regulator's words are that all insurance companies must offer a discount if you install winter tires, and its filing guidelines for private passenger automobile insurance state that a winter tire discount is mandatory.

How big it is, and what dates the tires must be on, is set by each company. The regulator says only to ask your insurer how to qualify, and no official source publishes a percentage — so this page will not invent one. The numbers you see quoted come from brokers, not from the regulator.

Manitoba does something different again. There is no law there either, but the public insurer runs a low-interest loan of up to two thousand dollars a vehicle, over as much as forty-eight months, to help people buy winter tires.

Does any of it actually work?

Yes, and the evidence is public rather than promotional. After Quebec tightened its winter tire standard in 2013, Transport Canada recorded that the average number of collisions during winter months in Quebec fell by nineteen percent against the previous five-year average, while across Canada the same figure fell by four percent.

Manitoba's public insurer studied more than a hundred and eleven thousand vehicles over seven insurance years and found that winter tire use was estimated to reduce collision claim frequency by six point three percent, with damage severity five point seven percent lower.

Transport Canada also braked a minibus from eighty kilometres an hour on snow. On six winter tires it stopped an average of five point six metres shorter than with all-seasons on the front axle. In a slalom at the same speed the mixed-tire vehicle could not hold the path and hit the cones. That is the argument against fitting only two.

Things people say that are not the law

Winter driving attracts confident advice. These are the four we hear most, checked against the actual rules:

  • Winter tires are required in Ontario. They are not. Only the insurer's offer of a discount is required.
  • You need winter tires everywhere in British Columbia. Only on signed routes, and much of the Lower Mainland is not one.
  • All-season tires are illegal in winter. True in Quebec, where M and S does not qualify. Not true in British Columbia, where it is the legal minimum.
  • You must clear the snow off your roof, everywhere. Quebec has a clear rule of that kind and fines to match. Ontario has no roof-clearing offence — what it has are obstructed-view charges and an offence for depositing snow on the roadway. For the other provinces we could not confirm a specific offence, so this page does not claim one.

Where these facts come from

The rules here come from the Quebec government and the SAAQ, the Quebec regulation on winter tires, the British Columbia government's winter driving and enforcement pages, Transport Canada, Ontario's insurance regulator, and each other province's own winter driving page.

Two kinds of number are deliberately missing. There is no percentage for the Ontario discount, because no official source gives one. And there are no dramatic stopping-distance claims, because the ones in wide circulation come from tire industry campaigns rather than from a government test.

Common questions

Which provinces require winter tires by law?

Quebec province-wide from 1 December to 15 March, and British Columbia on signed routes from 1 October. Nowhere else in Canada requires them.

Do all-season M+S tires count as winter tires?

In British Columbia yes, as the legal minimum. In Quebec no — only the three-peaked mountain and snowflake symbol or legal studs qualify.

Is the winter tire insurance discount mandatory in Ontario?

Yes, on the insurer. Every company must offer a discount. The size of it and the conditions are set by each company, and no official source publishes a percentage.

Is it illegal to drive with snow on your roof?

In Quebec there is a clear offence for driving a vehicle covered in snow or ice that may come off. Ontario has no roof-clearing offence, only obstructed-view charges. For other provinces we could not confirm one.
