🍁 Canadian Food: Poutine, Maple Syrup and Other Tastes of Canada

A friendly guide to the foods Canada is famous for — poutine, maple syrup, butter tarts, Nanaimo bars, ketchup chips and more.

Canada is a country of many cultures, and its food shows it. You will find dishes brought from every corner of the world, but there are also foods that are proudly and completely Canadian. Here are the ones every newcomer, visitor and curious kid should know about.

Maple syrup: Canada's sweetest symbol

Maple syrup comes from the sap of maple trees, mostly sugar maples. In late winter and early spring, when the days are warm and the nights are still cold, the sap starts to run. Farmers tap the trees, collect the sap, and boil it down until it becomes thick, golden syrup. It takes roughly 40 litres of sap to make just one litre of syrup, which is why real maple syrup is not cheap.

Quebec produces the large majority of the world's maple syrup, and spring "sugar shack" (cabane à sucre) visits are a beloved family tradition. One classic treat is maple taffy: hot syrup poured onto clean snow, then rolled onto a stick as it cools.

Poutine: fries, cheese curds and gravy

Poutine is probably the most famous Canadian dish in the world. It started in rural Quebec in the 1950s and is simple in the best way: hot french fries, fresh cheese curds, and hot gravy poured over the top. The gravy warms the curds just enough to make them soft and stretchy, but not fully melted — that squeaky, fresh curd is the whole point.

Today you can find poutine everywhere in Canada, from small-town diners to hockey arenas, and in dozens of versions with pulled pork, smoked meat, butter chicken or vegetables on top.

Other Canadian favourites

  • Butter tarts — small pastry tarts filled with butter, sugar and egg. Canadians argue endlessly about runny versus firm, and raisins versus no raisins.
  • Nanaimo bars — a no-bake dessert from Nanaimo, British Columbia, with a crumb base, custard-flavoured middle, and chocolate on top.
  • Montreal-style bagels — thinner and sweeter than New York bagels, boiled in honey water and baked in a wood-fired oven.
  • Montreal smoked meat — cured, spiced beef brisket, piled high on rye bread with mustard.
  • Tourtière — a savoury meat pie from Quebec, traditionally eaten around Christmas and New Year.
  • Bannock — a simple bread with deep roots in Indigenous communities across the country.
  • Peameal bacon — Toronto's famous back bacon rolled in cornmeal, best in a soft bun.
  • Ketchup chips — bright red, tangy potato chips that visitors either love immediately or find very confusing.
  • Beaver Tails — flat fried dough shaped like a beaver's tail, covered in cinnamon sugar or chocolate spread.

Foods from around the world, made at home

Because Canada welcomes newcomers from everywhere, "Canadian food" today also means the food of the people who live here. Most Canadian cities have grocery stores and restaurants serving South Asian, Chinese, Filipino, Caribbean, Middle Eastern, Ukrainian, Italian, Somali and many other cuisines. For many families, the food of their home country is now simply part of Canadian food too — and that is exactly how Canadians like it.

Fun facts to remember

  • Canada produces most of the world's maple syrup.
  • Poutine came from Quebec in the 1950s.
  • The Nanaimo bar is named after a city on Vancouver Island.
  • Canada has an official maple syrup reserve to keep prices steady between good and bad seasons.
