💵 Canadian Money Explained: The Loonie, Toonie and Bills

A simple guide to Canadian coins and banknotes — the loonie, toonie, why the penny is gone, and who appears on each bill.

Canadian money is colourful, made of plastic rather than paper, and full of nicknames that nobody explains to newcomers. Here is everything you need to know to feel confident at the checkout.

The coins

  • 5 cents — the nickel. Silver-coloured, with a beaver on the back.
  • 10 cents — the dime. The smallest coin of all, which confuses almost everyone at first. It shows the Bluenose, a famous Nova Scotia racing schooner.
  • 25 cents — the quarter. Silver-coloured with a caribou.
  • 1 dollar — the loonie. Gold-coloured, with a loon (a water bird) on the back. That bird is where the nickname comes from.
  • 2 dollars — the toonie. Two-toned, silver outside and gold in the middle, with a polar bear. The name is a joke on "two" plus "loonie", and it stuck.

Why there is no penny

Canada stopped making the one-cent coin in 2012 because each penny cost more than one cent to produce. Pennies are still legal, but you will almost never see one. When you pay with cash, the total is rounded to the nearest five cents. If you pay by card, no rounding happens and you pay the exact amount.

The banknotes

Canadian bills are made of a smooth polymer, not paper. They are hard to tear, they survive the washing machine, and each has a clear window with detailed security features. Each denomination has its own colour, which makes them easy to tell apart:

  • $5 — blue. Sir Wilfrid Laurier, Canada's first French-Canadian prime minister, with the Canadarm on the back.
  • $10 — purple. Viola Desmond, a Black Nova Scotian businesswoman who challenged segregation in a movie theatre in 1946. She was the first Canadian woman on a regularly circulating bank note.
  • $20 — green. The most common bill, and the one you get from bank machines.
  • $50 — red. William Lyon Mackenzie King, Canada's longest-serving prime minister.
  • $100 — brown. Sir Robert Borden, prime minister during the First World War.

What about King Charles III?

This is a common question. The $20 bill in your wallet today still shows Queen Elizabeth II. A new $20 featuring King Charles III has been announced, but it is not in circulation yet — the Bank of Canada expects it in early 2027. In the meantime, the existing $20 remains completely valid and will stay valid even after the new one arrives. Canadian banknotes are never cancelled just because a new design comes out.

Coins are a different story: circulation coins with King Charles III's portrait have already been produced by the Royal Canadian Mint, so you may find both portraits in your pocket change.

Everyday money tips for newcomers

  • Prices on the shelf usually do not include sales tax. The amount added at the till depends on your province.
  • Tipping is expected in restaurants, taxis and hair salons — commonly around 15 to 20 percent of the bill before tax.
  • Debit and credit cards, and tap payment, are accepted almost everywhere. Many small purchases never involve cash at all.
  • Opening a bank account usually requires two pieces of identification. Many banks have free newcomer packages for your first year.
  • Keep an eye on the difference between a chequing account (daily spending) and a savings account (interest).
