A Sandbar in the Ocean, Full of Wild Horses

There is an island off Nova Scotia made of nothing but sand, with no trees, no roads and no shops, and about four hundred and twenty wild horses live on it.

An island made of sand

Sable Island sits in the Atlantic Ocean about 160 kilometres from the nearest point of Nova Scotia, and about 290 kilometres from Halifax. It is 42 kilometres long and only about 1.3 kilometres across at its widest.

Draw that on paper and you get a thin curved sliver, like a fingernail clipping in the middle of the sea. It is shaped like a crescent moon, and it is made of sand, all the way through.

No native trees grow there. Wind and salt and moving sand make it far too hard a place for a tree to get started. What grows instead is tough beach grass, called marram grass, which holds the dunes together with its roots.

The island does not even stay the same shape. Sand blows off it in autumn and the waves hand it back in spring. One end has been growing wider while the other has been getting shorter.

Where the horses really came from

Almost everyone will tell you the horses swam ashore from shipwrecks. It is a wonderful story. It is not what happened.

Parks Canada records that a Boston minister named Andrew LeMercier put horses on the island on purpose, in 1737 and 1738. Then, some time after 1755, a Boston ship owner called Thomas Hancock shipped more horses out there. Those horses had been taken from Acadian families who were being forced out of Nova Scotia.

So the horses did not escape a wreck. They were carried there by people, and then left. Everything since has been up to the horses.

They have been protected by law since 1961. Today about 420 of them live there. Nobody feeds them, nobody brushes them, and no vet ever comes. They are completely wild, and that is on purpose.

How do you drink on a sandbar?

There is no river on Sable Island. There is no stream. Sand swallows rain so fast that water never runs along the top of the ground at all.

Instead, the rain sinks down and gathers underneath, floating on top of the salt water below like a layer of fresh water trapped in a sponge. On the west end it comes back to the surface as ponds, and the horses drink there.

On the east end there are no ponds. So the horses dig. They make small holes in the sand with their hooves until fresh water seeps in, and then they drink from the well they just made.

They eat mostly marram grass, with some other grasses and even seaweed. It is a thin living, and Sable Island horses are small and stocky and shaggy because of it.

The graveyard of the Atlantic

A long low island of sand, sitting exactly where the shipping lanes run, is a terrible thing to meet in fog. Sailors could be almost on top of Sable Island before they saw it.

More than 350 ships have been wrecked there since 1583. People called it the Graveyard of the Atlantic. On one single day, the 27th of September 1812, three ships went aground at once, and the crews waited two weeks to be rescued.

Sand keeps moving, and every so often it uncovers something. In May 2025 archaeologists dug up wreckage believed to be one of those three ships, the sloop Swift, more than two hundred years after it went down.

Who else lives there

The horses are not alone. Every winter grey seals haul out onto the beaches to have their pups, and this is the biggest grey seal nursery in the world. More than 80,000 pups are born there in a season. In the early 1960s there were about a hundred.

A small brown bird called the Ipswich sparrow nests almost nowhere else in the world. And there is a tiny bee, five millimetres long, called the Sable Island sweat bee, which lives here and nowhere else on Earth at all.

In 2013 the whole island became Sable Island National Park Reserve, looked after by Parks Canada. You are allowed to visit, but only in the summer season, only for the day, only with permission, and there is nowhere to camp.

It is a strange kind of national park: a place protected mostly by being left alone.

Words to know

Sandbar
A long ridge of sand built up by the sea.
Marram grass
A tough beach grass whose roots hold sand dunes together.
Shipwreck
A ship that has been sunk or broken apart, often on rocks or sand.
National park reserve
A place the whole country agrees to protect and leave alone.
